**Short answer:** SB 440 (effective January 1, 2026) creates a faster administrative process for resolving change order disputes on public works projects. SB 61 (also effective January 1, 2026) caps retention on private construction projects at 5% of the contract value — down from the 10% that was standard practice.

Together, these bills represent the most significant shift in California construction payment law in a decade. They both solve the same underlying problem: contractors and subcontractors financing other people's projects with their own operating capital for months longer than the work justifies.

## SB 440: Change orders without the courtroom

### The problem it solves

Before SB 440, a disputed change order on a public works project meant one of two outcomes: eat the cost or litigate. For a $40,000 change order on a $2 million school renovation, no rational contractor files a lawsuit — the legal fees consume the claim. So contractors absorbed the loss, padded future bids to account for the risk, and taxpayers paid more for every public project.


### How the new process works

SB 440 creates a streamlined claims procedure for change order disputes on public works contracts:

1. **Written claim submission** — The contractor submits a written claim to the public entity within 30 days of the disputed work.
2. **Entity response deadline** — The public entity must respond in writing within 45 days. Silence = deemed rejection.
3. **Meet-and-confer** — If rejected, both sides must meet within 30 days to attempt resolution.
4. **Non-binding mediation** — If the meet-and-confer fails, either party can demand mediation. Costs split evenly.
5. **Expedited arbitration** — For claims under $375,000, the parties may elect binding arbitration with a compressed timeline.

The key innovation isn't any single step — it's the mandatory deadlines. Under the prior system, a public entity could simply ignore a change order claim for months. Now, inaction triggers automatic progression to the next phase.

### What it means for contractors

If you're a general or sub working public jobs in California, three things change immediately:

**Document everything in real time.** The 30-day submission window starts when the disputed work occurs. Not when the project ends. Not when you submit your final invoice. When the extra work happens.

**Your claim must be specific.** Vague assertions like "additional work was required" won't survive the process. Include dates, labor hours, material costs, and the specific directive (verbal or written) that triggered the extra work.

**The 45-day silence rule is your friend.** If the public entity ghosts your claim, that silence constitutes a rejection you can immediately escalate to meet-and-confer. No more waiting six months for a response that never comes.

> **SB 440 (eff. 1/1/2026)** — Creates Public Contract Code §§ 9204.1–9204.5, establishing mandatory timelines for resolution of change order disputes on public works contracts.


## SB 61: The 5% retention cap

### Why retention exists (and why 10% was too much)

Retention is the percentage of each progress payment that the owner withholds until project completion — a security deposit of sorts, meant to ensure the contractor finishes the job. For decades, 10% was the California norm on private projects.

The problem: on a $500,000 residential remodel, 10% retention means $50,000 of the contractor's earned revenue sits in someone else's account for the duration of the project. For a small contracting company, that's a line of credit they're extending to the property owner — involuntarily, at 0% interest.

SB 61 says: enough. The cap is now 5%.

### The math that changes

| Project Value | Old Retention (10%) | New Retention (5%) | Cash Flow Improvement |
|--------------|--------------------|--------------------|----------------------|
| $100,000 | $10,000 withheld | $5,000 withheld | +$5,000 |
| $500,000 | $50,000 withheld | $25,000 withheld | +$25,000 |
| $1,000,000 | $100,000 withheld | $50,000 withheld | +$50,000 |
| $2,500,000 | $250,000 withheld | $125,000 withheld | +$125,000 |

For subcontractors, the impact is even more dramatic. Under the old rules, a GC could hold 10% from the sub's payments even while the owner was already holding 10% from the GC — meaning the sub effectively financed 10% of *their* contract value while the GC bore no cost for it.

### What contracts must say now

Any private construction contract executed after January 1, 2026 that includes retention above 5% is *unenforceable* on the excess amount. The contractor or sub can demand release of any retention above the 5% cap at any time.


Important: this applies to contracts *signed* after January 1, 2026. Existing contracts with 10% retention clauses remain enforceable through project completion. But any new contract, change order, or amendment signed after that date must comply with the 5% cap.

> **SB 61 (eff. 1/1/2026)** — Amends Civil Code § 8814 to cap retention on private works of improvement at 5% of the contract price.

## How these two bills interact

Consider a subcontractor working a public school renovation in 2026:

**Before:** The school district disputes a $60,000 change order and holds 10% retention on the sub's $400,000 contract. The sub is out $100,000 ($60,000 disputed change order + $40,000 retention) with no clear path to resolution short of litigation.

**After:** SB 440 forces the district to respond to the change order claim within 45 days. SB 61 (on the private side) or existing public retention rules limit the cash tied up. The sub still has to fight for the money — but now there's a timeline and a process that doesn't require hiring a litigator on day one.

## The demand letter as first step

Neither bill eliminates the need for a formal written demand when a contractor or owner isn't honoring their obligations. What they do is give that demand letter sharper teeth: you're no longer just asking nicely — you're invoking a statutory process with built-in deadlines and escalation mechanisms.

A demand letter citing SB 440's timeline requirements tells a public entity: respond in 45 days or I'm escalating automatically. A demand letter citing SB 61's retention cap tells an owner: release the excess now or face an unenforceable clause challenge.

## Timeline: key dates for 2026

**January 1, 2026** — Both bills take effect. New contracts must comply.
**Q1 2026** — Existing contracts with 10% retention clauses remain valid. New amendments or change orders on existing contracts: unclear — consult counsel.
**Mid-2026** — First wave of SB 440 claims should be reaching the mediation/arbitration phase. Case law will begin to develop.
**Late 2026** — Expect industry associations to publish updated standard contract templates incorporating the 5% retention cap.

## What to do right now

If you're a contractor: review every contract you sign after January 1 for retention clauses above 5%. If you see one, it's unenforceable on the excess — but better to negotiate it out upfront than fight about it later.

If you're a subcontractor on public works: implement a system for documenting change orders in real time. The 30-day submission clock starts running whether you're watching it or not.

If you're a property owner: understand that the 5% cap applies to your next construction contract. Budget accordingly — you'll have less retained security, which means your [contractor vetting process](/blog/how-to-file-cslb-complaint-against-contractor-california) matters more upfront.
