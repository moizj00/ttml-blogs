If your lease prohibits subletting and a tenant sublets or lists the unit on a short-term platform anyway, that's typically a breach of the lease in California — and you have a defined path to respond. The right first step is usually a notice giving the tenant a chance to cure the violation, not an immediate lockout, which is illegal.

**Short answer:** Unauthorized subletting usually violates the lease. A California landlord can serve a 3-day notice to perform covenant or quit (CCP § 1161(3)); local short-term-rental rules may add restrictions.

## Is subletting without permission actually a violation?

It depends on the lease. Most California leases require landlord consent before a tenant may sublet or assign. If yours does and the tenant didn't get consent, they've breached that term — whether it's a long-term subtenant or a string of Airbnb guests. If your lease is silent on subletting, your position is weaker, which is why a clear no-subletting clause matters.

## What's the landlord's first move?

For a curable lease violation, the standard tool is a **3-day notice to perform covenant or quit** under Code of Civil Procedure § 1161(3). It tells the tenant to stop the unauthorized subletting (cure the breach) within three days or surrender the unit. If they cure, the tenancy continues; if they don't, you may proceed toward an unlawful detainer (eviction) action.

What you cannot do is change the locks, remove belongings, or force the subtenant out yourself. Self-help lockouts are illegal in California (Civil Code § 789.3) and create landlord liability.

## How do short-term rentals (Airbnb) factor in?

Short-term listing of a unit the tenant doesn't own is often both a lease violation and, in many cities, a violation of local short-term-rental ordinances that restrict or require permits for stays under 30 days. That can give you additional grounds and may bring city enforcement into play. Check your city's rules — in some jurisdictions unauthorized short-term rentals carry their own penalties.

## Does just cause affect this?

For tenancies covered by the Tenant Protection Act (AB 1482), a lease violation like unauthorized subletting can be an "at-fault" just cause for eviction — but you generally must give the tenant the opportunity to cure first where the breach is curable. Local just-cause ordinances may add their own steps. Follow the applicable notice requirements precisely; procedural mistakes are the most common reason these cases fail.

## When does this become an eviction?

If the tenant ignores a valid cure notice, the path leads to unlawful detainer. Many landlords prefer to resolve it short of court with a clear written notice and, where appropriate, a demand that the unauthorized arrangement end. For the full notice landscape, see [eviction notice types in California](/blog/2026-06-01-eviction-notice-types-3-30-60-90-day-california).

## Bottom line

Unauthorized subletting is usually a fixable lease breach, not an instant eviction. Serve the proper cure notice, lean on local short-term-rental rules where they apply, and never resort to a self-help lockout. Handled by the book, you either get the violation cured or build a clean record for the eviction that follows.
