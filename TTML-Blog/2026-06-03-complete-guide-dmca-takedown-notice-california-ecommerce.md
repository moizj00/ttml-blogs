If another seller is using your product photos, listing copy, or original content without permission, a DMCA takedown notice is one of the fastest ways to get it removed. Under the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (17 U.S.C. § 512), online platforms must "expeditiously" remove infringing material after receiving a valid takedown notice — or lose their safe harbor protection from copyright liability. Most major platforms respond within 24–72 hours.

This guide walks California ecommerce sellers through the entire process: what qualifies for a DMCA takedown, how to file one on every major platform, what happens if the other party files a counter-notice, and when you should pair a takedown with a [cease and desist letter](/blog/2026-05-13-attorney-letter-vs-dmca-takedown-counterfeit-listings) for stronger enforcement.

## What Does the DMCA Actually Protect?

The DMCA's takedown provisions cover copyrighted material — not trademarks, patents, or trade secrets. For ecommerce sellers, this typically means:

- **Product photographs** you took or commissioned
- **Listing descriptions** you wrote (if they contain sufficient originality)
- **Marketing videos, graphics, and infographics** you created
- **Blog content or guides** published on your website

Important limitation: the DMCA does not protect product designs, ideas, or functional features. If someone is selling a similar-looking product with their own photos and descriptions, that's not a copyright issue — it may be a trademark or trade dress issue instead.

## The Six Required Elements of a Valid DMCA Takedown Notice

Under 17 U.S.C. § 512(c)(3), your takedown notice must include all six of these elements. Missing even one can give the platform a reason to ignore it:

**1. Your physical or electronic signature.** A typed name is sufficient for electronic notices, but some platforms prefer a scanned signature.

**2. Identification of the copyrighted work.** Describe or link to your original content — the photo on your website, the original listing URL, the copyright registration number if you have one.

**3. Identification of the infringing material.** Provide the exact URL(s) where the infringing content appears. Be specific — "their whole store" isn't sufficient. Link to the exact listing or page.

**4. Your contact information.** Name, address, phone number, and email. The platform needs to be able to reach you.

**5. A good-faith statement.** You must state: "I have a good faith belief that the use of the material in the manner complained of is not authorized by the copyright owner, its agent, or the law."

**6. A statement under penalty of perjury.** You must state that the information in your notice is accurate and that you are the copyright owner or authorized to act on their behalf. This is important — filing a false DMCA notice can expose you to liability under 17 U.S.C. § 512(f).

## How to File a DMCA Takedown on Major Platforms

Each platform has its own process, but they all accept the same core notice:

**Amazon** — Use the Report a Violation tool through Brand Registry (if enrolled) or the Report Infringement form for non-enrolled sellers. Amazon typically acts within 1–3 business days. If you're in Brand Registry, you can also use Amazon's automated protections to proactively block infringing listings.

**Etsy** — File through Etsy's Intellectual Property Infringement form at etsy.com/legal/ip. Etsy usually responds within 1–2 business days. You'll need the exact listing URL and your original content URLs.

**Shopify** — Email legal-dmca@shopify.com or use their DMCA notice form. Shopify will forward your notice to the store owner and remove the content if the owner doesn't respond or file a counter-notice within 10 business days.

**eBay** — Use eBay's VeRO (Verified Rights Owner) program. You'll need to register as a rights owner first, which takes a few days. Once approved, you can report infringing listings directly through the VeRO portal.

**Social media (Instagram, Facebook, TikTok)** — Each platform has an IP reporting form in their help center. Response times vary from 24 hours to a week.

## What Happens After You File?

The typical DMCA timeline looks like this:

**Day 1–3:** Platform receives your notice and reviews it for completeness. If it meets the six requirements above, the platform removes or disables access to the infringing content.

**Day 3–13:** The alleged infringer receives a copy of your notice and has the option to file a counter-notice under 17 U.S.C. § 512(g). If they don't, the content stays down permanently.

**If a counter-notice is filed:** The infringer swears under penalty of perjury that their use was lawful or that you misidentified the content. The platform then notifies you and restores the content after 10–14 business days unless you file a federal lawsuit and notify the platform.

## When Should You Pair a DMCA Takedown With a Cease and Desist Letter?

A DMCA takedown removes a specific piece of content from a specific platform. It doesn't stop the infringer from re-uploading or using your content elsewhere. If you're dealing with a repeat infringer or a competitor who is systematically copying your content, you need both:

The DMCA takedown handles the immediate problem — get the stolen content off the platform within days.

The cease and desist letter addresses the person — put them on formal legal notice that any future infringement will result in a federal lawsuit. This also establishes willfulness, which can increase your damages from actual damages to statutory damages of up to $150,000 per work infringed (17 U.S.C. § 504(c)).

## Common Mistakes That Get DMCA Notices Rejected

**Filing for content you don't own.** If you hired a photographer and don't have a written copyright assignment, you may not be the legal copyright owner. Confirm ownership before filing.

**Being vague about the infringing URLs.** "Their entire shop is copying me" won't work. You need specific URLs for specific infringing items.

**Filing for non-copyrightable elements.** Product features, color choices for a single product, or common phrases aren't copyrightable. The DMCA only covers original creative expression.

**Not following up on counter-notices.** If the infringer files a counter-notice and you don't file suit within 14 days, the content goes back up. Know your timeline and budget before filing.

## How Much Does DMCA Enforcement Cost?

Filing a DMCA takedown notice yourself is free — you just need to fill out the form and send it. But the process is technical enough that many sellers get notices rejected on the first attempt.

Having an attorney prepare and file the DMCA notice costs $150–$400 per notice through a flat-fee service. If you're dealing with multiple platforms or a persistent infringer, a cease and desist letter ($199–$500) combined with DMCA takedowns is the most cost-effective approach.

Copyright registration — which you should do before or shortly after filing — costs $55–$85 through the Copyright Office's eCO system and takes 3–6 months for standard processing. This is a critical investment: without registration, you cannot file a federal lawsuit or recover statutory damages.
