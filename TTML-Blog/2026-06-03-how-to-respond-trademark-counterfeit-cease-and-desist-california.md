If you receive a trademark or counterfeit cease and desist letter as a California ecommerce seller, don't ignore it and don't panic-comply either — first evaluate whether the claim is valid, preserve your evidence, and respond in writing (often through an attorney) by the stated deadline. Trademark claims arise under the federal Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1114 and § 1125, and California's Unfair Competition Law, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200. A measured response protects you whether the claim is legitimate, exaggerated, or baseless.

**Short answer:** Read the letter carefully, identify exactly what the sender claims you did, assess whether they actually have rights and whether you're really infringing, preserve all records, and respond by the deadline. Many demands are negotiable; some are bluffs; a few are serious. Don't guess — evaluate.

## Why shouldn't you just ignore the letter?

Ignoring a cease and desist is one of the riskiest responses. If the claim is legitimate, silence can be used later as evidence that your infringement was "willful," which can increase damages. It can also prompt the sender to escalate straight to a lawsuit or a marketplace complaint that gets your listings — or your whole seller account — suspended without warning.

At the same time, a letter is not a court order. It's a demand, and demands are often opening positions. The right move is neither to ignore it nor to immediately do everything it asks, but to understand it and respond deliberately within the timeframe given.

## How do you evaluate whether the claim is valid?

Start with three questions. First, does the sender actually own the rights they claim? Ask for their trademark registration number and check it on the USPTO database, or confirm the copyright they're asserting. Some letters assert rights that are weak, unregistered, or don't cover the goods at issue.

Second, are you actually infringing? Trademark infringement turns on likelihood of confusion — using a similar mark on related goods in a way that confuses customers. Selling a genuine, lawfully acquired product is different from selling a counterfeit. There are also defenses, like nominative fair use (referring to a brand to describe a compatible or genuine product) and the first-sale doctrine (reselling authentic goods you legitimately bought). 

Third, what is the sender really demanding — that you stop using a logo, pull a listing, hand over profits, or something broader? Separating a reasonable demand from an overreaching one tells you how to respond.

## What should you do immediately after reading it?

Preserve everything. Save the letter, note the deadline, and keep all records related to the product: your suppliers and invoices, your listings, your sales data, and any communications. If the dispute involves authentic goods, your proof of legitimate sourcing may be your best defense. Do not destroy listings, messages, or inventory records — that can look like spoliation of evidence later.

Then resist the urge to fire off an angry or admitting reply. Anything you write can be used against you. A short acknowledgment that you received the letter and are reviewing it, sent before the deadline, buys time without conceding anything.

## What are your realistic response options?

Depending on your evaluation, you generally have a few paths. You can comply — stop the conduct and confirm in writing — which makes sense when the claim is clearly valid and compliance is cheap (for example, you unknowingly used a phrase that turns out to be someone's mark). You can negotiate, agreeing to modify a listing, change packaging, or sell through remaining inventory under agreed terms. You can push back, explaining why you're not infringing and asserting your defenses, especially if you're selling authentic goods or making fair use of a name. Or, if the letter is a baseless bluff, you can decline firmly while documenting your position.

The key is matching the response to the reality. Overreacting to a weak claim costs you sales you didn't have to lose; underreacting to a strong claim invites a lawsuit. An attorney can quickly tell you which situation you're in, and a lawyer's reply often carries more weight than your own.

## When does a counterfeit allegation get serious?

Counterfeit claims are more serious than ordinary trademark disputes because counterfeiting carries heightened remedies and, in some cases, criminal exposure. If you're genuinely reselling authentic products, the first-sale doctrine may protect you, and documenting your supply chain becomes critical. If, however, the goods are in fact unauthorized copies, the safest course is usually to stop and seek counsel immediately, because the downside of fighting a valid counterfeiting claim is steep.

Be careful with gray-market and "authentic but unauthorized" goods, which occupy a legal middle ground. Whether you can sell them depends on details like material differences from the authorized product and any quality-control conditions. This is an area where a quick legal read pays for itself.

## How can a lawyer help, and is it worth it?

For most California sellers, the highest-value step is a short consultation. A lawyer can verify the sender's rights, assess your defenses, and draft a response that protects you without conceding more than necessary. A measured attorney letter can also de-escalate — it shows the sender you're informed and prepared, which often leads to a reasonable resolution instead of litigation.

A flat-fee response letter is typically far cheaper than defending a lawsuit or losing a profitable storefront. ## The bottom line

## What if the letter triggered a marketplace suspension?

Sometimes the first sign of trouble isn't the letter at all — it's an Amazon, Walmart, or TikTok Shop notice that your listing or account was suspended after a rights holder complained. In that situation, the platform's appeal process runs in parallel with the legal dispute. You'll typically need to submit a plan of action or evidence of authenticity (your invoices and supply-chain records) to the marketplace, while separately responding to the rights holder's letter.

The two tracks are connected: if you can resolve the underlying claim — by showing you sell genuine goods, or by reaching a retraction agreement with the rights holder — that resolution often helps reinstate your listing. This is another reason to keep your sourcing documentation airtight before any dispute arises, because it's the evidence both the platform and the sender will want to see.

## How do you tell a real threat from a bluff?

Not every letter is backed by the willingness — or the rights — to litigate. Signs a claim is weaker include vague assertions with no registration number, demands wildly out of proportion to any actual harm, or "rights" that don't actually cover the goods you sell. Signs a claim is serious include a verifiable registration, specific identification of your infringing conduct, a counterfeiting allegation (which carries heightened remedies), and a letter from counsel who clearly litigates these cases.

You usually can't make this call confidently on your own, and guessing wrong is costly in both directions. A brief attorney review — often a flat fee — tells you whether you're looking at a bluff, a negotiable demand, or a genuine threat, so you can respond with the right level of force.

## The bottom line

A cease and desist letter is a serious document but not a verdict. Read it carefully, find out whether the sender truly has rights and whether you're truly infringing, preserve your evidence, and respond by the deadline — ideally after a quick legal review. California sellers who approach these letters calmly and deliberately almost always end up in a better position than those who ignore them or surrender on the spot.
