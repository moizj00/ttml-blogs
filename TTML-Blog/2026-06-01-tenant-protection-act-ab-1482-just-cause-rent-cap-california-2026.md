California's Tenant Protection Act of 2019, known as AB 1482, did two big things statewide: it capped how much rent can rise each year for covered units, and it required landlords to have a valid "just cause" to end many tenancies. For landlords and tenants alike, knowing whether a unit is covered — and what the rules require — is essential, because the Act reshapes both pricing and the eviction process for a large share of California rentals.

**Short answer:** AB 1482 caps annual rent increases at 5% plus local CPI (max 10%) and requires just cause to terminate covered tenancies after 12 months. Single-family homes (with proper notice) and newer construction are commonly exempt.

## What does AB 1482 cap on rent?

For covered units, a landlord generally cannot raise rent in any 12-month period by more than **5% plus the local percentage change in the cost of living (CPI), capped at 10% total**. Whichever is lower — the 5%+CPI figure or 10% — is the ceiling. The CPI figure varies by region and year, so the exact maximum changes annually and by location.

The cap also limits how increases can be structured: a landlord can't dodge it by imposing multiple increases that together exceed the annual limit. The first rent set at the start of a tenancy isn't capped, but every increase after that is.

## What is "just cause" to evict?

For tenants who have occupied a covered unit for 12 months or more, AB 1482 requires the landlord to state a valid reason — "just cause" — to terminate the tenancy. Just causes fall into two buckets:

- **At-fault just cause:** the tenant did something, such as failing to pay rent, materially breaching the lease, committing a nuisance, or unauthorized subletting. For curable violations, the tenant generally must be given a chance to fix the problem first.
- **No-fault just cause:** the landlord ends the tenancy for a reason unrelated to tenant conduct, such as an owner or family-member move-in, a withdrawal of the unit from the rental market, a government order, or a qualifying substantial remodel.

For no-fault terminations, the landlord generally must provide relocation assistance — typically one month's rent — or a waiver of the final month's rent.

## Which units are covered, and which are exempt?

AB 1482 covers much of California's rental housing, but several categories are exempt:

- **Single-family homes and condos** are generally exempt **if** the owner isn't a corporation or REIT and the landlord provides the required written notice of exemption.
- **New construction:** units with a certificate of occupancy issued within the last 15 years (a rolling window) are typically exempt.
- **Certain owner-occupied** duplexes and specific other categories.
- **Units already under stricter local rent control** follow the local ordinance instead.

The single-family-home exemption is widely used but conditional — miss the written notice requirement and the exemption can be lost. Landlords relying on an exemption should make sure the required language is in the lease.

## How does AB 1482 interact with local rent control?

AB 1482 is a statewide floor, not a ceiling. Cities with their own rent-control and just-cause ordinances — Los Angeles, San Francisco, Oakland, and others — often have stricter rules that govern instead. Where a local ordinance is more protective, it generally controls. Always check the local rules for the city where the unit sits before setting an increase or serving a notice.

## What notices does the Act require?

Covered tenancies come with notice obligations: the lease must include specific statutory language about AB 1482's protections, and termination notices must state the just cause. For no-fault terminations, the relocation-assistance requirement must be satisfied. Procedural precision matters here — a technically defective notice is one of the most common reasons an eviction stalls or fails.

## What does this mean in practice?

For landlords, AB 1482 means pricing within the annual cap, confirming whether your unit is covered or exempt (and papering the exemption if it applies), and building a documented just-cause record before serving any termination. For tenants, it means a rent increase above the cap or a termination without stated cause may be unlawful for a covered unit — worth scrutinizing rather than accepting at face value.

Disputes often turn on documentation: whether the exemption notice was given, whether the increase exceeded the cap, whether the stated cause is valid. A clear written record is the best protection on either side, and a documented demand or notice frequently resolves disagreements without litigation. For the mechanics of the notices themselves, see [eviction notice types in California](/blog/2026-06-01-eviction-notice-types-3-30-60-90-day-california).

## Bottom line

AB 1482 caps annual rent increases at 5% plus CPI (10% maximum) and requires just cause to end covered tenancies after a year, with relocation assistance for no-fault terminations. Single-family homes with proper notice and newer construction are commonly exempt, and stricter local ordinances can override. Whether you're raising rent or ending a tenancy, the safe path is the same: confirm coverage, respect the cap, state a valid cause, and document every step.
