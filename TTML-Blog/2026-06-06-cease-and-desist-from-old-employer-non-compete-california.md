Almost certainly not — and the letter itself may be unlawful. California Business and Professions Code § 16600 makes every contract that restrains someone from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business void, with only narrow exceptions tied to the sale of a business or dissolution of a partnership. Since January 1, 2024, § 16600.5 goes further: it is a civil violation for an employer to attempt to enforce a void non-compete, and employees who win can recover damages and attorney fees. So a cease and desist demanding you stop working for a competitor is, in most cases, a threat California law specifically prohibits.

**Short answer:** Non-compete agreements are void for California employees under B&P Code § 16600, regardless of where the agreement was signed (§ 16600.5). But don't ignore the letter — respond, because some demands buried inside it (trade secrets, client data) can be legitimate.

## Why Are Non-Competes Void in California?

California made a policy choice over a century ago: employee mobility beats employer protection. Section 16600 voids any agreement restraining lawful work, and courts apply it strictly — there is no "reasonableness" balancing like in most other states. The 2023 legislation (SB 699 and AB 1076) sharpened this further: § 16600.5 makes void non-competes unenforceable regardless of where they were signed, prohibits employers from attempting to enforce them, and AB 1076 required employers to notify current and former employees by February 2024 that existing non-compete clauses are void.

The narrow exceptions: a person selling the goodwill of a business, an owner selling their interest, or partners/LLC members upon dissolution can agree to limited non-competes (B&P Code §§ 16601, 16602, 16602.5). If you were an ordinary employee, these don't apply to you.

## So Why Did My Old Employer Send This Letter?

Three common reasons. First, intimidation — many employers know the clause is void but bet you don't. Second, the letter may actually be about trade secrets, not competition: California's Uniform Trade Secrets Act (Civil Code § 3426 et seq.) does protect genuine confidential information like customer lists built with substantial effort, pricing models, and proprietary processes. Third, non-solicitation of employees or misuse of confidential data may be raised — these occupy murkier territory than pure non-competes, though California courts have grown skeptical of customer non-solicit clauses too.

Read the letter carefully and separate the void demand (don't work for a competitor) from the potentially valid ones (don't use our confidential files).

## Should You Respond or Ignore It?

Respond — through an attorney, in writing. A short, firm response letter does three things: it states that the non-compete is void under § 16600 and that attempted enforcement violates § 16600.5; it confirms you have not taken and will not use any trade secrets; and it warns that continued threats — including contacting your new employer — may expose them to liability. That last point matters: employers sometimes send copies of these letters to the new employer to get the employee fired. A documented response makes that tactic riskier for them.

Ignoring the letter rarely ends the matter and leaves your new employer guessing if they receive a copy.

## Can You Go on Offense?

Yes. Under § 16600.5, an employee or prospective employee who is harmed by attempted enforcement of a void non-compete can sue for injunctive relief and actual damages, and a prevailing employee recovers attorney fees and costs. If the employer's letter cost you a job offer, that's recoverable harm. Many disputes settle quickly once the former employer's counsel realizes the fee-shifting now runs against their client.

## What If You Really Did Take Files?

Then treat the letter seriously. Return or delete anything taken, confirm it in writing, and get counsel before responding — trade secret claims under CUTSA are the one part of these letters with real teeth, including injunctions and damages.
