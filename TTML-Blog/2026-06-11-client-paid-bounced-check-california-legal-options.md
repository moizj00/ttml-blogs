A client paid you with a check that bounced, and now they're dodging your calls. In California, you're not stuck eating the loss. The state's bad check statute gives you a fast, aggressive collection tool that most freelancers don't know about — and it can triple what you're owed.

**Short answer: Under California Civil Code § 1719, you can demand the face value of the check plus up to three times that amount in damages (capped at $1,500), plus your costs. You must send a written demand first and give them 30 days to pay before you can collect the treble damages.**


## What Happens When a Check Bounces in California?

A bounced check — technically a "dishonored" check — means the bank refused payment, usually for insufficient funds (NSF). The person who wrote it still owes you the original amount. But California goes further.

> **California Civil Code § 1719** — If a check is dishonored and the payee sends a written demand, the check writer owes: (1) the face amount of the check, (2) a service charge of $25 for the first bounced check or $35 for subsequent ones, and (3) treble damages up to $1,500 if they fail to pay within 30 days of the demand.

That treble damages provision matters. A $2,000 bounced check can become a $7,500 claim — the original $2,000 plus $1,500 in statutory damages plus costs. That math changes the conversation fast.

## The 30-Day Demand Letter: Your First Move

Before you can collect treble damages, you must send a written demand via certified mail. The letter needs to include:

- The date and amount of the original check
- The bank that refused it
- A demand for payment of the check amount plus the service charge
- A statement that failure to pay within 30 days subjects them to treble damages under Civil Code § 1719

This is where a [demand letter from an attorney](/blog/what-is-a-demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices) lands differently than a DIY note. The statute requires a specific format, and letterhead signals you know what comes next.


## Your Options After the 30 Days Expire

| Option | Cost | Timeline | Best For |
|--------|------|----------|----------|
| Small claims court (treble damages) | $30–$75 filing fee | 30–70 days | Checks under $12,500 face value |
| Attorney demand letter | $199–$500 | 7–14 days to send | Checks where the client has assets |
| District attorney bad check unit | Free | Varies widely | Checks written with intent to defraud |
| Civil lawsuit (superior court) | $435+ filing fee | 6–12 months | Large amounts or corporate clients |

Small claims is the sweet spot for most freelancers. You file for the original amount plus treble damages, and California small claims courts regularly award both. No attorney needed.

## When the DA Gets Involved

California Penal Code § 476a makes it a crime to write a check knowing your account can't cover it. Most county DA offices have a bad check restitution program — they'll send the check writer a letter demanding payment before deciding whether to prosecute.

You don't control whether the DA files charges, but the restitution letter alone often gets payment. Contact your county DA's office and bring a copy of the bounced check, your demand letter, and proof of certified mailing.

## The Bottom Line

A bounced check in California isn't just a broken promise — it's a statutory claim with teeth. Send the 30-day demand letter first. If they pay within that window, you get your money plus the service charge. If they don't, you take them to small claims and ask for treble damages. The math makes settling the smarter move for them, which is the entire point.

A flat-fee [attorney demand letter](/blog/how-much-does-demand-letter-cost-california-2026) costs far less than what treble damages add to your claim. For most freelancers, it's the fastest path from bounced check to paid invoice.
