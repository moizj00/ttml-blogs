California Civil Code § 3342 makes a dog owner strictly liable for damages when their dog bites someone in a public place or lawfully in a private place — regardless of whether the dog ever showed aggression before. There is no "one free bite" in California. Because most dog owners carry homeowner's or renter's insurance that covers dog bite liability, the practical path to compensation is a demand letter package sent to the owner and their insurer: injury documentation, medical bills, lost wages, and a settlement demand. You have two years from the bite to file suit (Code of Civil Procedure § 335.1), and the insurance negotiation happens inside that window.

**Short answer:** The owner is liable by statute, the insurer usually pays, and the demand letter is the document that converts your injury into a valued claim. Send it after treatment stabilizes, document everything, and don't accept the insurer's first offer — initial offers on unrepresented claims routinely come in far below the claim's settlement range.

## What Does Strict Liability Mean for Your Claim?

It means you don't have to prove the owner did anything wrong. Under § 3342, the owner is liable for a bite if you were in a public place or lawfully in a private place (including the owner's property, if invited or performing a lawful duty — mail carriers are expressly covered). You don't need to show negligence, prior bites, or knowledge of viciousness. The main defenses left to owners are trespass (you weren't lawfully present), provocation (comparative fault if you provoked the dog), and the veterinarian/dog-professional assumption-of-risk line of cases. Injuries caused by a dog without a bite — being knocked down, chased into traffic — fall outside § 3342 but can still be pursued under ordinary negligence.

This is why dog bite demand letters are unusually strong: liability is usually a settled question, so the negotiation is almost entirely about damages.

## Who Actually Pays — and Why Does That Change Your Strategy?

Homeowner's and renter's liability coverage typically pays dog bite claims, with policy limits commonly $100,000 to $300,000. This changes everything about tone and strategy. Your real audience is an insurance adjuster, not the neighbor whose dog bit you. The owner reports the claim, the insurer assigns an adjuster, and from then on you're negotiating with a professional who values claims for a living. A demand letter aimed at an adjuster needs documentation density — bills, records, photos, wage proof — because adjusters value files, not stories.

Two cautions early on. First, give the insurer basic facts but decline recorded statements until you understand your claim's value — recorded statements are mined for provocation and trespass angles. Second, don't sign medical authorizations giving the insurer your lifetime records; provide the bite-related records yourself.

## What Should the Demand Letter Include?

Six components. The incident: date, location, how the bite occurred, and why you were lawfully present — with the statutory frame stated plainly: "Under Civil Code § 3342, your insured is strictly liable." The injuries: each wound, treatment received, and treating providers — emergency care, sutures, rabies protocol if any, infection treatment, scar revision consults. The documentation: itemized medical bills, records, photographs of the injuries at multiple stages (fresh, healing, scarred), and the animal control report if one exists — filing one creates an official record and helps the next victim. The economic damages: medical totals, future treatment estimates (scar revision is commonly underclaimed), and lost wages with employer verification. The non-economic damages: pain, scarring, anxiety around dogs, sleep disruption — described concretely, not generically. And the demand: a specific dollar figure with a response deadline, typically 30 days for insurers.

Children's claims deserve a special note: kids are a large share of serious bite victims, facial scarring is more consequential for them, and minors' settlements in California generally require court approval — factors that usually justify attorney involvement rather than a DIY letter.

## How Are Dog Bite Damages Valued?

Economic damages are arithmetic: bills plus wage loss plus reasonably certain future costs. Non-economic damages are negotiated, and scarring drives them. A bite that heals clean settles very differently from one leaving visible facial or hand scarring. Adjusters weigh medical specials, permanence, visibility of scars, infection complications, psychological treatment, and the claimant's credibility. Insurers' first offers on unrepresented claims are systematically low — the adjuster's job is to close files cheaply, and they price in your inexperience. A documented demand letter with a justified number, and a demonstrated willingness to escalate, moves the range. Claims worth real money (surgery, permanent scarring, child victims) usually warrant contingency counsel; claims in the small-claims range (up to $12,500) can be run effectively with a strong letter and a small claims filing as backup.

## What Deadlines and Procedural Traps Apply?

The statute of limitations is two years from the bite (CCP § 335.1). Two exceptions matter. If the dog's owner is a government entity (police K-9s, dogs at government facilities), the Government Claims Act requires a written claim to the entity within six months — a trap that forfeits valid claims every year. And for minors, the limitations period is tolled until age 18, but evidence goes stale long before — document now even if filing waits. Report serious bites to county animal control; the report is admissible documentation and triggers quarantine verification that protects you on rabies questions.

## What If the Owner Has No Insurance?

You can still demand and sue the owner personally — the strict liability statute doesn't care about coverage — but collectability becomes the question. A small claims judgment (up to $12,500) against a working owner is collectible through wage garnishment and bank levies. For larger uninsured claims, weigh the cost of superior court litigation against realistic recovery. Sometimes landlords share liability when they knew a tenant's dog was dangerous and could have removed it — a question worth attorney review in serious cases. The collection mechanics mirror other money judgments — see [how to document a claim before sending a demand](/blog/2026-06-04-how-to-document-unpaid-invoice-claim-before-demand-california) for the evidence-first approach that applies equally here.

## Should You Negotiate or File Suit?

Negotiate first — bite claims with clear liability settle at high rates — but negotiate with a credible alternative. The sequence that works: treat until stable, assemble the file, send the demand with a deadline, evaluate the response against your documented damages, and file (small claims or superior court, depending on size) if the insurer won't move into a defensible range. Every step you documented earlier pays off at this decision point, because your filing threat is only as strong as the file behind it.
