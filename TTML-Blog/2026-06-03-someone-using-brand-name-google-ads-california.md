If a competitor is bidding on your brand name as a Google Ads keyword, the answer depends on how they're using it. Bidding on your trademarked name as a keyword — so their ad appears when someone searches for you — is generally legal and permitted by Google's policies. But using your trademark in the actual ad text, headlines, or display URL is a different story. That can constitute trademark infringement under the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)) and California Business and Professions Code § 14245, and Google's own trademark policy prohibits it.

The distinction matters: keyword bidding is advertising strategy; using your name in ad copy is potentially passing off.

## Is Bidding on a Competitor's Brand Name as a Keyword Illegal?

In most cases, no. The Ninth Circuit — which covers California — addressed this directly in *Network Automation, Inc. v. Advanced Systems Concepts, Inc.*, 638 F.3d 1137 (9th Cir. 2011). The court held that purchasing a competitor's trademark as a search engine keyword is a "use in commerce" under the Lanham Act, but it doesn't automatically constitute infringement. The analysis turns on the likelihood of confusion — whether consumers are actually confused about who's advertising.

Google's own policy permits advertisers to bid on trademarked keywords. What Google restricts is the use of trademarked terms in ad text (headlines, descriptions, and display URLs) by unauthorized parties.

In practice, this means a competitor can pay to show their ad when someone searches for your brand — but they can't write "Official [Your Brand] Products" in their ad headline unless they actually are authorized.

## When Does Keyword Advertising Cross the Line Into Trademark Infringement?

The Ninth Circuit's *Sleekcraft* test (from *AMF Inc. v. Sleekcraft Boats*, 599 F.2d 341 (9th Cir. 1979)) governs likelihood of confusion in California. The court considers eight factors, but the ones most relevant to keyword advertising disputes are:

**Similarity of the marks** — If the competitor's ad uses your exact brand name, this weighs heavily in your favor.

**Proximity of the goods** — If you sell competing products, consumers are more likely to be confused.

**Evidence of actual confusion** — If customers are clicking the competitor's ad thinking it's yours, that's strong evidence.

**The competitor's intent** — If they're deliberately trying to divert your customers, the court will weigh this against them.

**The labeling and presentation** — Google ads now clearly label "Sponsored" results, which reduces confusion. But if the ad copy itself is misleading — using your brand name, claiming to be an "alternative" without clearly identifying themselves — confusion is more likely.

The line: a competitor bidding on "your brand name" as a keyword and running an ad that says "Compare [Their Brand] vs Others" is probably fine. An ad that says "Buy [Your Brand Name] — Official Products on Sale" when they're not authorized is almost certainly infringement.

## How to File a Trademark Complaint With Google

Google has a specific process for trademark complaints in advertising:

**Step 1: Submit a trademark complaint** through Google's Advertising Policies Help Center. You'll need your trademark registration details, the specific ad text containing your trademark, and the advertiser's domain.

**Step 2: Google investigates.** If Google determines the ad text uses your trademark in a way that violates their policy, they'll restrict the advertiser from using your trademark in their ad copy. Response time is typically 2–4 weeks.

**Step 3: Ongoing enforcement.** Google's restriction applies to that specific advertiser. If other advertisers start using your trademark, you'll need to file separate complaints.

**Important limitation:** Google's trademark complaint process only restricts the use of your trademark in ad text. It does not prevent competitors from bidding on your brand name as a keyword. For that, you need a different strategy.

## When Should You Send a Cease and Desist Letter?

A cease and desist letter is warranted when:

- The competitor is using your trademark in their ad text (not just bidding on the keyword)
- Google's complaint process hasn't resolved the issue
- The competitor's ads are causing demonstrable customer confusion or lost sales
- The competitor's landing page uses your trademark in a misleading way
- You want to establish a record of willful infringement for potential litigation

The letter should reference the specific ads, include screenshots with dates, cite the Lanham Act and California's trademark statutes, and demand that the competitor stop using your trademark in all advertising — not just Google Ads.

An attorney-signed cease and desist carries particular weight in this context because most businesses understand the potential damages in a trademark case. Under the Lanham Act, you can recover the competitor's profits from the infringing ads, your own lost profits, and potentially treble damages for willful infringement (15 U.S.C. § 1117).

## What About California's Unfair Competition Law?

California's UCL (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200) provides an additional avenue that federal law doesn't. The UCL covers "unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business acts or practices," and its definition of "unfair" is broader than the Lanham Act's likelihood of confusion test.

If a competitor's keyword advertising is diverting your customers — even if it might not meet the federal trademark infringement standard — it could still qualify as an "unfair" business practice under the UCL. California courts have applied the UCL to keyword advertising in cases where the advertising creates initial interest confusion, even if consumers eventually figure out they're dealing with a different company.

The UCL also allows any person who has "suffered injury in fact and has lost money or property" to bring a claim — a lower standing requirement than federal trademark law, which typically requires trademark ownership.

## Practical Strategies Beyond Legal Action

While you pursue the legal angle, there are practical steps to minimize the damage:

**Bid on your own brand name.** This is the simplest defense. If you're running Google Ads on your own brand name, your ad will appear above or alongside the competitor's — and you'll have the advantage of relevance, which typically means lower cost-per-click and higher placement.

**Optimize your organic presence.** Ensure your website ranks first in organic results for your brand name. Strong organic rankings plus your own brand ads make the competitor's ad less visible and less effective.

**Track the impact.** Use Google Search Console and Google Ads data to quantify how much traffic or revenue you're losing to the competitor's brand bidding. This data strengthens both your cease and desist letter and any potential lawsuit.

**Document everything.** Screenshot the competitor's ads regularly (with dates), record your own metrics, and keep copies of any customer complaints about confusion. This evidence is essential for establishing likelihood of confusion and calculating damages.

## What's the Most Effective Approach?

For most California businesses, the optimal strategy is layered: (1) bid on your own brand name to defend your ad real estate, (2) file a Google trademark complaint if the competitor is using your mark in their ad text, (3) send a [cease and desist letter](/blog/2026-05-27-california-intellectual-property-laws-small-businesses) if the behavior continues or if the ad text is misleading, and (4) pursue federal or state court litigation only if the competitor ignores the letter and the financial impact justifies the cost.

Most cases resolve at step 2 or 3. The combination of Google's enforcement and an attorney letter is enough to stop the overwhelming majority of trademark abuse in advertising.
