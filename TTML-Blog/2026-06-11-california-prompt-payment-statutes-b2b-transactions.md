**Short answer: California has at least four distinct prompt payment regimes — one for construction subcontractors, one for government contracts, one for commercial transactions generally, and one for freelancers. Each imposes specific payment deadlines (ranging from 7 to 30 days) and statutory penalties (2% per month, attorney fees, or both) that go well beyond ordinary breach-of-contract damages. If you're owed money in a B2B context, there's almost certainly a prompt payment statute working in your favor.**

## Why prompt payment statutes matter more than your contract

You might have net-30 terms in your contract. Your client might have a purchasing policy that pays on net-60. None of that matters if a prompt payment statute sets a shorter deadline — because these statutes are mandatory. A contract clause that tries to waive or extend a statutory payment deadline is void as against public policy.

That's the leverage most businesses miss. They think the contract controls payment timing. In California, the statute often overrides the contract, and the penalties for violation are designed to be painful enough to deter slow-paying.

Here's the landscape:

## The four prompt payment regimes

### 1. Construction: Business and Professions Code §§ 7108.5, Civil Code §§ 8812-8822

California's construction prompt payment law is the most aggressive. Under Civil Code § 8812, a direct contractor (prime/GC) must pay each subcontractor within **7 days** of receiving payment from the owner — the "pay-when-paid" timeline. And under § 8814, a higher-tier subcontractor must pay lower-tier subcontractors within **7 days** of receiving their own payment.

The penalty for violation: **2% per month** on the unpaid amount, plus reasonable attorney fees for the prevailing party.

| Party | Payment trigger | Deadline | Penalty |
|-------|---------------|----------|---------|
| Owner → Direct contractor | Progress payment approval | Per contract | Civil Code § 8800 |
| Direct contractor → Sub | Receipt of owner payment | 7 days | 2%/month + attorney fees (§ 8818) |
| Higher-tier sub → Lower-tier sub | Receipt of payment from above | 7 days | 2%/month + attorney fees (§ 8818) |
| Retention release (private) | Project completion + 45 days | Per § 8814 | 2%/month on withheld retention |

The construction prompt payment act also restricts "pay-if-paid" clauses. Under Civil Code § 8122, a provision conditioning a subcontractor's right to payment on the direct contractor's receipt of payment from the owner is void. The GC bears collection risk — not the sub.

> **California statute spotlight:** Civil Code § 8818 provides that "if a direct contractor or subcontractor is not paid within the time period required... the direct contractor or subcontractor shall be entitled to a penalty of 2 percent per month on the amount wrongfully withheld, in addition to any other remedies."

### 2. Government contracts: Government Code §§ 927-927.15

When the State of California owes you money, the prompt payment timeline is **45 days** from receipt of an undisputed invoice. After 45 days, the state must pay interest at the rate set annually by the Director of Finance — typically in the range of 7-10%.

For local government entities, the timeline is **30 days** under many local ordinances, though this varies by jurisdiction. The California State Prompt Payment Act (Government Code § 927) is mandatory — agencies can't contract around it.

| Government level | Payment deadline | Penalty rate | Governing statute |
|-----------------|-----------------|--------------|-------------------|
| State agencies | 45 days from undisputed invoice | Director of Finance rate (~7-10%) | Gov. Code § 927 |
| Most counties/cities | 30 days (varies by ordinance) | Varies by jurisdiction | Local ordinance |
| Caltrans/CDOT | 30 days from certification | Same as state rate | PCC § 7107 |
| Federal (applies in CA) | 30 days | IRS underpayment rate | Prompt Payment Act, 31 USC § 3903 |

If you're a subcontractor on a public works project, you get the construction prompt payment rules *plus* the government prompt payment rules — whichever is more favorable applies.

### 3. Commercial transactions: Civil Code § 3289(b) and Commercial Code § 1305

For general B2B transactions — consulting fees, software licenses, professional services — California provides two interest mechanisms:

**Civil Code § 3289(b):** If a contract doesn't specify an interest rate for late payment, the statutory rate is **10% per annum** on any amount due under a written contract. This is automatic — you don't need to prove anything beyond the existence of a written contract and a missed payment.

**Commercial Code § 1305:** For transactions governed by the UCC (sale of goods), the interest rate on unpaid amounts is the Federal Reserve discount rate plus 2%, adjusted every six months.

The practical difference: § 3289(b) gives you a flat 10%, which is almost always higher than the Commercial Code rate. If your transaction involves services rather than goods, § 3289(b) is your statute.

### 4. Freelancers: Labor Code § 2778 (SB 988)

Since January 1, 2025, the Freelance Worker Protection Act adds another layer. For any freelance contract worth $250 or more:

- Payment is due on or before the date specified in the contract
- If no date is specified, payment is due **within 30 days** of completion
- Double damages for willful failure to pay on time
- Attorney fees to the prevailing freelancer

The SB 988 penalties stack on top of § 3289(b) interest. A client who's 90 days late on a $10,000 freelance invoice faces: the original $10,000, plus $10,000 in double damages, plus 10% annualized interest, plus attorney fees. That's a $20,000+ exposure on what started as a $10,000 bill.

## How to use prompt payment statutes in your demand letter

The demand letter is where these statutes do their best work — before litigation, when the cost-benefit analysis is staring the debtor in the face. A well-drafted demand letter should:

**Identify the specific statute.** Don't just cite "California law." Name the section: "Under Civil Code § 8818, your failure to pay within the required 7-day period entitles our client to a penalty of 2% per month on the amount wrongfully withheld." Specificity signals competence.

**Calculate the running penalty.** Show the math: principal × penalty rate × months overdue = current exposure. Update this number in every follow-up communication. Watching the penalty climb is a powerful motivator.

**Note the attorney fee provision.** Many prompt payment statutes include fee-shifting. If the debtor knows they'll pay your legal fees on top of the penalties, the rational move is to settle early.

**Set a deadline shorter than the next penalty accrual.** If penalties compound monthly, give the debtor a 14-day deadline — they'll know the meter is running.

A [flat-fee demand letter](/blog/how-much-does-demand-letter-cost-california-2026) that cites the correct prompt payment statute converts what feels like a collections nuisance into a statutory violation with compounding penalties. That reframing changes the debtor's calculus.

## Decision tree: Which statute applies to your situation?

```
START: What type of work?
│
├── Construction (sub/GC/supplier)?
│   └── YES → Civil Code §§ 8812-8822 (7-day rule, 2%/month)
│
├── Government contract?
│   ├── State → Gov. Code § 927 (45 days)
│   └── Local → Check local ordinance (usually 30 days)
│
├── Freelance/independent contractor ($250+)?
│   └── YES → Labor Code § 2778 (SB 988) + Civil Code § 3289(b)
│
└── Other B2B (consulting, services, goods)?
    ├── Written contract, no interest clause → Civil Code § 3289(b) (10%/year)
    ├── Written contract, with interest clause → Contract rate (if lawful)
    └── Sale of goods → Commercial Code § 1305
```

## Common defenses — and why they usually fail

**"Our contract says net-60."** Irrelevant if a prompt payment statute sets a shorter deadline. Construction subs get paid in 7 days regardless of contract terms. Freelancers get paid in 30 days if the contract is silent.

**"We're disputing the work quality."** A legitimate dispute delays the prompt payment clock only if the payer gives timely written notice of the dispute. Under the construction prompt payment act, the payer must identify the specific items in dispute — they can't withhold the entire payment over a complaint about 5% of the work.

**"We haven't been paid by our client."** Pay-if-paid clauses are void in California construction under Civil Code § 8122. For other B2B relationships, the question is whether the contract makes payment truly conditional (rare and disfavored) or merely sets a timing expectation (common and unenforceable as a defense).

**"The invoice wasn't submitted properly."** This works only if the payer objected in writing within a reasonable time and specified exactly what was wrong with the invoice. Sitting on a technically deficient invoice for 60 days and then claiming it was submitted incorrectly looks like bad faith — and judges treat it accordingly.

## The Bottom Line

California doesn't treat late B2B payments as a mere inconvenience — it treats them as statutory violations with real penalties. The construction prompt payment act imposes 2% monthly penalties plus attorney fees. SB 988 doubles the damages for freelancers. And even for generic commercial transactions, § 3289(b) hands you 10% annual interest automatically on any written contract. The move is to identify which statute applies, calculate the running penalty, and put that number in a demand letter with a short deadline. Most businesses pay when they see the math.
