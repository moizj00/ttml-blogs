**Short answer: Winning a small claims judgment in California doesn't automatically put money in your account. You become a "judgment creditor" and must use California's enforcement tools — wage garnishments, bank levies, property liens, and debtor examinations — to actually collect. The court won't do this for you. But the tools are powerful, and most debtors pay once they realize you know how to use them.**

## The gap between winning and collecting

Here's the uncomfortable truth about small claims court: roughly 80% of judgments go uncollected. Not because they're uncollectable, but because winners don't know what to do next. The judge bangs the gavel, you get a piece of paper that says "Judgment for plaintiff in the amount of $X," and then... nothing happens automatically.

The California court system explicitly says it will not collect the judgment for you. You have to do the work. But the state gives judgment creditors a remarkably robust toolkit — and once you understand the sequence, collection becomes mechanical rather than mysterious.

First, a timing note: the judgment debtor has **30 days** to appeal a small claims decision (CCP § 116.710). No enforcement actions during this window. If they do appeal, the case gets a new trial in superior court — but they must also post bond or pay the judgment into court.

## Step 1: The judgment debtor statement of assets

Within 30 days of a small claims judgment, the debtor must complete and serve a Judgment Debtor's Statement of Assets (form SC-133) unless they pay in full or appeal. Under CCP § 116.830, failure to file this form is punishable as contempt of court.

This form is your roadmap. It lists the debtor's employer, bank accounts, real property, vehicles, and other assets. Many debtors ignore it — which gives you grounds for a debtor examination (more on that below).

## Step 2: The writ of execution

To use any enforcement tool, you need a writ of execution from the court clerk (form EJ-130). Cost: approximately $40-60 depending on the county. The writ is valid for **180 days** and can be renewed.

The writ goes to the county sheriff or a registered process server, who executes the levy on your behalf. You direct the sheriff to the specific asset — you're the one who tells them which bank, which employer, which property.

## Your enforcement toolkit

### Wage garnishment (earnings withholding order)

The most reliable collection tool for employed debtors. Under CCP § 706.010, you file an Application for Earnings Withholding Order (form WG-001). The sheriff serves the order on the debtor's employer, who must withhold the lesser of:

- **25% of the debtor's disposable earnings**, or
- The amount by which disposable earnings exceed **40 times the state minimum wage per week**

For 2026, with California's minimum wage at $16.50/hour, that floor is $660/week. If the debtor earns $1,000/week in disposable income, the garnishment is $250/week (25%). If they earn $700/week, the garnishment is $40/week ($700 - $660).

The garnishment continues until the judgment is paid in full — you don't need to refile each pay period.

| Income level (weekly disposable) | Garnishment amount | Effective rate |
|--------------------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| $660 or less | $0 (exempt) | 0% |
| $700 | $40 | 5.7% |
| $1,000 | $250 | 25% |
| $1,500 | $375 | 25% |
| $2,000 | $500 | 25% |

### Bank levy

Under CCP § 700.140, the sheriff can levy the debtor's bank account. This is a one-time grab — the sheriff freezes the account, notifies the debtor, and if no exemption claim is filed within **10 days**, the funds are turned over to you.

The bank levy is powerful but imprecise. You need to know which bank the debtor uses (the statement of assets should tell you, or a debtor examination). The debtor can claim an exemption for amounts necessary for living expenses under CCP § 703.520 — the "necessity exemption" protects approximately $1,950 for a single person (adjusted periodically).

**Pro tip:** Levy right after payday. Account balances are highest, and the amounts above the exemption threshold are largest.

### Real property lien (abstract of judgment)

Recording an abstract of judgment (form EJ-001) with the county recorder in any county where the debtor owns real property creates a **10-year lien** under CCP § 697.310. The lien attaches to all real property the debtor currently owns in that county — and to any they acquire during the lien period.

The lien doesn't force an immediate sale. But it means the debtor can't sell or refinance without paying your judgment first. For debtors who own a home, this is often the most effective long-term enforcement tool because eventually they'll want to sell or refi, and your lien is sitting there.

### Vehicle lien

Under CCP § 697.530, you can create a lien on the debtor's vehicle by filing with the DMV. Combined with a till tap order or keeper levy on a business, this covers mobile assets.

### Debtor examination (order for appearance)

If the statement of assets wasn't filed or wasn't complete, you can compel the debtor to appear in court for an examination under CCP § 708.110. The judge can order the debtor to answer questions, under oath, about:

- Where they work
- What banks they use
- What real property they own
- What vehicles, equipment, or other assets they have
- Who owes them money (accounts receivable)

Failure to appear is contempt of court — which can mean a bench warrant. This is the tool that makes judgment debtors take collection seriously.

> **California statute spotlight:** CCP § 708.120 allows the judgment creditor to subpoena third parties — banks, employers, business associates — to the debtor examination. If the debtor won't tell you where the money is, the people who hold the money might have to.

## Comparison: Which enforcement tool for which debtor?

| Debtor profile | Best tool | Why |
|---------------|-----------|-----|
| W-2 employee | Wage garnishment | Steady, automatic, hard to evade |
| Self-employed/1099 | Bank levy + debtor exam | No employer to garnish; find the accounts |
| Homeowner | Abstract of judgment (lien) | They'll need to clear it eventually |
| Business owner | Keeper levy on business | Sheriff parks at the register |
| Evasive/uncooperative | Debtor examination + contempt | Court compels disclosure under oath |
| Out-of-state (assets in CA) | Domesticate + levy CA assets | File abstract in county where assets sit |

## The timeline: What collection looks like in practice

```
Day 0   ──── Judgment entered
Day 1-30 ──── Appeal period (no enforcement)
Day 30  ──── Debtor must file Statement of Assets (SC-133)
Day 31  ──── Obtain writ of execution (EJ-130)
Day 35  ──── File earnings withholding order OR bank levy
Day 45  ──── If debtor didn't file SC-133: schedule debtor examination
Day 60  ──── Record abstract of judgment for real property lien
Day 90  ──── First garnishment payments arrive
Day 180 ──── Renew writ if needed
Year 10 ──── Renew judgment (CCP § 683.110) — good for another 10 years
```

## What about post-judgment interest?

California adds interest to your judgment at **10% per year** under CCP § 685.010. This is not negotiable — it accrues automatically from the date of the judgment. On a $10,000 judgment, that's $1,000/year, or roughly $2.74/day. After two years of non-payment, your $10,000 judgment is worth $12,000.

Post-judgment interest applies to the entire unpaid balance, including costs. It compounds annually. And it's added on top of whatever enforcement costs you incur — the debtor pays for the writ, the sheriff's fees, and the bank levy charges.

## When to send a demand letter before going to court

If you haven't filed your small claims case yet, a [demand letter](/blog/demand-letter-for-unpaid-invoices-california-2026) is the most cost-effective first step. California small claims judges look favorably on plaintiffs who attempted to resolve the dispute before filing. And in practice, a formal demand letter resolves many disputes without the need for a court appearance.

For a debtor who's already lost in small claims and hasn't paid, a post-judgment demand letter can also be effective — especially one that details the specific enforcement tools you plan to use. "I will be filing a wage garnishment with your employer at [Company Name] on [date]" tends to concentrate minds.

## The Bottom Line

A judgment is a hunting license, not a check. The court declared you're owed money — now you enforce it. Start with the statement of assets to find where the money sits. Garnish wages for employed debtors. Levy bank accounts when you know the bank. Record a lien on real property for the long game. And use the debtor examination to compel disclosure when the debtor goes quiet. California's 10% post-judgment interest means time is on your side — the judgment only gets bigger. The debtor's best move is to pay. Your job is to make sure they know it.
