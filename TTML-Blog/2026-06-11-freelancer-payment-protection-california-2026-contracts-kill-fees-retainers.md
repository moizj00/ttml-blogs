Every freelancer collection story starts the same way: finished the work, sent the invoice, waited, waited, waited. But here's the part nobody wants to hear — by the time you're chasing payment, you've already lost the negotiation. The real leverage existed weeks or months earlier, in the terms you did or didn't put in writing.

**Short answer: California freelancers have strong statutory protections under SB 988 (the Freelance Worker Protection Act), which requires written contracts for engagements over $250, mandates payment within 30 days of completion, and allows double damages for violations. But the statute is a floor, not a ceiling. The right contract clauses — milestone payments, kill fees, retainer structures, and payment-contingent IP transfer — do more to prevent non-payment than any collection remedy after the fact.**


## What SB 988 Changed for California Freelancers

California's Freelance Worker Protection Act (Labor Code § 2778, effective January 1, 2025) created baseline protections that didn't exist before:

> **California Labor Code § 2778 (SB 988)** — Hiring parties must provide a written contract for freelance work valued at $250 or more. The contract must include the scope of work, pay rate, and payment date. Payment must be made by the date specified in the contract, or within 30 days of work completion if no date is specified. Violations allow the freelancer to recover double the unpaid amount plus attorney fees.

The double-damages provision is the teeth. A client who owes you $5,000 and refuses to pay now faces a $10,000 exposure. But SB 988 is reactive — it helps you collect after a problem. The clauses below prevent the problem.

## The Five Clauses Every California Freelancer Contract Needs

### 1. Milestone Payment Structure

The single most effective protection against non-payment: don't let the balance get large enough to be worth fighting over.

**Day 0 (signing):** 30–50% deposit before work begins.
**Milestone 1:** 25% at first deliverable approval.
**Milestone 2:** Remaining balance due on final delivery.

If the client balks at a deposit, that's information. Clients who intend to pay don't resist paying early. The deposit also creates psychological commitment — they've invested and are more likely to see the project through.

### 2. Kill-Fee Clause

A kill fee protects you when the client cancels midstream. Without one, the client walks away and you eat the hours.


Standard kill-fee language: "If Client terminates this agreement prior to completion, Client shall pay Freelancer for all work completed to date, plus a kill fee equal to [25–50]% of the remaining contract value."

California courts enforce these provisions as liquidated damages under Civil Code § 1671, provided the percentage is reasonable relative to the anticipated harm. A 25% kill fee on a $10,000 project is almost always enforceable. A 90% kill fee might not be.

### 3. Payment-Contingent IP Transfer

This is your nuclear option — and it's perfectly legal:

"All intellectual property, deliverables, and work product created under this agreement remain the sole property of Freelancer until full payment is received. Upon receipt of final payment, all rights transfer to Client."

Until they pay, you own the work. They can't use it, publish it, or modify it. This clause converts every late payment into an IP infringement exposure for the client — a much stronger position than simply being owed money.

### 4. Late-Payment Interest Clause

> **California Civil Code § 3289(b)** — When a contract does not specify an interest rate, prejudgment interest accrues at 10% per year. A contract can specify a different reasonable rate.

Include a clause like: "Invoices unpaid after 30 days accrue interest at 1.5% per month (18% annually)." California courts enforce [reasonable contractual interest rates](/blog/interest-on-unpaid-business-debts-california-prejudgment) up to the usury limit (10% for non-exempt lenders, though business-to-business transactions are generally exempt under Article XV, § 1 of the California Constitution).

The interest clause changes the client's math: every month they delay, the bill grows.

### 5. Attorney-Fee Shifting Clause

"In any dispute arising under this agreement, the prevailing party shall be entitled to recover reasonable attorney fees and costs."


Without this clause, each side pays its own legal fees — which means a client can stonewall a $3,000 invoice knowing you'd spend more than that to sue. With it, the client pays your attorney fees if you win. The economics of fighting versus paying flip completely.

## Retainer Structures: Getting Paid Before You Work

| Retainer Type | How It Works | Best For |
|--------------|-------------|----------|
| **Prepaid retainer (trust account)** | Client deposits funds upfront; you bill against the balance | Ongoing relationships, monthly service agreements |
| **Earned retainer (flat fee)** | Client pays a fixed amount for a defined scope; non-refundable once work begins | Project-based work with clear deliverables |
| **Evergreen retainer** | Client maintains a minimum balance; auto-replenishes when it drops below threshold | Long-term clients with recurring needs |

The prepaid retainer eliminates the collection problem entirely. You never do more work than the money on deposit. When the balance runs low, work pauses until it's replenished. No chasing, no demand letters, no small claims.

## How These Clauses Stack in a Real Dispute

Consider a $8,000 web development project with all five clauses in place:

**Day 0:** Client pays $3,200 deposit (40%).
**Week 4:** First milestone delivered, client approves. Client pays $2,000 (25%).
**Week 8:** Final delivery. Client owes $2,800 and goes silent.

Your position:
- You hold the IP (clause 3) — the website isn't theirs until they pay
- Interest is accruing at 1.5%/month (clause 4)
- SB 988 entitles you to double damages: $5,600
- Attorney fees are recoverable (clause 5)
- Total exposure for the client: $2,800 + $5,600 + interest + attorney fees = roughly $9,000+

A [demand letter](/blog/california-freelance-worker-protection-act-sb-988-guide) laying out this math usually ends the standoff. The client's cost of not paying exceeds the original invoice by a factor of three.

## When No Contract Exists

If you're already in a dispute without a written contract, SB 988 itself becomes your leverage — the hiring party violated the statute by not providing one, and that violation strengthens your position in any claim. You can still [collect under an implied or oral contract](/blog/no-written-contract-can-i-still-collect-california-freelancer) in California, and the absence of a written agreement counts against the client, not you.
