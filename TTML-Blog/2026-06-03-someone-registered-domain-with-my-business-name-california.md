If someone registered a domain containing your business name to profit from your brand or sell it back to you, that's likely cybersquatting, and you have two ways to recover the domain: a lawsuit under the federal Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act (ACPA), 15 U.S.C. § 1125(d), or a faster, cheaper arbitration under the Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy (UDRP) that every major registrar agrees to. California businesses also have unfair-competition claims under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200.

**Short answer:** Send a demand to the registrant first. If they refuse, file a UDRP complaint to transfer the domain (weeks, low cost) or sue under the ACPA for transfer plus statutory damages of $1,000 to $100,000 per domain when bad faith is clear.

## What counts as cybersquatting versus a legitimate registration?

The ACPA requires bad-faith intent to profit from your mark. Classic signs: the registrant offered to sell you the domain, parked it with ads, used it to divert your customers, or registered many domains matching well-known brands. Someone who registered a similar name for a genuine, unrelated business is usually not a squatter. Having a registered or strong common-law trademark makes your claim much stronger.

## Is UDRP or an ACPA lawsuit the better route?

UDRP is administered through providers like the WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center, costs far less than litigation, and typically resolves in roughly two months — but the only remedy is transfer or cancellation of the domain. The ACPA is slower and pricier but lets you recover money damages and reach registrants who hide overseas. Most California businesses start with UDRP unless they want damages or the squatter is also infringing offline.

## What should you do before filing anything?

Document everything — screenshots of the parked page, any sale offers, and your trademark use predating the registration. Then send a cease and desist. Many squatters fold once a real demand arrives. ## What if the registrant is anonymous or overseas?

Many squatters hide behind privacy services or register from outside the United States, which can make a normal lawsuit impractical. The ACPA anticipates this. It allows an "in rem" action filed against the domain name itself — in the judicial district where the registry or registrar is located — even when you can't identify or serve the human behind it. The remedy in that proceeding is limited to transfer or cancellation of the domain, but that's often exactly what you want.

UDRP is also well suited to anonymous registrants, because the proceeding runs through the registrar by contract and doesn't require you to physically serve anyone. For a hidden or foreign squatter, UDRP or an in rem ACPA action is usually more realistic than a standard damages suit.

## How do you reduce the risk of future squatting?

Once you recover the domain, protect your brand going forward. Register the key variations of your name (common extensions and obvious misspellings), keep your registrations on auto-renew so they never lapse, and consider a watch service that alerts you when someone registers a domain containing your mark. A registered trademark also makes every future UDRP or ACPA claim faster and stronger. Prevention is far cheaper than recovery.
