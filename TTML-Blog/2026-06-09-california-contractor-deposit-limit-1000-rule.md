**Short answer:** California Bus. & Prof. Code § 7159(d)(1) limits home improvement contractor deposits to $1,000 or 10% of the total contract price — whichever is less. If your contractor took more than that upfront, they've violated the law regardless of whether the work is good. That violation strengthens every other legal claim you might have.

## The math most homeowners get wrong

The rule isn't "$1,000 or 10%." It's "$1,000 or 10%, whichever is *less*."

On a $15,000 kitchen remodel, 10% is $1,500 — but the cap is still $1,000 because $1,000 is less than $1,500. The contractor can only take $1,000 down.

On a $8,000 bathroom job, 10% is $800. Since $800 is less than $1,000, the cap is $800.

The only time the deposit legally exceeds $1,000 is on contracts over $10,000 where 10% still comes out below what the contractor asked for. Even then, the absolute ceiling is 10%.


## Why this violation matters beyond the deposit itself

A § 7159 deposit violation isn't just a technical infraction. It triggers three consequences:

1. **CSLB disciplinary action.** The Contractors State License Board treats deposit violations as grounds for license suspension or revocation under § 7109.
2. **Enhanced refund leverage.** When you send a demand letter to a contractor who violated the deposit cap, you're not just asking for your money back — you're reminding them that the CSLB can pull their license over this specific act.
3. **Small claims advantage.** Judges in California small claims courts treat § 7159 violations as near-automatic wins on the deposit question. The statute is clear, the math is simple, and the burden falls on the contractor to justify why they took more.

> **Bus. & Prof. Code § 7159(d)(1)** — "The down payment may not exceed $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract price, whichever is less."

## What about "materials deposits" or "special order" exceptions?

Some contractors claim they need a larger deposit for materials. The law is narrow here: § 7159(d)(1) allows a larger down payment *only* if the contractor provides a payment schedule in the contract that allocates payments to specific work milestones. Even then, no single payment can exceed the value of the work performed plus materials delivered to the site.

A contractor who says "I need 50% upfront for materials" on a home improvement job is violating the statute. Period.

## The timeline that works

**Day 0:** Document what you paid (bank records, canceled check, Venmo/Zelle receipt).
**Day 1:** Send a written demand for return of the excess deposit, citing § 7159(d)(1).
**Day 14:** If no response, file a CSLB complaint online and consider a [formal attorney demand letter](/blog/demand-letters-for-freelancers-collect-when-client-wont-pay).
**Day 30:** File in small claims if the contractor hasn't refunded the excess.

## Your position is stronger than you think

If your contractor took more than the legal deposit limit, you have a documented statutory violation in your favor before you even get to the quality-of-work question. A demand letter citing the specific code section — especially one from an attorney — often produces a refund within two weeks, because the contractor knows what the CSLB will do if this escalates.
