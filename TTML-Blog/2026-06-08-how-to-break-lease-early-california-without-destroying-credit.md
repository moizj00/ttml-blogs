**Short answer: California law gives tenants several legal grounds to break a lease without penalty — uninhabitable conditions, landlord harassment, domestic violence, active military orders, and others. Even without a legal excuse, your landlord must make reasonable efforts to re-rent the unit, and you only owe rent until they do.**

The lease feels like a cage. You signed for 12 months, something changed — a job across the state, a relationship that ended, a unit that reeks of mold — and now you're calculating the cost of staying versus the cost of leaving.

Here's what most tenants don't know: leaving early is almost never as expensive as the landlord claims. California law tilts the math in your favor, if you know which statutes to cite.


## When You Can Walk Away With Zero Penalty

California carves out several situations where a tenant can terminate a lease early and owe nothing. These aren't loopholes. They're statutory rights.

### Uninhabitable Conditions (Civ. Code § 1942)

If your landlord fails to maintain the unit in habitable condition — serious mold, no hot water, broken heating, pest infestation, structural hazards — you can invoke the repair-and-deduct remedy or, if the conditions are severe enough, vacate. This is constructive eviction: the landlord's neglect effectively ended the lease, not you.

> **California Civ. Code § 1942** — If a landlord fails to maintain the premises in a condition fit for human occupation after reasonable notice, the tenant may vacate and is discharged from further rent obligations.

The key word is "reasonable notice." Give your landlord written notice of the problem. Give them a reasonable time to fix it (courts typically say 30 days for non-emergencies). Document everything — photos, emails, certified mail receipts. If they don't fix it, you leave and the lease is over.

### Domestic Violence, Sexual Assault, Stalking, Human Trafficking, or Elder Abuse (Civ. Code § 1946.7)

A tenant who is a victim of domestic violence, sexual assault, stalking, human trafficking, or elder or dependent adult abuse can terminate a lease with 14 days' written notice. The tenant must provide either a copy of a temporary restraining order, a police report, or documentation from a qualified third party (therapist, counselor, shelter worker).

No early termination fee applies. The landlord cannot report the early departure to a credit agency as a breach.

### Active Military Duty (federal SCRA, 50 U.S.C. § 3955)

If you receive orders for a permanent change of station or a deployment of 90 days or more, the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act lets you terminate any residential lease with 30 days' written notice after the next rent payment is due. State law can't override this — it's federal.

### Landlord Harassment or Privacy Violations (Civ. Code § 1954)

Repeated unauthorized entries, utility shutoffs, lock changes, removal of doors or windows — these are all illegal landlord behaviors that can constitute constructive eviction. If your landlord is making the unit unlivable through harassment rather than neglect, the legal theory is the same: they broke the lease, not you.

### Senior Citizen or Disabled Tenant Moving to a Care Facility

California law (Civ. Code § 1946.5) allows tenants aged 62 or older, or who are disabled, to terminate a lease with 30 days' notice when relocating to a residential care facility, if the lease was signed on or after January 1, 2022.

## When You Don't Have a Legal Excuse: The Mitigation Rule

This is where most tenants actually are. You need to leave — new job, relationship change, financial crunch — and none of the statutory exits apply. You're breaking the lease for personal reasons.

The landlord's first move is usually to quote the remaining rent on the lease. Eight months left at $2,400? That's $19,200, they'll say.

But California doesn't let them sit back and collect.

> **California Civ. Code § 1951.2** — When a tenant breaches a lease by abandoning the premises, the landlord's damages are limited to the rent that would have been earned, *minus the amount that could have been reasonably avoided* — i.e., by re-renting the unit.

This is called the **duty to mitigate**. Your landlord must make reasonable efforts to find a new tenant. They can't leave the unit empty for six months, collect your rent the whole time, and call it your fault. If the unit re-rents in three weeks — which it often does in California's tight rental markets — you owe three weeks of rent, not eight months.

### The Step-by-Step Playbook for a Clean Exit

**Day 0:** Decide to leave. Don't just disappear — that makes everything worse.

**Day 1–3:** Review your lease for an early termination clause. Some leases include one — typically one or two months' rent as a buyout. If it's there, use it. It's the cleanest path.

**Day 3–7:** Give your landlord written notice. State your move-out date (give at least 30 days). Offer to cooperate with showings, keep the unit clean, and make the transition easy. This letter establishes the timeline and your good faith.

**Day 7–14:** If your landlord threatens to charge you for the full remaining lease or report you to collections, send a formal response citing Civ. Code § 1951.2 and the duty to mitigate. An [attorney demand letter](/blog/how-much-does-a-demand-letter-cost-in-california-the-full-breakdown-2026) is the most effective version of this response.

**Day 30:** Move out. Return keys. Document the unit's condition with timestamped photos.

**Day 30–51:** Your landlord must return your security deposit (or an itemized statement of deductions) within 21 days of your departure per Civ. Code § 1950.5. If they try to deduct the remaining lease rent from your deposit, that's a separate fight — and one you can win.

## How to Protect Your Credit

The credit question is what keeps most tenants trapped. Here's the reality:

A landlord can't directly report a lease break to the credit bureaus. What they *can* do is send an unpaid balance to collections, and the collection agency reports it. The way to prevent that:

| Action | Effect on Credit |
|---|---|
| Pay the early termination fee (if your lease has one) | No impact — clean exit |
| Negotiate a written buyout agreement | No impact — document it |
| Leave with notice + landlord re-rents quickly | Minimal exposure — you owe only the gap |
| Leave without notice, ignore landlord's demands | High risk — balance goes to collections |
| Dispute an inflated claim in writing (citing § 1951.2) | Prevents unjust collection — paper trail protects you |

The pattern: communicate in writing, cite the statute, and don't let a landlord manufacture a debt that the law doesn't support.
