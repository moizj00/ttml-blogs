Discovering a squatter in a property you own is alarming, and the instinct is to change the locks and force them out. In California, that instinct is also illegal. Even someone with no right to be in the property generally can't be removed by self-help — you have to use the lawful process. The good news is that the process exists and works; the key is distinguishing a true squatter from someone who has acquired tenant rights, and then acting by the book.

**Short answer:** Don't lock them out — that's illegal in California (Civil Code § 789.3). Remove a squatter through the formal process: a written notice to quit followed, if needed, by an unlawful detainer lawsuit. Involve law enforcement only for genuine criminal trespass.

## Squatter vs. tenant vs. trespasser: why the distinction matters

The label determines your path:

- **A trespasser** just broke in and has no colorable claim to be there. If it's a clear, recent break-in, this can be a criminal matter for the police.
- **A squatter** is occupying without permission but has been there long enough, or with enough apparent color of occupancy, that police often treat it as a "civil matter" and decline to remove them — pushing you toward the eviction process.
- **A tenant** has permission (even informal) or has paid rent that was accepted, which creates tenant rights and a right to the full eviction process.

The hard cases sit between trespasser and tenant. Once someone has established occupancy and law enforcement won't act, you generally must treat removal as a civil eviction, not a police call.

## Why you can't just lock them out

California strictly prohibits "self-help" evictions. Under Civil Code § 789.3, a landlord (or owner) can't lock out an occupant, remove their belongings, shut off utilities, or use threats to force them out. Doing so exposes you to statutory penalties and damages — and can flip the dynamic so that you, the rightful owner, end up liable. However satisfying a lockout sounds, it's the single most expensive mistake you can make here.

## The lawful removal process

For a squatter who has established occupancy, the path mirrors a standard eviction:

1. **Serve a written notice to quit.** Because there's no lease and no rent owed, this is typically a notice to vacate appropriate to the situation. Serving notice properly is essential.
2. **File an unlawful detainer** if they don't leave. This is the formal court eviction lawsuit. The court process gives the occupant a chance to respond, but for a true squatter with no right to possession, the owner is on strong ground.
3. **Get a judgment and writ of possession.** If you win, the court issues a writ, and the **sheriff** — not you — carries out the physical removal.

Only law enforcement executes the removal. Your job is to run the legal process correctly so it gets there.

## What about adverse possession?

Owners sometimes fear a squatter will "claim the property." Adverse possession in California has demanding requirements — including open and continuous possession for five years **and payment of the property taxes** during that period, among other elements. The tax-payment requirement alone defeats the vast majority of squatter claims. It's a reason to act promptly, but not a reason to panic or resort to self-help.

## When can the police help?

For a genuine, fresh trespass — someone who just broke in and has no plausible claim of residency — law enforcement may treat it as criminal trespass and remove the person. The challenge is that once an occupant asserts they live there, many departments decline to make that judgment call and direct the owner to civil court. Calling the police early, before occupancy looks established, gives you the best chance of a criminal-trespass resolution. Document the break-in (forced entry, no belongings, no utilities in their name) to support that characterization.

## How to protect your property

- **Monitor vacant properties.** Squatters target units that look unoccupied. Regular checks, secured entries, and visible maintenance deter them.
- **Act fast.** The sooner you respond, the more likely it's still a police-removable trespass rather than an established occupancy.
- **Keep documentation.** Photos, dates, evidence of forced entry, and proof of your ownership all support both a criminal-trespass approach and a civil filing.
- **Never negotiate yourself into a tenancy.** Accepting "rent" from a squatter can convert them into a tenant with full rights. Don't do it without understanding the consequences.

## Bottom line

Removing a squatter in California is about discipline, not force. Don't lock them out — that's illegal and turns the tables on you. Distinguish a fresh trespasser (call the police) from an established occupant (use the eviction process), serve proper notice, file an unlawful detainer if needed, and let the sheriff carry out any removal. Acting quickly and lawfully is what gets your property back without creating new liability.
