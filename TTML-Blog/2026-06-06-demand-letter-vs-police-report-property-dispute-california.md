Use a police report when a crime occurred — theft, fraud, embezzlement. Use a demand letter when the dispute is civil — unpaid debts, unreturned property after a breakup or business deal, broken promises, botched services. The frustrating reality most Californians discover: police treat the vast majority of money disputes as civil matters and will not investigate them, no matter how wronged you feel. A demand letter, followed by small claims court if necessary, is the tool that actually recovers money in civil disputes.

**Short answer:** If someone took your property by deception or without any claim of right, file a police report. If they owe you money, kept a deposit, failed to deliver, or won't return property they originally received lawfully, that's civil — send a demand letter. When in doubt, you can do both; they are not mutually exclusive.

## Why Do Police Refuse Most Money Disputes?

Because criminal theft requires intent to steal, and a dispute about who owes what doesn't establish it. If a contractor took your deposit and did shoddy work, he intended to do the job (badly) — that's breach of contract, not larceny. If your ex keeps the TV you say is yours, she claims a right to it — that's civil conversion. Officers are trained to label these "civil standby at most" situations. Pushing a criminal framing where it doesn't fit wastes weeks you could spend on a recovery path that works.

The flip side: genuine fraud — someone who took payment never intending to deliver, fake identities, forged documents — is criminal (Penal Code § 484 defines theft to include obtaining money by fraudulent representation, and § 487 makes it grand theft above $950). Report it.

## What Does a Police Report Actually Get You?

For a true crime: a case number, possible investigation, and restitution if there's a conviction — though restitution depends on prosecution, which you don't control, and recovery can take years. For a civil dispute: documentation value only. A report can still be worth filing when fraud is plausible, because the report itself becomes evidence of how and when you discovered the wrongdoing, and insurance claims sometimes require one. But no one at the station will call your debtor and demand payment.

## What Does a Demand Letter Get You?

Control and speed. A demand letter states your claim, the amount, the deadline, and the consequence — typically a small claims filing (up to $12,500 for individuals). It frequently produces payment, a payment plan, or return of property within weeks because the recipient can see the lawsuit coming and knows exactly what losing costs. It also builds your court file: judges expect a pre-filing demand, and the letter plus any reply (or silence) frames the dispute on your terms.

The statute of limitations keeps running either way — four years on written contracts (CCP § 337), three years for taking of personal property (CCP § 338(c)), two years on oral agreements (CCP § 339) — so don't let a stalled police report consume your civil deadline.

## When Should You Do Both?

When the facts genuinely support both: an employee who embezzled (criminal) also owes the money back (civil); a seller who took payment for goods that never existed committed fraud (criminal) and breached the contract (civil). File the report, then pursue the civil claim in parallel — you do not have to wait for prosecutors. One caution: never threaten criminal prosecution in a demand letter to gain advantage in the civil dispute. California's State Bar rules prohibit attorneys from doing this, and from anyone it can constitute extortion. State the civil claim and the civil consequence; report crimes to police separately.

## What's the Practical Decision Rule?

Ask one question: did the person obtain the money or property through deception from the start, or did a legitimate transaction go bad afterward? Deception from the start — police report plus civil claim. Transaction gone bad — demand letter, then small claims.
