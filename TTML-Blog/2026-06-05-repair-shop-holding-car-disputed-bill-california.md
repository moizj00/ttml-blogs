A California repair shop can legally hold your car under a possessory lien (Civil Code § 3068) — but only for charges you actually authorized. If the shop did work you never approved, never gave you a written estimate, or padded the bill, the lien may be invalid and you may be entitled to get your car back without paying the disputed amount.

**Short answer:** A shop's right to keep your car depends on whether it followed the Automotive Repair Act. No written estimate or unauthorized work can void the lien and expose the shop to penalties under the Consumers Legal Remedies Act.

## Can a repair shop legally keep my car in California?

Yes — but conditionally. Under Civil Code § 3068, a repair facility has a possessory lien for the reasonable value of labor and parts it was authorized to provide. The key word is *authorized*. California's Automotive Repair Act (Business & Professions Code § 9884 and following) requires the shop to give you a written estimate before starting work and to get your approval before exceeding it.

If the shop skipped the estimate, performed work you never approved, or charged far above the estimate without consent, the lien can be challenged and the shop may face discipline from the Bureau of Automotive Repair (BAR).

## What makes the lien invalid?

A lien is vulnerable when the shop: started repairs without a signed written estimate; performed additional work without documented authorization; charged more than 10% over the estimate without your approval; or cannot show you authorized the specific charges. Each of these violates the Automotive Repair Act and can strip the shop of its right to hold the car.

## How do I get my car back without paying the disputed bill?

Start by asking the shop for the written estimate and the work-authorization documents in writing. If they cannot produce them, point out the Automotive Repair Act violation and demand release of the vehicle. File a complaint with the Bureau of Automotive Repair, which can investigate and pressure the shop to resolve the dispute.

If the shop still refuses, a demand letter that cites Civil Code § 3068 and the Automotive Repair Act often resolves the standoff quickly — shops know an invalid lien can turn into a conversion claim and CLRA penalties. As a last resort, you can pay under protest to recover the car and then sue in small claims court (limit $12,500 for individuals under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220) to recover the overcharge.

## What if the shop already sold or threatens to sell my car?

A lienholder must follow strict notice and sale procedures before disposing of a vehicle. Selling your car without proper notice can be conversion, exposing the shop to damages. If you receive a lien-sale notice, act immediately — these deadlines are short, and once a sale completes, recovery is far harder.

## When is a demand letter the right move?

If the shop is holding your car or refusing to refund an unauthorized charge, a firm, statute-citing letter often breaks the impasse faster and cheaper than a lawsuit. It signals you understand the Automotive Repair Act and are prepared to escalate to BAR and the courts.
