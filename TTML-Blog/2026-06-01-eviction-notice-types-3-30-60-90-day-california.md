Every California eviction begins with a notice, and choosing the right one is where landlords most often go wrong. The notice type depends on why you're ending the tenancy and how long the tenant has lived there — and serving the wrong notice, or filling it out incorrectly, can force you to start over and lose weeks. This guide explains the main notice types, when each applies, and how the Tenant Protection Act (AB 1482) changes the picture for covered units.

**Short answer:** Use a 3-day notice for at-fault reasons like unpaid rent or lease violations; 30- or 60-day notices to end a no-fault tenancy (30 if under a year, 60 if a year or more); and 90-day notices in specific cases like some subsidized tenancies. Just cause rules apply to covered units.

## The 3-day notice (at-fault)

The 3-day notice is for situations where the tenant has done something that justifies ending the tenancy. The main variants:

- **3-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit** (CCP § 1161(2)): for unpaid rent. It must state the exact amount owed and give the tenant three days to pay or leave. Overstating the amount is a classic fatal error.
- **3-Day Notice to Perform Covenant or Quit** (CCP § 1161(3)): for a curable lease violation — like unauthorized pets, subletting, or other breaches. The tenant gets three days to fix the problem or vacate.
- **3-Day Notice to Quit** (incurable): for serious, non-curable conduct such as significant property damage, illegal activity, or creating a nuisance. No opportunity to cure.

Note that the three days generally exclude weekends and judicial holidays for pay-or-quit notices, which affects the deadline.

## The 30-day and 60-day notices (no-fault)

When you're ending a month-to-month tenancy without tenant fault, the notice period depends on length of tenancy:

- **30-Day Notice:** generally used when the tenant has lived in the unit for less than one year (CCP § 1946.1).
- **60-Day Notice:** generally required when the tenant has lived there for a year or more.

Important: for tenancies covered by AB 1482, you can't simply serve a no-fault notice to end the tenancy after 12 months — you need a recognized just cause (such as owner move-in or removal from the market), and no-fault just causes require relocation assistance. The 30/60-day distinction governs timing; just cause governs whether you can terminate at all.

## The 90-day notice

A 90-day notice applies in specific circumstances, most commonly certain subsidized tenancies (such as some Section 8 situations) and particular post-foreclosure scenarios where federal or state law requires longer notice. These are narrower cases, but using a shorter notice where 90 days is required will sink the action.

## How just cause changes everything

For units covered by AB 1482 (and stricter local ordinances), the notice is only half the story. You also need a valid reason:

- **At-fault just cause** pairs with the appropriate 3-day notice (with a cure opportunity for curable breaches).
- **No-fault just cause** pairs with a 30/60-day notice and requires relocation assistance.

If your unit is exempt (for example, a single-family home with the proper exemption notice, or new construction under the rolling 15-year window), the just-cause requirement may not apply — but the notice-period rules still do.

## What a notice must contain

Whatever the type, the notice generally must:

- Identify the tenant and the property.
- State the reason and, for pay-or-quit, the exact amount due.
- Give the correct number of days.
- Be served in a legally valid way (personal service, substituted service, or post-and-mail as allowed).

Defects in any of these — wrong amount, wrong period, improper service — are the leading reasons evictions get dismissed and have to restart.

## What happens after the notice?

The notice itself isn't the eviction. If the tenant doesn't comply (pay, cure, or move out) within the period, the next step is filing an unlawful detainer lawsuit — the formal court eviction process. Self-help measures like changing the locks or removing belongings remain illegal at every stage (Civil Code § 789.3). The notice simply opens the door to the lawful court process.

## Practical guidance

- Match the notice to the reason: at-fault → 3-day; no-fault → 30/60-day; special cases → 90-day.
- For covered units, confirm you have a valid just cause before serving anything.
- Get the numbers exactly right — especially the rent amount and the day count.
- Serve the notice properly and keep proof of service.
- When in doubt, have the notice reviewed; a small error is cheaper to fix before service than after a dismissal.

## Bottom line

The right California eviction notice depends on why you're ending the tenancy and how long the tenant has been there — 3-day for at-fault, 30/60-day for no-fault, 90-day for special cases — all layered with AB 1482's just-cause rules for covered units. Precision is everything: the correct notice, accurate contents, and proper service are what keep an eviction on track instead of back at square one.
