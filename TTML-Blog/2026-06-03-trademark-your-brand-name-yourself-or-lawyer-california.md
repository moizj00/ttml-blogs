You can legally file your own trademark application in California — either a federal mark through the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) under the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1051) or a state mark through the California Secretary of State under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 14200 et seq. Nothing requires you to hire an attorney. But roughly a third of self-filed applications hit avoidable refusals, and a defective filing can cost you priority and money. Whether to DIY depends on how crowded your market is and how much the name matters to your business.

**Short answer:** Yes, you can trademark a brand name yourself in California. DIY makes sense for a distinctive name with no obvious conflicts. Hire a lawyer when your name is descriptive, when a clearance search shows similar marks, or when the brand is central to your revenue.

## What does it actually cost to file a trademark yourself?

A federal application costs $350 per class of goods or services using the USPTO's standard TEAS application. A California state registration through the Secretary of State is far cheaper — currently a modest per-class filing fee — but it only protects you inside California. If you sell online to customers nationwide, the federal mark is usually the better investment because it gives you rights across all 50 states and the ability to record your mark with Amazon Brand Registry and customs.

## When is filing it yourself a reasonable choice?

DIY filing works best when your brand name is "fanciful" or "arbitrary" — an invented word or a real word unrelated to your product (think a made-up name, not "Fresh Coffee" for a café). These distinctive marks face fewer refusals. Run a free knockout search on the USPTO's trademark database first. If nothing close comes up in your category, a careful self-filer can often get through the process.

## When should you hire a lawyer instead?

Get help when your name is descriptive (it describes what you sell), when a search turns up similar marks, or when an examining attorney issues an "Office Action" you don't understand. Attorneys also catch the most expensive mistake DIY filers make: listing the wrong goods or filing in the wrong class, which can sink the whole application. If the brand is the face of your business, the cost of a lawyer is small next to the cost of losing the name.

## What's the difference between protecting and enforcing the mark?

Registration is only step one. If someone copies your name later, you'll need to enforce it — often starting with a cease and desist letter. For more on that, see [What Is a Trademark Cease and Desist Letter and When Does It Work?](/blog/2026-05-13-what-is-a-trademark-cease-and-desist-letter) and our guide to [California Intellectual Property Laws for Small Businesses](/blog/2026-05-27-california-intellectual-property-laws-small-businesses). A flat-fee attorney letter is often far cheaper than a lawsuit and resolves most disputes.
