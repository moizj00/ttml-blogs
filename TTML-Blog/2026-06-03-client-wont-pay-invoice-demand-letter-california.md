# The Client Who Won't Pay: What a Demand Letter Actually Does

You finished the work three weeks ago. The invoice went out the same day. Since then: one "still looping in finance," two emails that went nowhere, and a read receipt that lands like a shrug.

Here's what most people do next. They send a fourth polite nudge. They soften the subject line. They start to wonder if they're being difficult.

You're not being difficult. You're being slow-walked.

A late invoice stops being a misunderstanding somewhere around day sixty. By then it's a decision: yours to keep chasing, or theirs to keep stalling. A demand letter is how you take that decision back.

## What changes when it's a letter

An email asks. A demand letter notifies. That's a shift in posture, and the person on the other end feels it.

A real demand letter says three things, plainly: what you're owed, the date you expect it by, and what happens if that date passes. It points to the agreement. It states a number. And it reads like the last conversation before a courtroom, because that's what it is.

Most invoices that survived months of "checking on it" get paid within two weeks of a letter like that. Not because the letter threatens anything. Because it ends the ambiguity the other side was comfortable living in. Stalling only works while stalling is free.

## What California actually puts behind you

You have more going for you than the silence suggests.

- **Time is on your side, for now.** On a written contract, California generally gives you four years to sue (Code of Civil Procedure § 337); on a spoken deal, two. That's a long runway. But the longer you wait, the colder the trail and the shakier everyone's memory.
- **Interest is yours to claim.** When a contract doesn't name an interest rate, an unpaid debt generally earns 10% a year once it's breached (Civil Code § 3289). Put that in the letter. It quietly turns "later" into "more."
- **Small claims is closer than you think.** An individual can sue for up to $12,500 in California small claims — no lawyer required, a hearing in weeks. For most freelance and small-business invoices, that ceiling sits above the bill.

(Statutes and dollar limits change, and your situation may not fit the general rule. An attorney should confirm any of this before you rely on it.)

## The letter, in plain shape

One page. Date the work. Name the agreement. State the amount. Give a firm deadline — ten business days is standard — and say what you'll do when it passes. Attach the invoice and the contract. Send it a way that proves it arrived.

That's the whole craft. The power isn't in the wording. It's in the fact that you finally drew a line and put a date on it.
