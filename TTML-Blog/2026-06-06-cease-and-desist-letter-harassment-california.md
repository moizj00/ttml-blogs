A harassment cease and desist letter is a formal written demand — most effective on attorney letterhead — that a person stop specified conduct: repeated calls, abusive messages, showing up at your workplace, contacting your family, spreading falsehoods. It works through deterrence, not legal force: it proves the harasser knew the conduct was unwanted, which strengthens every later remedy. For dangerous or obsessive harassers, skip the letter and go directly to a civil harassment restraining order under California Code of Civil Procedure § 527.6, which carries criminal penalties when violated.

**Short answer:** Send a cease and desist when the harasser is rational and has something to lose — a job, a business, a reputation. Go straight to court for a § 527.6 restraining order when there are threats of violence, stalking, or escalating obsession. The letter is a deterrent; the order is an enforceable command backed by arrest.

## What Legal Effect Does a Cease and Desist Letter Actually Have?

None by itself — and understanding this is the key to using it well. A cease and desist letter is not a court order; ignoring it is not contempt and not arrestable. Its power is evidentiary and psychological. Evidentiary: a § 527.6 restraining order requires showing a "course of conduct" that "seriously alarms, annoys, or harasses" with no legitimate purpose — and a dated letter demanding the conduct stop, followed by continued conduct, is close to perfect proof that the behavior was knowing and unwanted. Psychological: for harassers embedded in normal life — a coworker, a business associate, a neighbor, an ex with a professional license — a letter from an attorney is often the first moment the behavior carries a visible price tag.

This dual function means the letter is rarely wasted even when ignored. It either stops the conduct or builds your court file.

## What Conduct Counts as Civil Harassment in California?

CCP § 527.6 defines harassment as unlawful violence, a credible threat of violence, or a knowing and willful course of conduct directed at a specific person that seriously alarms, annoys, or harasses, serves no legitimate purpose, and would cause a reasonable person substantial emotional distress. A "course of conduct" means a pattern — repeated calls, texts, emails, surveillance, showing up uninvited — over any period of time. Single rude incidents don't qualify; patterns do.

Note the carve-out: conduct with a legitimate purpose isn't harassment. A creditor calling about a real debt, a neighbor complaining about a real fence problem, or someone's attorney sending letters about a real dispute generally can't be enjoined as harassment — though the manner can cross the line even when the subject is legitimate. Persistent debt-collection abuse has its own remedies under the Rosenthal Act — see [a debt collector is harassing me in California](/blog/2026-06-05-debt-collector-harassment-rights-california).

## What Should a Harassment Cease and Desist Include?

Specificity, dates, and a clean demand. Identify each category of conduct with dates and examples: "Between March 3 and May 28, you called the recipient's mobile phone 47 times, including 11 calls after midnight." Demand that all contact stop — and define contact broadly: calls, texts, email, social media (including new accounts), third-party messages, and physical approaches at home or work. State the consequences accurately: a petition for a civil harassment restraining order under CCP § 527.6, civil claims where applicable, and referral to law enforcement if the conduct independently violates criminal law — repeated harassing electronic communications can violate Penal Code § 653m.

Two drafting cautions. Don't exaggerate or threaten consequences you can't deliver; a harasser who calls one bluff is emboldened. And never engage in back-and-forth after sending — the letter should designate the attorney as the sole contact point, because every reply you personally send dilutes the message that contact is over.

## When Should You Skip the Letter and Get a Restraining Order?

Three situations. Threats of violence — any credible threat moves this out of letter territory immediately; courts can issue a temporary restraining order the same day you file, without the harasser present. Stalking and surveillance — following you, tracking your location, appearing wherever you are signals obsession that a letter can inflame rather than deter. And escalation despite boundaries — if you've already clearly told them to stop and the conduct intensified, a letter is just a slower version of what already failed.

A § 527.6 order can prohibit contact, require the harasser to stay a specified distance from you, your home, your job, and your family, and lasts up to five years with renewal available. Violation is a crime (Penal Code § 273.6) — police can arrest on the spot. That enforcement gap is the whole difference: a letter asks; an order commands.

There's also a tactical consideration in the other direction: for a borderline-rational harasser, filing for a restraining order first can sometimes trigger a contested hearing where you must prove your case, while a letter resolves it quietly. An attorney who handles both can read which path fits your harasser's profile.

## Can You Recover Money Damages for Harassment?

Sometimes. Harassment often overlaps with civil claims: intentional infliction of emotional distress for extreme and outrageous conduct, defamation if falsehoods were spread, invasion of privacy for surveillance or disclosure of private facts. A demand letter can pair the cease-and-desist demand with a damages demand where losses are concrete — therapy costs, lost work, security expenses. In practice, most victims prioritize making it stop over collecting, and settlement agreements in these disputes commonly trade a release of damage claims for enforceable written conduct promises — which, unlike the original letter, can be enforced as a contract.

## What If the Harasser Is Anonymous or Online?

Document first: screenshots with timestamps and URLs, preserved messages, platform report numbers. Platforms remove some abuse on report, but for persistent anonymous harassment, identifying the person usually requires filing a lawsuit and subpoenaing the platform or ISP — a step worth attorney guidance because of cost and anti-SLAPP considerations if speech issues are entangled. If you already know who's behind it, the analysis returns to normal: letter for the rational, court for the obsessive. For falsehoods specifically, see [what is a defamation cease and desist letter](/blog/2026-06-06-what-is-a-defamation-cease-and-desist-letter-california).

## What Does Each Path Cost?

A flat-fee attorney cease and desist letter costs a few hundred dollars. A civil harassment restraining order has modest filing costs (fee waivers exist, and filing is free when the petition alleges violence or credible threats), but a contested hearing with counsel costs more. Doing the letter first costs little and often ends the matter; the key is honest triage at the start about which kind of harasser you're dealing with.
