# Your Landlord Won't Make the Repair. Here's the Letter That Moves Them.

The heater died two weeks ago. You've called twice. The answer both times: "Maintenance is aware."

Aware isn't fixed. And in California, your landlord owes you a livable home. Not as a courtesy. As a legal baseline.

That gap, between "aware" and "fixed," is where most tenants get stuck. They keep calling. The calls don't create a record, don't start a clock, and don't cost the landlord a thing. A written demand does all three.

## What "livable" means here

California reads a promise into every residential lease, whether or not anyone typed it out: the place will be fit to live in. Courts have held that since Green v. Superior Court in 1974, and the legislature listed the essentials in Civil Code § 1941.1 — working heat, safe plumbing, hot and cold water, weatherproofing, sound locks, no infestation.

A dead heater is exactly the kind of condition the law names by hand.

## Why the letter beats the call

A phone call evaporates. A letter sits in a file.

When you put the problem in writing — dated, specific, sent so you can prove it landed — you do three things at once. You give the landlord the formal notice the law expects. You start the count on what "a reasonable time to fix it" means. And you build the record that matters if this ever reaches a judge or a rent board.

Specific is the word that does the work. Not "the heating's bad." Instead: "The furnace has produced no heat since May 20. Nighttime temperatures in the unit have been in the low 50s. I reported it by phone on May 21 and again on May 27."

## The tools behind the letter

California gives tenants real options when a landlord stalls on a genuine repair, including, in some cases, the right to fix the problem and subtract the cost from rent, up to one month's worth (Civil Code § 1942).

That path comes with rules, limits, and ways to get it wrong, and a misstep can put your tenancy at risk. So this is the moment to get advice before you act, not after. The letter goes first. The escalation comes later, with a clear head and good counsel.

## What to do today

Write it down. The condition, the dates, the times you reported it, and a reasonable deadline to fix it. Keep it factual and short. Send it a way you can prove — email with a read confirmation, or mail you can track.
