Yes — you can often stop a competitor from using your slogan in California if the slogan is distinctive enough to function as a trademark and the copying is likely to confuse customers. Slogans are protectable under the federal Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a), and California's Unfair Competition Law, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200. The key question is whether your tagline identifies the source of your goods rather than just describing them.

**Short answer:** A distinctive, source-identifying slogan can be protected and enforced with a cease and desist letter. Generic or purely descriptive phrases ("Best Pizza in Town") usually can't be monopolized. The stronger and more unique your slogan, the easier it is to stop a copycat.

## When is a slogan actually protectable?

Courts protect slogans that are "inherently distinctive" or that have acquired "secondary meaning" — meaning customers associate the phrase specifically with you. A short, memorable, non-descriptive tagline you've used consistently in advertising has the best chance. Common laudatory phrases and ordinary descriptions are weak. Registering the slogan with the USPTO strengthens your position considerably and creates a public record of your rights.

## What do you need to prove against the competitor?

You generally must show that you used the slogan first, that it's distinctive, and that the competitor's use creates a likelihood of confusion among consumers. Side-by-side advertising, overlapping customers, and a near-identical phrase all help. If the competitor adopted your exact tagline in the same market, confusion is easier to demonstrate.

## What's the fastest way to make them stop?

Start with a cease and desist letter documenting your prior use and demanding they drop the slogan by a set date. Most disputes end there. If they refuse, you can seek an injunction and damages. For how enforcement letters work, see [What Is a Trademark Cease and Desist Letter and When Does It Work?](/blog/2026-05-13-what-is-a-trademark-cease-and-desist-letter) and [California Intellectual Property Laws for Small Businesses](/blog/2026-05-27-california-intellectual-property-laws-small-businesses).

## How do you build evidence that your slogan deserves protection?

Because slogan disputes turn on distinctiveness and prior use, the strength of your file matters as much as the law. Gather proof that you used the tagline first and used it consistently: dated advertising, social posts, packaging, press mentions, and sales tied to campaigns that featured the slogan. If customers and reviewers repeat the phrase back to you, that's powerful evidence of "secondary meaning" — the legal shorthand for a phrase the public associates specifically with your brand.

The more you can show that the slogan functions as a source identifier rather than a generic sales pitch, the easier it is to stop a copycat. Registering the slogan with the USPTO crystallizes this by creating a public record and a presumption of ownership, which makes a cease and desist letter land harder.

## When is it not worth fighting over a slogan?

Be realistic about weak taglines. If your phrase is a common, laudatory, or purely descriptive expression that many businesses use, courts are unlikely to hand you a monopoly on it, and chasing it can waste money and goodwill. In those cases, the smarter play may be to strengthen and register a more distinctive tagline going forward rather than litigate over a generic one. A quick legal read can tell you which side of that line your slogan falls on before you invest in enforcement.
