A late fee on a California invoice is generally enforceable when two things are true: your client agreed to it in advance, and the amount is a reasonable estimate of what late payment actually costs you. A fee that functions as a punishment rather than compensation can be unenforceable under California law, even if the client technically signed off.

**Short answer:** Late fees are enforceable in California if they were agreed in writing before the work and stay reasonable. Punitive or surprise fees can be thrown out under Civil Code § 1671.

## When is a late fee actually enforceable?

The fee has to be part of the deal before the work happens. A penalty you invent after the client is already late, or one buried in fine print they never saw, is far weaker. Put the late-fee term in your written agreement or on the invoice terms the client accepted: the trigger (e.g., "net 30"), the rate (e.g., 1.5% per month), and when it starts.

California treats contract penalties skeptically. Under Civil Code § 1671, a liquidated-damages or fee clause is valid only if it's a reasonable attempt to estimate the harm caused by late payment. A 1–1.5% monthly finance charge is widely used and generally defensible. A fee that balloons to 50% of the invoice looks like a penalty, and a court can refuse to enforce it.

## How much can you charge?

There's no single statutory cap for ordinary business-to-business invoices, but "reasonable" is the ceiling. Most freelancers and agencies use 1% to 1.5% per month (roughly 12%–18% annualized). Stay in that range, tie it to the unpaid balance, and stop it from compounding into something punitive.

If your contract is silent on a rate but the debt is clearly owed, you may still be entitled to prejudgment interest at 10% per year under Civil Code § 3289 once the amount is fixed. That's separate from a contractual late fee and is a reason not to panic if you forgot to write one in.

## Does a late fee help you get paid?

Sometimes the fee itself motivates payment. More often, the leverage is the signal that you're treating this as a real debt. When informal reminders stop working, an attorney-signed [demand letter for unpaid invoices](/blog/2026-06-04-statute-of-limitations-unpaid-invoices-california-2026) that itemizes the principal, the agreed late fee, and your intent to pursue collection tends to move a client who's been ignoring you.

## What if the client says they never agreed to it?

This is why documentation matters. Save the signed contract, the accepted proposal, or the invoice terms the client approved. If the only evidence of the late fee is an invoice you sent after the fact, expect a fight over it — though the underlying principal is usually still collectible. Drop or reduce a shaky late fee rather than let it sink an otherwise clean claim.

## Bottom line

Agree the fee in writing, keep it reasonable (1–1.5% per month is the safe zone), and document that the client accepted it. Do that, and the fee usually sticks. Skip those steps, and you may still collect the principal plus statutory interest — just not the penalty.
