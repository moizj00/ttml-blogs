**Short answer:** Yes. Every licensed contractor in California must carry a $25,000 surety bond under Bus. & Prof. Code § 7071.6. If a contractor caused you financial harm — abandoned the project, did defective work, or took your deposit and vanished — you can file a claim directly with the bonding company. The bond exists specifically to pay people like you.

## How the contractor's bond works (and why most people don't know about it)

Here's the thing about the contractor's license bond that surprises almost everyone: it's not insurance for the contractor. It's insurance for you.

When a contractor gets licensed, they must purchase a surety bond from a bonding company (the "surety"). The bond is a three-party agreement: the contractor promises to follow the law, the surety promises to pay valid claims up to $25,000, and you — the homeowner or customer — are the protected party.

If the contractor breaks the law or breaches the contract, you file a claim with the surety. The surety investigates. If the claim is valid, they pay you out of the bond. Then the surety goes after the contractor to get reimbursed. The contractor can't dodge this — the bond is a condition of keeping their license.

> **California Bus. & Prof. Code § 7071.6** — The contractor's license bond must be $25,000 for all active licenses. This bond is separate from any workers' compensation insurance or general liability policy. Claims can be filed by any person damaged by the contractor's violation of the Contractors License Law.

## How to file a bond claim step by step

**Step 1: Find the bond information.** Go to the CSLB website (cslb.ca.gov), look up the contractor's license number, and note the bonding company name and bond number. This information is public.

**Step 2: Write a formal claim letter** to the bonding company. Include the contractor's license number, the bond number, a description of what went wrong, the dollar amount you're claiming, and copies of your contract, receipts, and photos.

**Step 3: The surety investigates.** Most bonding companies respond within 30-60 days. They'll contact the contractor for their side, review the evidence, and make a determination.

**Step 4: Get paid or escalate.** If the surety accepts the claim, they issue payment up to $25,000. If they deny it, you can sue both the contractor and the surety — and now the denied bond claim becomes evidence in your case.

## The $25,000 cap — and what to do when your losses exceed it

The bond caps at $25,000. If your damages are $40,000, the bond claim gets you $25,000 and you pursue the remaining $15,000 through other channels. That's where a demand letter earns its keep — it puts the contractor on notice for the full amount, and the bond claim running in parallel shows you're serious.

One catch: multiple claimants can file against the same bond. If three homeowners each have valid $15,000 claims, the $25,000 bond gets split proportionally. First in line doesn't take all — the surety allocates fairly across valid claims.

## When to file a bond claim vs. send a demand letter

The bond claim is strongest when you have clear documentation (contract, payments, photos of defects) and your damages are under $25,000. It's essentially free money recovery — no court filing fees, no lawyer required.

But the bond claim alone doesn't create urgency. The surety takes its time. Pair the bond claim with a demand letter that cites Bus. & Prof. Code § 7071.6 and mentions the pending claim. Most contractors get very attentive when they learn their bond is being hit — a paid claim makes their bond premium skyrocket, and enough claims can make them unbondable.
