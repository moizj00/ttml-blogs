If you have ever clicked "buy" expecting one price and then watched mandatory "service," "convenience," or "processing" fees pile on at checkout, California's junk-fee law was written for you. Senate Bill 478, which amended the Consumers Legal Remedies Act, requires that the advertised price be the full price a consumer must pay — no surprise mandatory add-ons revealed only at the end.

**Short answer:** California's SB 478 (part of the Consumers Legal Remedies Act, Civil Code § 1750 and following) bans hidden mandatory fees. Businesses must show the all-in price up front, and consumers can challenge violations as unlawful and deceptive practices.

This guide explains what SB 478 actually requires, which charges are and aren't covered, and what you can do when a business ignores it in 2026.

## What does SB 478 actually require?

At its core, SB 478 establishes "all-in" or "honest" pricing. It makes it unlawful to advertise, display, or offer a price for a good or service that does not include all mandatory fees or charges. The only amounts a business may add after the advertised price are government-imposed taxes and fees and, in some contexts, reasonable shipping costs.

In plain terms: if a fee is mandatory — if every customer must pay it to complete the purchase — it has to be baked into the price you see at the start, not sprung on you at checkout. The law targets "drip pricing," the tactic of advertising a low headline number and then dripping in unavoidable charges step by step until the real total is much higher.

## Which fees are covered by the ban?

The ban reaches mandatory charges that businesses historically tacked on late: service fees, convenience fees, processing fees, "resort" or "destination" fees, mandatory handling charges, and similar add-ons that a consumer cannot avoid. If the customer has no realistic choice but to pay it, the charge must be in the displayed price.

What remains separable is narrow: taxes and fees imposed by the government, and certain postage or carriage costs for physical goods. Everything else that's mandatory belongs in the up-front number.

## Are restaurants and food delivery covered?

This is where the rules got nuanced. After SB 478 took effect, the Legislature clarified the treatment of the restaurant and food-service sector through follow-up legislation, allowing certain restaurants to display a service charge separately under specific disclosure conditions rather than folding it into menu prices. The practical upshot for diners: a clearly disclosed mandatory service charge in a restaurant context is treated differently from a hidden online checkout fee. Delivery platforms and ticketing still face the core all-in disclosure expectation. Because this area has seen targeted amendments, check the current rule for the specific industry you're dealing with.

## How is SB 478 enforced?

Because SB 478 operates through the Consumers Legal Remedies Act, a violation can be treated as an unlawful and deceptive business practice. That opens several enforcement doors: the California Attorney General and district attorneys can pursue businesses, and consumers themselves can assert CLRA claims. The CLRA allows for actual damages, injunctive relief, and in appropriate cases attorney's fees, and it requires a pre-suit notice for damages claims that itself often prompts a business to correct course.

The Unfair Competition Law (Business & Professions Code § 17200) provides an additional avenue, as undisclosed mandatory fees can be both "unlawful" and "unfair" within that statute.

## What can I do if a business charged me a hidden fee?

Start by documenting the transaction: screenshot the advertised price and the final charged total, and keep your receipt. Then raise it with the business in writing, citing SB 478 and the all-in pricing requirement. Many businesses, especially those operating statewide, will refund a disputed junk fee rather than risk a CLRA exposure.

If the business refuses, you have options. For the individual charge, a credit-card dispute or a small claims action (limit $12,500 for individuals under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220) can recover the money. For a pattern affecting many consumers, the CLRA's notice-and-cure process and broader remedies come into play. ## How does this connect to broader consumer protection?

SB 478 is part of a wider tightening of California consumer law, sitting alongside the CLRA's existing bans on deceptive practices and rules against false advertising under Business & Professions Code § 17500. A hidden-fee problem frequently overlaps with a false-advertising problem — the headline price was, in effect, a misrepresentation. Our guide to [false advertising and bait-and-switch in California](/blog/2026-06-05-false-advertising-bait-and-switch-california-2026) covers that overlap in detail.

## The bottom line

SB 478 reflects a straightforward principle: the price you're shown should be the price you pay. Mandatory fees belong in the advertised number, with only government taxes and certain shipping costs added afterward. If a business hides unavoidable charges until checkout, document it, raise it in writing with a reference to the law, and escalate through the CLRA, a chargeback, or small claims if needed. In 2026, the legal presumption favors the consumer who expected honest pricing.
