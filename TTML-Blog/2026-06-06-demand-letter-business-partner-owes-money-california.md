A demand letter to a business partner works the same way as any other demand letter — state the claim, the amount, the deadline, the consequence — but with one powerful addition: partners owe each other fiduciary duties under California Corporations Code § 16404, including duties of loyalty and care. A partner who diverts partnership money, withholds your share of profits, or refuses to honor a buyout isn't just breaching a contract; they may be breaching a fiduciary duty, which carries heavier consequences in court. A well-drafted demand letter puts that leverage on the table before anyone files anything.

**Short answer:** Identify the exact obligation (loan, distribution, buyout, reimbursement), calculate the amount with documentation, invoke the partnership agreement and fiduciary duties where they apply, set a 10–14 day deadline, and state the specific next step — mediation, arbitration if your agreement requires it, or a lawsuit. Most partner money disputes settle at or shortly after the letter stage because both sides have a business reputation to protect.

## What Kinds of Partner Debts Does a Demand Letter Cover?

The common scenarios fall into four groups. Unpaid loans: you lent money to the partnership or to the partner personally, and repayment is overdue. Withheld distributions: the business is profitable and distributing to one partner but not you, or your agreed profit share isn't being paid. Stalled buyouts: you've left or been pushed out, the agreement or the Corporations Code entitles you to be bought out, and the remaining partner keeps delaying. Reimbursements and capital: you fronted expenses or capital contributions that were supposed to be repaid or equalized.

Each has a different legal basis, and the letter should name it precisely. A loan claim rests on the loan terms and CCP § 337's four-year deadline for written agreements. A distribution claim rests on the partnership agreement and § 16404. A buyout claim for a dissociated partner rests on Corporations Code § 16701, which entitles a dissociated partner to be bought out at a statutorily defined price. Vague letters that mix everything into "you owe me money" invite a vague denial.

## Why Do Fiduciary Duties Change the Picture?

Under Corporations Code § 16404, partners owe the partnership and each other a duty of loyalty — including accounting for any benefit derived from partnership business and refraining from self-dealing — and a duty of care. An ordinary debtor who stalls owes you the debt plus interest. A partner who pays himself while withholding your distributions may face a breach of fiduciary duty claim, an accounting action, and in egregious self-dealing cases, punitive damages — remedies a normal contract debtor never faces.

Your demand letter should say this calmly and specifically: "Your continued distribution of partnership profits to yourself while withholding my share constitutes a breach of the duty of loyalty under Corporations Code § 16404." Partners with their own counsel will immediately understand the escalation that sentence represents.

## What Should the Letter Include, Step by Step?

First, the relationship and the obligation: cite the partnership agreement section, the loan document, or the oral agreement and conduct establishing the debt. Second, the numbers: principal, dates, payments made, interest if your agreement provides for it, and a current total. Attach or reference the ledger entries, bank records, K-1s, or messages that prove it. Third, the legal basis: breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, or the § 16701 buyout right. Fourth, the demand: a specific dollar figure and deadline, or a concrete buyout process — valuation method, appraiser selection, payment schedule. Fifth, the consequence: check your partnership agreement first, because many require mediation or arbitration before suit, and your letter should track that sequence to show you're complying with it.

Keep the tone professional and forward-looking. In partner disputes the recipient often remains your co-owner during the fight — scorched-earth letters make the eventual resolution (usually a buyout or dissolution) more expensive for both of you.

## What If There's No Written Partnership Agreement?

California's Revised Uniform Partnership Act fills the gap. If two or more people carried on a business as co-owners for profit, a partnership exists whether or not anything was signed (Corporations Code § 16202), and the default rules apply: equal profit shares, equal management rights, fiduciary duties, and buyout rights on dissociation. Your demand letter can rest on the statute itself plus the conduct evidence — bank accounts, tax returns, how profits were historically split. A handshake partnership is harder to prove but not weaker once proven. The same evidence-gathering discipline that applies to [collecting without a written contract](/blog/2026-06-04-no-written-contract-can-i-still-collect-california-freelancer) applies here, with tax documents doing the heaviest lifting.

## What Are Your Options If the Letter Is Ignored?

Four escalation paths, roughly in order of cost. Mediation: a half-day session with a neutral resolves a surprising share of partner money disputes, and many agreements require it first. Arbitration: if your agreement has an arbitration clause, this replaces court — faster and private, though you pay the arbitrator. An accounting action: a court-ordered formal accounting of partnership finances, the classic remedy when you suspect the books are being manipulated. And a lawsuit for breach of contract and fiduciary duty, with dissolution under Corporations Code § 16801 as the nuclear option — a court can wind up the partnership entirely when it's no longer reasonably practicable to carry on.

The buyout deserves special mention. If you've dissociated, § 16701 requires the partnership to purchase your interest at the greater of liquidation value or going-concern value, with interest from the date of dissociation. Partners who stall buyouts are accruing interest against themselves — a fact worth stating in the letter.

## How Long Do You Have to Act?

Breach of a written partnership agreement: four years (CCP § 337). Oral partnership terms: two years (CCP § 339). Breach of fiduciary duty: generally four years (CCP § 343), but only three years if the claim is grounded in fraud (CCP § 338(d)), with discovery rules affecting when the clock starts. Buyout obligations accrue from dissociation. Because partner disputes often involve years of slow-building grievances, get a clear timeline analysis early — the oldest withheld distributions may already be time-barred while recent ones remain collectible.

## Does Sending the Letter Risk Blowing Up the Business?

It usually clarifies rather than destroys. By the time you're drafting a demand letter, the partnership has a problem the letter didn't create. A professional, well-supported demand frequently triggers the conversation that should have happened months earlier — and gives your partner's attorney something concrete to advise settlement on. The alternative, simmering silently while distributions stay unequal, only burns your limitations period and your leverage.
