**Short answer:** In California, a contractor generally cannot charge you for extra work without a written, signed change order. Under Bus. & Prof. Code § 7159, home improvement contracts must specify the scope and price — and any changes to that scope require your written approval before the work begins. If a contractor did unauthorized extra work and is billing you for it, you have strong legal ground to refuse payment.

## Why change orders cause more construction fights than anything else

The original contract says $45,000 for a kitchen remodel. Six weeks in, the contractor hands you a bill for $62,000. The extra $17,000? "Change orders" — upgraded materials, unexpected plumbing, a structural issue behind the wall. Some of those changes are legitimate. Some are padding. And the distinction comes down to one question: did you agree in writing before the work happened?

That question sounds simple. In practice, it's the fault line in most contractor disputes — because contractors routinely skip the paperwork, homeowners verbally approve things on the fly, and by the time the final bill arrives, nobody agrees on what was authorized.

California law anticipated this problem. The rules are specific, and they favor the homeowner when the contractor cuts corners on documentation.

## What California law requires for a valid change order

> **Bus. & Prof. Code § 7159(d)(8)** — A home improvement contract must include a provision that "extra or change-order work" will not be performed without the homeowner's prior written authorization. The change order must describe the scope of the additional work, the cost, and any effect on the project timeline.

Three requirements must be met for a contractor to legally charge you for work beyond the original contract:

The change must be in writing. A text message or email can qualify, but a verbal "go ahead" in the middle of demolition day is not a written authorization under § 7159.

The homeowner must sign before the work begins. Post-hoc change orders — "sign this to confirm the work we already did" — violate the statute's requirement for prior authorization. A contractor who does extra work before getting your signature took that financial risk themselves.

The price must be stated. "We'll figure out the cost later" is not a valid change order. The homeowner is entitled to know what the additional work will cost before agreeing to it.

## When you must pay for changes — even without a formal change order

There is one exception that catches homeowners off guard: emergency work necessary to protect life or property. If a contractor opens a wall and discovers active knob-and-tube wiring that's a fire hazard, they can take immediate corrective action without waiting for a signed change order. This is a narrow exception — it covers safety, not aesthetics.

California courts have also recognized an implied change order when the homeowner actively directed specific changes, supervised the extra work, and made no objection during performance. But the burden of proving an implied change order falls on the contractor, and courts are skeptical.

## The five most common change-order disputes — and who wins

| Dispute | Who Typically Wins | Why |
|---------|-------------------|-----|
| Contractor did extra work without written approval | Homeowner | § 7159 requires written authorization before work begins |
| Homeowner verbally approved, contractor has witnesses | Usually homeowner | Statute requires written consent; verbal agreements are hard to enforce |
| Legitimate hidden condition (mold, rot, code violation) | Depends | If safety-related, contractor may recover; if cosmetic, homeowner can refuse |
| Contractor upgraded materials without asking | Homeowner | No authorization = homeowner only owes for the originally specified materials |
| Change order signed but price left blank | Homeowner | The written change order must include the cost per § 7159(d)(8) |

## How to dispute an unauthorized change order

The playbook depends on where you are in the project.

**If the project is still in progress:** Put your objection in writing immediately. Send the contractor a letter (email works) stating: "I did not authorize this change order. I am not agreeing to pay for work I did not approve. Please proceed only with the work specified in our original contract dated [date]." This creates a contemporaneous record. If the contractor walks off the job over it, that's abandonment — a separate Bus. & Prof. Code violation.

**If the project is finished and you've received the inflated bill:** Do not pay the disputed portion. Pay what you owe under the original contract plus any properly documented, signed change orders. For the rest, send a demand letter disputing the unauthorized charges and citing § 7159. The letter should specify:

The original contract price, the amount you've already paid, the amount of unauthorized changes you're disputing, and the specific statutory violation.

> **Bus. & Prof. Code § 7113** — A contractor who departs from plans or specifications without the homeowner's written consent commits an act subject to license discipline. This isn't just a breach of contract — it's a licensing violation that can be reported to the CSLB.

**Timeline for taking action:**

**Day 0:** Receive the disputed bill. Compare line-by-line to the original contract and any signed change orders.

**Day 1-3:** Draft a written objection or demand letter. Cite § 7159(d)(8) and § 7113.

**Day 14:** If no response, file a CSLB complaint for the contract violation and consider a claim against the contractor's $25,000 surety bond.

**Day 30:** If the contractor still refuses to adjust the bill, file in small claims court (up to $12,500) or superior court for larger amounts.

## What contractors should know about protecting themselves

This isn't a one-sided issue. Legitimate contractors get burned by change-order disputes too — they do real extra work at the homeowner's direction and then the homeowner refuses to pay.

The fix is documentation discipline. Get every change in writing, with the homeowner's signature, before starting the work. A simple one-page change order form with the date, description of additional work, cost, timeline impact, and both signatures is enough. Keep a copy on-site and email a copy to the homeowner the same day.

For contractors who need to collect on legitimate change orders that a homeowner is disputing, a demand letter citing the signed documentation is the first step. If the documentation is solid, the demand letter often resolves it — the homeowner's attorney will tell them a signed change order is enforceable.

## How change orders interact with mechanics liens

A contractor who does authorized extra work can include that amount in a mechanics lien under Civ. Code § 8400. But a contractor who did unauthorized work and tries to lien the property for it faces a serious problem: the lien claim includes amounts the homeowner never agreed to pay, which makes the lien vulnerable to a petition to release under Civ. Code § 8480.

If a contractor files a mechanics lien that includes unauthorized change-order amounts, the homeowner can petition the court to release the lien. The homeowner can also recover attorney's fees if the court finds the lien was filed in bad faith.
