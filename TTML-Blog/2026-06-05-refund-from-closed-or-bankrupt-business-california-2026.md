Few consumer situations feel as hopeless as a business closing its doors while still holding your money — a prepaid gym membership, an undelivered furniture order, a deposit for a service that never happened. The good news: a closure or bankruptcy does not automatically erase your right to a refund. Several recovery routes survive a shutdown, and acting quickly matters more than anything.

**Short answer:** Even when a California business closes or files bankruptcy, you may recover through a credit-card chargeback, a surety bond or trust account, a bankruptcy claim, or the owners personally if the business broke the law. Speed is critical.

This 2026 guide walks through each path and how to prioritize them.

## Can I still get my money back if the business closed?

Yes, often. A business closing is not the same as your money disappearing. The key is finding which "pocket" can still pay: the payment network you used, a bond or insurance the business was required to carry, money held in trust, the bankruptcy estate, or in some cases the individuals behind the business. The right strategy depends on how you paid and what kind of business it was.

The single most important factor is time. Chargeback windows are short, bankruptcy claim deadlines are firm, and bonds can be exhausted by the many other customers racing for the same funds. Move now, not later.

## Is a credit-card chargeback my best first step?

Usually, yes — if you paid by credit card. Under the federal Fair Credit Billing Act (and the card networks' own rules), you can dispute a charge for goods or services you never received. This is frequently the fastest way to recover from a vanished business, because the money comes back from the payment system rather than the defunct company. Contact your card issuer immediately, explain that the business closed without delivering, and provide your receipt and any communications.

Debit-card and bank-transfer protections are weaker and time-sensitive, so if you paid that way, escalate the other routes below in parallel. Gift cards and cash offer the least protection, which is why prepaying large sums to a shaky business is risky.

## Does the business have a bond or trust account I can claim against?

Many California businesses are legally required to carry a surety bond or hold customer prepayments in trust, and those funds survive a closure. Licensed contractors must carry a bond on file with the Contractors State License Board, which wronged consumers can claim against. Health-studio and gym contracts are regulated and often require bonding or trust protection for prepaid memberships. Sellers of certain prepaid services, travel, and discount programs face similar requirements.

If your business type was licensed or bonded, find the licensing agency, confirm the bond, and file a claim promptly — bonds are finite and shared among all claimants. This route can pay even when the company itself has no assets left.

## What happens to my refund if the business files bankruptcy?

When a business files bankruptcy, an "automatic stay" generally halts collection efforts, and your claim becomes part of a structured process. You become a creditor and should file a proof of claim by the deadline stated in the bankruptcy notice. Consumers who prepaid for undelivered goods or services may hold a claim that gets limited priority treatment, but most consumer creditors are unsecured and recover only a fraction — or nothing — depending on the estate.

Bankruptcy makes the chargeback and bond routes even more important, because they can pay you outside the bankruptcy estate. If you only have a bankruptcy claim, file it correctly and on time, and watch for any consumer-deposit priority that may apply.

## Can I pursue the owners personally?

Sometimes. A corporation or LLC normally shields its owners, but that shield can fall away. If owners took deposits while knowing the business was about to fold, commingled trust funds, committed fraud, or operated without required licensing or bonding, you may have grounds to reach them personally through theories like fraud or "piercing the corporate veil." These claims are fact-intensive, but they matter most exactly when the business entity is empty — the owners may be the only solvent target.

## How should I sequence my recovery efforts?

Move on several fronts at once. First, file the credit-card chargeback immediately if you paid by card. Second, identify and claim any bond or trust protection tied to the business type. Third, if there's a bankruptcy, file your proof of claim before the deadline. Fourth, preserve evidence — contracts, receipts, ads, and communications — in case you pursue the owners or file in small claims court (limit $12,500 for individuals under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220) against any solvent party. For help choosing complaint channels along the way, see [where to file a consumer complaint in California](/blog/2026-06-05-where-to-file-consumer-complaint-california-2026).

## Does a demand letter help against a closing business?

It can — if sent early and to the right target. A demand letter to owners who are still reachable, citing potential personal liability for taking money they couldn't honor, sometimes produces a refund before formal bankruptcy or dissolution closes the door. Once the entity is fully wound down, the letter's value shifts to supporting a bond or veil-piercing claim. Either way, a clear written record of your demand strengthens every other route.

## The bottom line

A closed or bankrupt business is a setback, not a dead end. Recovery usually comes from a different pocket than the company itself: the card network through a chargeback, a required bond or trust account, the bankruptcy estate, or the owners when they crossed a legal line. The consumers who recover are the ones who act fast, paper their claim, and pursue every available pocket at once.
