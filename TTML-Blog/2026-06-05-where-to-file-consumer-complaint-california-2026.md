When a California business overcharges you, refuses a refund, or sells you something defective, the hard part is often knowing where to turn. There are several complaint channels — state regulators, the Attorney General, the Better Business Bureau, federal agencies, and the courts — and they do very different things. Picking the right one (or the right combination) determines whether you actually get your money back.

**Short answer:** California consumers can complain to the Department of Consumer Affairs, the Attorney General, industry regulators, the CFPB, or the BBB, and can sue in small claims court (limit $12,500). Regulators pressure businesses; small claims and demand letters recover money.

This guide maps each option, what it can and can't do, and when a demand letter is the smarter first move in 2026.

## Should I file with the California Department of Consumer Affairs (DCA)?

The Department of Consumer Affairs oversees dozens of licensing boards and bureaus — contractors (CSLB), auto repair (Bureau of Automotive Repair), and many licensed professions. If your dispute is with a licensed business, the relevant board is one of the most effective places to complain, because it controls something the business cares about: its license. A board can investigate, mediate, discipline, and in some programs help recover funds.

The limitation: regulators enforce licensing rules and public protection, not your personal contract. A board complaint can pressure a business and create a record, but it does not directly hand you a check. Use it in parallel with a money-recovery step, not instead of one.

## When should I go to the California Attorney General?

The California Attorney General's office (oag.ca.gov) takes consumer complaints and is the chief enforcer of the state's consumer-protection statutes — the Unfair Competition Law (Business & Professions Code § 17200), the False Advertising Law (§ 17500), and the Consumers Legal Remedies Act (Civil Code § 1750 and following). The AG generally pursues patterns and practices that harm many consumers rather than litigating individual disputes, but your complaint feeds enforcement priorities and is worth filing when you suspect widespread wrongdoing.

## What about the Better Business Bureau (BBB)?

The BBB is a private nonprofit, not a government agency, and it has no power to fine, license, or order a refund. What it does offer is a public complaint record and an informal mediation channel. Because businesses watch their BBB profile and ratings, a BBB complaint sometimes prompts a quick resolution for reputational reasons. Treat it as a low-cost pressure tactic, not an enforcement mechanism — and never as a substitute for preserving your legal deadlines.

## Which federal agencies handle consumer complaints?

Some disputes have a federal home. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) handles banks, lenders, credit reporting, and debt collectors. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) tracks fraud and deceptive practices. For a debt-collection problem, the CFPB and California's regulators both apply — see our guide to [debt collector harassment rights in California](/blog/2026-06-05-debt-collector-harassment-rights-california). Federal complaints, like state regulatory ones, build pressure and records but rarely produce an individual refund directly.

## When is small claims court the right move?

Small claims court is California's most direct path to actually recovering money. It is designed for people without lawyers, the filing fees are modest, and the individual limit is $12,500 under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220 (lower limits apply to certain entities). If a business owes you a defined sum and won't pay, small claims gives you a judgment you can enforce. The trade-off is time and effort: you file, serve the business, and present your case at a hearing.

## So when should I send a demand letter instead?

A demand letter often outperforms every other first step. It is faster than a regulator, more pointed than a BBB post, and cheaper than filing suit. A well-drafted letter states the facts, cites the specific California statute the business violated, demands a concrete remedy by a deadline, and signals you're prepared to escalate. Because it shows the business you understand your rights, it frequently produces a refund before any complaint or lawsuit is necessary.

The smartest strategy is usually sequential: send a statute-citing demand letter first; if it's ignored, file the regulatory complaint and small claims action you already warned about. Each step you took strengthens the next.

## How do I choose the right path for my situation?

Match the channel to your goal. If you want your money back, prioritize a demand letter and small claims. If you want a business disciplined or stopped, prioritize the relevant DCA board or the Attorney General. If the business is a bank, lender, or collector, add the CFPB. For most everyday disputes — a refused refund, an overcharge, a defective product — the combination of a firm demand letter and the credible threat of small claims does the heavy lifting. ## The bottom line

California gives consumers a deep bench of complaint options, but they aren't interchangeable. Regulators and the BBB apply pressure; small claims and demand letters recover money. Start with a clear, statute-backed demand, preserve your deadlines, and escalate in sequence. Knowing which door to knock on — and in what order — is often the difference between a refund and a runaround.
