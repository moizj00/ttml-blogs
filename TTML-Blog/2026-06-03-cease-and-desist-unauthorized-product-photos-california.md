Yes — if someone is using your product photos without permission, you can absolutely send a cease and desist letter. Under the Copyright Act (17 U.S.C. § 501), the person who created (or paid for) original product photography owns the copyright automatically. You don't need to register the copyright to own it, but registration with the U.S. Copyright Office unlocks statutory damages of up to $150,000 per work and attorney's fees if you need to sue.

## Do I Automatically Own the Copyright to My Product Photos?

If you took the photos yourself, yes — copyright attaches the moment you press the shutter. If you hired a photographer, it depends on the contract. Under the Copyright Act's "work made for hire" doctrine (17 U.S.C. § 101), photos taken by an employee within the scope of employment belong to the employer. But if you hired an independent contractor, the photographer owns the copyright unless there's a written assignment.

This is a critical detail for ecommerce sellers. Before sending a cease and desist, confirm you actually hold the rights.

## What Should the Cease and Desist Letter Include?

An effective cease and desist for photo theft should include:

- **Identification of the copyrighted works** — include the original image URLs or timestamps proving you created them first.
- **Evidence of infringement** — screenshots showing the unauthorized use, with dates.
- **A specific demand** — remove the images within a stated deadline (typically 10–15 business days).
- **A warning of legal consequences** — reference 17 U.S.C. § 504 (damages) and the possibility of a DMCA takedown if they don't comply.

An attorney-signed letter carries far more weight than a self-drafted email. Most infringers remove the images within days of receiving one.

## Should I File a DMCA Takedown Instead?

A [DMCA takedown notice](/blog/2026-05-13-attorney-letter-vs-dmca-takedown-counterfeit-listings) is another option — and you can do both. A DMCA notice goes directly to the platform (Amazon, Shopify, Etsy) and can get the listing removed within 24–72 hours. But it only removes the specific listing; it doesn't stop the infringer from re-uploading or using your photos elsewhere.

A cease and desist addresses the person directly and puts them on notice that continued use will lead to a lawsuit. The strongest approach is both: file the DMCA takedown for immediate removal, and send the cease and desist to prevent future infringement.

## How Much Does This Cost?

A flat-fee cease and desist letter for copyright infringement typically costs $199–$500. Copyright registration costs $55–$85 through the Copyright Office's online system. If you need to escalate to federal court, litigation costs start around $10,000 — but that's rarely necessary when the letter is backed by a registered copyright.

The ROI math is simple: professional product photography costs $500–$5,000 per shoot. Protecting that investment with a $199 letter is almost always worth it.
