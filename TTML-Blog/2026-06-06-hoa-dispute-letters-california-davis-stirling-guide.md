Disputes with a California homeowners association — unfair fines, ignored repair requests, selective enforcement, denied records — are governed by the Davis-Stirling Common Interest Development Act (Civil Code § 4000 et seq.), and the Act is built around written process. Homeowners who put demands in the right written form, citing the right sections, get traction that phone calls and board-meeting venting never achieve. Better still, the Act requires the HOA to participate in internal dispute resolution when an owner requests it (Civil Code § 5910), making a well-aimed letter one of the few demands an HOA cannot lawfully ignore.

**Short answer:** Match the letter to the dispute. Challenge fines using the hearing and notice rights in Civil Code § 5855. Demand records under § 5200 et seq. Force a meet-and-confer through Internal Dispute Resolution under § 5910 — the HOA may not refuse. And before most enforcement lawsuits, both sides must be offered Alternative Dispute Resolution under §§ 5930–5935. Each step creates a record that wins later if the dispute escalates.

## What Rules Actually Bind Your HOA?

A hierarchy: the Davis-Stirling Act and other California law on top, then your development's CC&Rs (covenants, conditions, and restrictions), then bylaws, then board-adopted operating rules. A board rule that conflicts with the CC&Rs or the Act is unenforceable. This matters because boards frequently act beyond their authority — imposing fine schedules never properly adopted, enforcing "rules" that appear nowhere in the governing documents, or skipping required procedures. The first step in any HOA dispute is pulling the actual documents and finding where (or whether) the board's claimed authority lives. Under Civil Code § 5975, CC&Rs are enforceable as equitable servitudes — by the HOA against you, but equally by you against the HOA and other owners.

## How Do You Challenge an HOA Fine?

Start with procedure, because boards trip over it constantly. Before disciplining a member, Civil Code § 5855 requires the board to give at least 10 days' written notice of a hearing, stating the date, time, and nature of the alleged violation, and the member must be given the opportunity to be heard. After the hearing, the board must notify you of its decision in writing within 15 days. Fines must also follow a fine schedule that was adopted and distributed to members (Civil Code § 5850). A fine imposed without proper notice, without a hearing opportunity, or outside the adopted schedule is procedurally defective — and your challenge letter should say exactly which step was skipped.

Your letter should: identify the fine and date; state each procedural defect with the code section; address the merits (photos, dates, witness statements showing no violation or correction); raise selective enforcement if neighbors with identical conditions weren't cited; and demand the fine be rescinded, with IDR under § 5910 requested if it isn't. One critical warning for owners: assessments and fines are different animals. Don't withhold regular assessments to protest a fine — unpaid assessments can lead to liens and foreclosure, while disputing a fine in writing protects you without that exposure.

## Can You Force the HOA to Make Repairs?

Often, yes. Unless the CC&Rs say otherwise, Civil Code § 4775 makes the association responsible for repairing and maintaining the common area, while owners maintain their separate interests (with exclusive-use common areas typically split: owner maintains, association repairs). Leaking roofs over condos, broken gates, dying common landscaping, failing plumbing in shared walls — these are usually association obligations. A repair demand letter should describe the condition with photos and dates, identify it as common area under § 4775 and your CC&Rs, document every prior report, and warn of consequences: damage to your unit from the unrepaired condition becomes the association's liability, and continued refusal will trigger IDR and ADR demands.

Boards respond to repair letters that mention consequence because deferred maintenance is a board's most litigated failure. If interior damage has already occurred — a stained ceiling from a common-area roof leak — your letter doubles as a damages demand, and the association's insurance may come into play.

## What Is Internal Dispute Resolution and Why Is It Powerful?

IDR (Civil Code §§ 5900–5920) is a statutory meet-and-confer: an informal, prompt, and free process to resolve disputes between an owner and the association involving the Act or the governing documents. Here's the asymmetry homeowners should exploit: if the association requests IDR, you may decline — but if you request IDR, the association may not refuse (§ 5910). The board must meet with you, in good faith, at a mutually convenient time. Any resolution reached, signed by both sides, is binding and judicially enforceable.

A one-page IDR demand letter — "Pursuant to Civil Code § 5910, I request internal dispute resolution regarding [dispute]" — forces a face-to-face that boards otherwise dodge for months. You may bring an attorney (notify them in advance if you do). Even when IDR doesn't settle the matter, it generates admissions and a record of the board's reasoning that becomes valuable later.

## What Is the ADR Requirement Before an HOA Lawsuit?

For most enforcement disputes seeking declaratory or injunctive relief (with damage claims under $5,000 attached), Civil Code §§ 5930–5935 prohibit either side from filing suit until the parties have attempted alternative dispute resolution — mediation or arbitration. The process starts with a Request for Resolution served on the other side, which has 30 days to accept or reject. A homeowner who skips this step can have their case dismissed; an HOA that skips it faces the same defense from you. Your attorney's serving a Request for Resolution is itself a serious escalation signal that frequently produces settlement talks — boards know that prevailing parties in Davis-Stirling enforcement actions recover attorney fees (§ 5975(c)), which makes losing expensive.

## How Do You Get the HOA's Records?

Civil Code §§ 5200–5240 give members the right to inspect association records: financials, budgets, board minutes, membership lists, vendor contracts, insurance policies, and more, on specified timelines (generally 10 business days for most records). A records demand letter should list the records by category with the code section, designate inspection or copies, and note that unjustified denial entitles a member to a civil penalty and attorney fees in an enforcement action (§ 5235). Records demands are the standard first move when you suspect financial mismanagement or want to organize neighbors — and the response often reveals how much process trouble the board is really in.

## When Should an Attorney Send the Letter Instead of You?

Three triggers: money at stake exceeds small-claims comfort (fines compounding, special assessments, damage to your unit); the board has lawyered up — once the association's counsel is writing to you, self-help letters get discounted; or you're building toward ADR and litigation, where the §§ 5930–5935 sequence and fee-shifting stakes reward precision. An attorney letter that cites the exact Davis-Stirling sections the board violated tells the association's lawyer — who advises the board on risk — that this owner will be expensive to steamroll.
