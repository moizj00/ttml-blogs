If a business lured you in with a deal that turned out to be false — an item that was never really in stock, a "sale" price that wasn't real, or a product that doesn't do what the ad promised — California law is firmly on your side. The state has three overlapping statutes that target deceptive marketing: the False Advertising Law, the Unfair Competition Law, and the Consumers Legal Remedies Act.

**Short answer:** California's False Advertising Law (Business & Professions Code § 17500), Unfair Competition Law (§ 17200), and Consumers Legal Remedies Act (Civil Code § 1750 and following) let consumers challenge false ads and bait-and-switch tactics and recover damages, restitution, and injunctive relief.

This 2026 guide explains what counts as false advertising, what bait-and-switch is, which law applies, and how to recover.

## What counts as false advertising in California?

Under Business & Professions Code § 17500, it is unlawful to make or disseminate any statement about a product or service that is untrue or misleading, and that the business knew or reasonably should have known was untrue or misleading. The standard is broad: an ad can be literally true yet still illegal if it is likely to deceive a reasonable consumer.

This sweeps in inflated "original" prices used to manufacture fake discounts, "free" offers with undisclosed mandatory costs, performance claims a product can't back up, fake scarcity ("only 2 left!") that isn't real, and misleading photos or specifications. The test isn't whether the savviest shopper would catch the trick — it's whether an ordinary consumer would likely be misled.

## What is bait-and-switch, exactly?

Bait-and-switch is a specific deceptive pattern: a business advertises an attractive product or price (the "bait") with no genuine intent to sell it on the advertised terms, then steers the customer toward a different, usually pricier, item (the "switch"). Classic signs include the advertised item being mysteriously "just sold out," staff disparaging the advertised model to push an upgrade, or refusal to honor an advertised price. California treats bait advertising as unlawful, and it can violate all three consumer statutes at once.

## How does the Unfair Competition Law (UCL) help?

The Unfair Competition Law, Business & Professions Code § 17200, prohibits any business practice that is "unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent." Its breadth is the point. A practice that violates § 17500 or the CLRA is automatically "unlawful" under the UCL. A practice that misleads the public is "fraudulent" under the UCL even without proof of intent to defraud. The UCL primarily offers restitution (getting your money back) and injunctive relief (a court order to stop the practice), which makes it a powerful clean-up tool against ongoing deceptive marketing.

## How does the Consumers Legal Remedies Act (CLRA) help?

The CLRA, Civil Code § 1750 and following, lists specific deceptive acts that are forbidden in consumer transactions — including representing that goods have characteristics or benefits they don't, advertising goods with intent not to sell them as advertised (the textbook bait-and-switch), and making false statements about price reductions. Unlike the UCL, the CLRA allows recovery of actual damages, and a prevailing consumer can recover attorney's fees. The CLRA also requires a consumer to send the business written notice at least 30 days before filing for damages, giving the business a chance to cure — a step that itself frequently produces a refund.

## What remedies can I actually get?

Depending on the statute and facts, you may obtain a refund or restitution of what you overpaid, actual damages under the CLRA, an injunction stopping the deceptive practice, and attorney's fees in CLRA cases. For willful or particularly egregious conduct, additional remedies may be available. Government enforcers — the Attorney General and district attorneys — can also seek civil penalties, which is why a credible complaint can prompt fast resolution from a business that doesn't want regulatory attention.

## How do I prove a false-advertising or bait-and-switch claim?

Evidence wins these cases. Preserve the advertisement exactly as you saw it: screenshots with timestamps, the printed circular, the email, or the listing URL. Save your receipt and any chat or email where staff steered you to a different product or refused the advertised price. Note dates, names, and what was said. The stronger your record that (a) the ad made a specific claim and (b) the business didn't honor it, the easier it is to recover.

## What's the first step if I've been deceived?

Begin with a written demand to the business that identifies the ad, the misrepresentation, and the remedy you want, and that cites the relevant statutes. For CLRA damages, this also satisfies the 30-day pre-suit notice requirement. Many disputes end here, because businesses understand the CLRA's fee-shifting and the UCL's injunction risk.

If the business won't resolve it, your venue depends on scale. For an individual overcharge, a credit-card dispute or small claims court (limit $12,500 for individuals under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220) is efficient. For larger or recurring harm, a civil action under the CLRA and UCL is the path. ## The bottom line

California's trio of consumer statutes — Section 17500, the UCL, and the CLRA — make deceptive advertising and bait-and-switch genuinely risky for businesses. As a consumer, your leverage comes from two things: documentation and a clear, statute-citing demand. Capture the ad, keep your receipts, and put the business on written notice. More often than not, that combination recovers your money without ever reaching a courtroom.
