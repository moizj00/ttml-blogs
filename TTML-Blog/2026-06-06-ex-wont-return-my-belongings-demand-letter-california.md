Yes. When an ex-partner, former roommate, or anyone else refuses to return property that belongs to you, California law calls that conversion — the wrongful exercise of control over someone else's property. A formal demand letter requesting return by a specific date is usually the fastest way to get your things back, and it creates exactly the record you need if you have to escalate to small claims court. Damages for conversion can include the property's full value plus compensation for time and money spent recovering it (Civil Code § 3336).

**Short answer:** You don't have to accept "come get it whenever" or silence. A demand letter identifying each item, proving ownership, and setting a deadline for return resolves most property-holdout disputes within weeks. If ignored, you can sue for the property's value in small claims court — your claim is generally subject to a three-year deadline (CCP § 338(c)).

## Is Keeping Someone's Property After a Breakup Actually Illegal?

Civilly, yes. Once you ask for your property back and the other person refuses or stalls indefinitely, their continued possession becomes conversion. It does not matter that they came into possession lawfully — what matters is the refusal to return it after demand. That demand is legally significant: in many conversion cases, the formal request and refusal is what starts the wrongdoing. This is why a written, dated demand letter matters more here than in almost any other dispute type.

Police rarely intervene in ex-partner property disputes, treating them as civil matters unless there's clear theft. A civil standby (an officer accompanying you to retrieve items) is sometimes available, but officers won't force entry or adjudicate ownership.

## What Should a Property Demand Letter Include?

List every item with enough specificity that there's no ambiguity — make, model, serial numbers where possible. Attach proof of ownership: receipts, photos of you with the items, registration records, or messages acknowledging the property is yours. Set a concrete deadline and a practical return method — a neutral pickup time, a shipping arrangement you'll pay for, or delivery to a third party. Then state the consequence: a small claims filing for the full replacement value plus statutory damages under Civil Code § 3336.

Offering a simple, face-saving logistics path matters. Many holdouts are about friction and spite, not genuine ownership claims — removing the friction removes the excuse.

## What Are Your Damages If They Refuse?

Civil Code § 3336 measures conversion damages as the value of the property at the time of conversion plus interest, or an amount sufficient to compensate for the loss, plus fair compensation for time and money properly spent pursuing the property. That last clause is unusual — it means documented hours and costs spent recovering your belongings can be added to a judgment. For sentimental items with low market value, courts can consider value to the owner where market value wouldn't fairly compensate.

## When Should You Go Straight to Small Claims?

If the property is being actively sold, given away, or destroyed, skip extended negotiation — file in small claims (up to $12,500 for individuals) and let the letter serve as your pre-filing demand. The same applies if the holder denies you own the items at all. Bring your proof of ownership, the demand letter, and any refusal messages. Judges in these cases routinely award replacement value when return is no longer possible.

If a landlord rather than an ex is holding your belongings after a move-out, different rules apply — see [a tenant left belongings behind after moving out in California](/blog/2026-06-01-tenant-left-belongings-behind-after-move-out-california) for that scenario from the other side.

## Does a Lawyer's Letter Make a Real Difference Here?

Usually, yes — disproportionately so. Property holdouts are emotional disputes, and an attorney letter signals the game has changed: there's now a documented conversion claim with statutory damages attached, reviewed by a professional. The holder's lawyer (or a quick web search) will tell them they cannot win, and that losing includes paying for your recovery time. Most items come back within the deadline.
