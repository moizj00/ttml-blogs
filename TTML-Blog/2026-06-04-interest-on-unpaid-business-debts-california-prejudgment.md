When a California client pays an invoice months late — or never — the principal isn't always the whole story. The law often entitles you to interest on the money you were wrongly denied, compensating you for the time the funds sat in the client's account instead of yours. This is called prejudgment interest, and for many unpaid business debts the rate is 10% per year, even if your contract never mentioned interest at all.

**Short answer:** California allows prejudgment interest on unpaid debts — typically 10% per year under Civil Code § 3289 when there's no agreed rate — once the amount owed is fixed and certain.

Understanding this does two things: it increases what you can legitimately demand, and it strengthens your leverage, because a client who keeps stalling is watching the debt grow. This guide explains where the right comes from, when it starts, how to calculate it, and how it fits into a demand.

## What is prejudgment interest?

Prejudgment interest is interest that accrues on a debt for the period *before* a court enters judgment — essentially, from the time the money was due until you're made whole. The theory is straightforward: if a client should have paid you on a certain date and didn't, they've had the use of your money, and you've lost the use of it. Interest compensates for that loss.

It's distinct from a contractual late fee. A late fee is something you and the client agreed to in advance. Prejudgment interest is a statutory right that can apply even when your agreement is silent on interest entirely.

## Where does the right come from?

Two parts of the California Civil Code do most of the work:

- **Civil Code § 3289** sets a default rate of 10% per year on a breach of a contract that doesn't specify an interest rate. So if you and the client never agreed on interest, the law fills the gap at 10% for contract debts.
- **Civil Code § 3287** addresses when interest is recoverable — generally once the damages are "certain, or capable of being made certain by calculation," and the right to recover is vested in the creditor on a particular day.

The practical upshot: for an ordinary unpaid invoice — a fixed, known dollar amount that came due on a known date — you're often in exactly the territory where 10% statutory interest applies.

## When does the interest start accruing?

Interest generally begins on the date the amount became due and certain. For a typical invoice with clear terms, that's the payment due date — the day "net-30" expired and payment didn't arrive. From that day forward, the debt is, in effect, earning 10% a year for you.

The "certain" requirement matters. Prejudgment interest is easiest to claim when the amount is fixed and undisputed — a specific invoice for a specific sum. If the amount itself is genuinely in dispute (for example, an unliquidated claim where reasonable people could calculate different totals), the analysis gets more complicated. A clean, itemized invoice is your friend here.

## How do you calculate it?

The math is simple once you have the inputs:

1. Take the principal (the unpaid invoice amount).
2. Apply the annual rate (often 10% under § 3289).
3. Multiply by the time elapsed since the due date.

For example, a $6,000 invoice that's been unpaid for nine months accrues roughly $6,000 × 10% × (9/12) = $450 in interest. That number grows every month the client delays — which is precisely why it functions as pressure as well as compensation.

Keep your calculation transparent: principal, rate, start date, and running total. A demand that shows the interest accruing tells the client the cost of continued silence.

## How does interest strengthen a demand letter?

Two ways. First, it raises the legitimate amount owed, so the client isn't just deciding whether to pay the invoice — they're deciding whether to pay the invoice *plus* a growing interest figure *plus*, potentially, a contractual late fee. Second, it reframes delay as expensive. Every month the client stalls, the number on the page gets larger, and a well-drafted demand makes that explicit.

An attorney-signed demand that itemizes principal, statutory interest, and any agreed late fee reads as a calculated claim rather than a vague grievance — and that precision is part of what gets clients to pay. ## What about contractual interest rates?

If your contract *does* specify an interest rate on past-due balances, that agreed rate generally governs instead of the § 3289 default — provided it's reasonable and not an unlawful penalty. This is another reason to put a sensible finance-charge clause in your standard terms: you set the rate in advance rather than relying on the statutory backstop. Keep any contractual rate reasonable, because an excessive rate can be challenged as a penalty under Civil Code § 1671.

## Common misconceptions

- **"I can't claim interest because my contract didn't mention it."** Often false — § 3289 supplies a 10% default for contract debts with no stated rate.
- **"Interest only starts when I file suit."** No — prejudgment interest can run from the date the debt became due and certain, well before any lawsuit.
- **"It's not worth bothering with."** On a large or long-overdue debt, interest can add hundreds or thousands of dollars and meaningfully change the client's incentive to settle.

## Bottom line

A late-paying California client may owe you more than the face of the invoice. Prejudgment interest — frequently 10% per year under Civil Code § 3289 — compensates you for the time your money was withheld and accrues from the date the debt came due. Calculate it, itemize it, and put it in your demand. It both increases what you recover and makes every additional month of delay a measurably worse deal for the client.
