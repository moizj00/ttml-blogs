When someone bound by a nondisclosure agreement leaks, uses, or threatens to use your confidential information, a demand letter is the standard first strike — and in NDA disputes it does double duty. It demands the conduct stop and the information be returned or destroyed, and it creates the written record of notice that strengthens an injunction motion if you need one. California enforces NDAs as contracts, and where the information qualifies as a trade secret, the California Uniform Trade Secrets Act (Civil Code §§ 3426–3426.11) adds remedies a plain contract claim doesn't carry: injunctions, royalty damages, and — for willful and malicious misappropriation — exemplary damages and attorney fees.

**Short answer:** Move fast, in writing. An NDA breach letter should identify the agreement, the specific information, the specific breach, demand immediate cessation plus return/destruction with written certification, and preserve all evidence. Speed matters doubly here: confidential information loses protection as it spreads, and courts weigh delay against you when you later ask for an injunction.

## What Two Legal Theories Does an NDA Breach Trigger?

First, breach of contract. The NDA is a contract; using or disclosing covered information breaches it. Remedies are contract remedies — provable damages, plus whatever the agreement itself provides (some NDAs include attorney-fee clauses, which then run in favor of whoever prevails). The limitations period is four years for a written agreement (CCP § 337).

Second, trade secret misappropriation under CUTSA — but only if the information qualifies. A trade secret is information that derives independent economic value from not being generally known and is the subject of reasonable efforts to maintain its secrecy (Civil Code § 3426.1(d)). Customer lists compiled with substantial effort, pricing structures, formulas, manufacturing processes, and product roadmaps can qualify. CUTSA's remedies are stronger: injunctive relief against actual or threatened misappropriation (§ 3426.2), damages including unjust enrichment or a reasonable royalty (§ 3426.3), and for willful and malicious misappropriation, exemplary damages up to twice the award plus attorney fees (§§ 3426.3(c), 3426.4). The limitations period is three years from discovery (§ 3426.6).

A well-drafted demand letter invokes both theories where both fit, because they pressure different nerves: contract breach is certain but modest; CUTSA is conditional but severe.

## Does Your Information Actually Qualify for Protection?

Before sending anything, audit two things honestly. Secrecy efforts: did you mark documents confidential, restrict access, use passwords, require NDAs consistently? CUTSA requires "reasonable efforts," and defendants always attack this element first. Actual secrecy: information that's on your public website, in your marketing materials, or readily reconstructable from public sources isn't a trade secret no matter what the NDA calls it. An NDA can contractually protect some non-trade-secret confidential information, but California courts won't enforce confidentiality clauses so broad they operate as de facto non-competes — Business and Professions Code § 16600's policy against restraining lawful work limits how far "confidential information" definitions stretch, especially against former employees. If your real grievance is that an ex-employee is competing, see [is my non-compete even enforceable in California](/blog/2026-06-06-cease-and-desist-from-old-employer-non-compete-california) — the answer changes your leverage.

This audit determines your letter's honest strength. A letter built on genuinely secret, well-guarded information can credibly threaten an injunction. A letter built on generalized "they know our business" grievances invites a confident refusal.

## What Should the NDA Breach Demand Letter Contain?

Seven elements. The agreement: date, parties, and the specific confidentiality provisions breached — quote them. The protected information: described with as much specificity as you can without re-disclosing secrets in the letter itself (a real drafting skill — categories and document identifiers, not the secrets). The breach: what they did, when, and how you know — the investor deck shopped to a competitor, the customer list in a personal email account, the identical pricing matrix appearing in a rival's bid. The demand: immediate cessation of use and disclosure; return or destruction of all copies in every medium; written certification of compliance by a deadline (10–14 days is typical); and identification of every person or entity to whom disclosure was made. The preservation notice: instruct them to preserve all relevant documents, devices, and accounts — spoliation after notice devastates defendants in trade secret cases. The legal consequences: breach of contract and, where applicable, CUTSA misappropriation with its injunction, royalty, exemplary damages, and fee provisions. And the escalation path: litigation including a temporary restraining order if cessation isn't confirmed.

If the recipient took the information to a new employer, consider a separate, carefully worded notice letter to that employer. Done right, it creates knowledge that supports liability for the new employer's continued use; done carelessly, it invites a tortious interference claim — this letter especially benefits from attorney drafting.

## What Remedies Are Realistic?

Injunctions are the heart of NDA enforcement, because money rarely fixes a leak. Courts can order defendants to stop using the information, return materials, and refrain from soliciting customers obtained through misappropriation. The demand letter materially helps here: when you later seek a TRO, the letter proves the defendant knew, was warned, and continued — which supports both irreparable harm and willfulness. Damages come in several flavors: your actual loss (lost customers, lost deals), the defendant's unjust enrichment, or — where neither is provable — a reasonable royalty for the use. Exemplary damages and attorney fees under CUTSA require willful and malicious misappropriation, a bar that continued use after your demand letter helps you clear. On the pure contract side, check your NDA for a liquidated damages clause; California enforces them if they were a reasonable estimate of anticipated harm, not a penalty.

Be realistic about the common case: most NDA disputes end with a compliance letter — certification of return/destruction, confirmation of non-use, sometimes a modest payment — rather than a damages trial. That outcome, achieved in weeks for the cost of a letter, usually beats litigation whose discovery costs can exceed the harm.

## What If You're on a Deadline — Is the Information Spreading?

Triage by velocity. If disclosure is imminent or ongoing — a product launch built on your designs, a pitch using your deck happening this week — skip extended correspondence: counsel can seek a TRO and expedited discovery, with the demand letter sent simultaneously rather than sequentially. If the breach is historical and contained — a former contractor kept files but no spread is evident — the letter-first path is right, and the certification demand does most of the work. Either way, start a written chronology now: when you learned what, from whom. CUTSA's three-year discovery clock (§ 3426.6) and the four-year contract clock (CCP § 337) both reward contemporaneous records, and your credibility on "reasonable efforts" is built from documents, not recollection.

## How Does This Differ From an Ordinary Contract Demand?

Three ways worth internalizing. Secrecy is the asset: every week of inaction and every additional disclosure erodes the thing you're protecting — delay is substantive harm, not just tactical drift. Certification replaces payment as the core demand: you're usually negotiating for verified deletion and non-use, not a check. And willfulness is built, not found: the demand letter itself manufactures the knowledge element that turns continued use into the willful and malicious misappropriation that carries doubled damages and fees. In ordinary debt disputes the letter asks for what you're owed; in NDA disputes the letter changes what the other side's future conduct will cost them.
