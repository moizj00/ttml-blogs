**Short answer:** If your California contractor has stopped working without justification and isn't responding to communication, you likely have a case for contract abandonment under Business & Professions Code § 7107. Your immediate steps: document everything, send a written cure notice giving them a deadline to return, then pursue three parallel tracks — CSLB complaint, bond claim, and demand letter. The CSLB bond is $25,000 as of 2026.

## Abandonment vs. breach: why the distinction matters

Not every contractor who stops working has "abandoned" your project in the legal sense. The distinction affects which remedies are available and how quickly you can pursue them.

**Abandonment** occurs when the contractor leaves the project without justification, fails to communicate, and demonstrates no intent to return. Under California law, this triggers enhanced penalties including potential license revocation.

**Breach** is broader — the contractor may still be communicating, may have a disputed justification for stopping, or may have partially performed but done so defectively. Breach gets you damages; abandonment gets you damages *plus* regulatory consequences that give you leverage.

The practical test: Has the contractor been unreachable for more than 7–10 business days with no explanation? That's abandonment, not just a scheduling delay.


## The first 48 hours: documentation protocol

Before you do anything else, create your evidence file. Courts and the CSLB both want specifics, and memory degrades fast.

**Photograph everything** — the current state of work, materials on site, any damage or exposed elements left unprotected. Timestamp your photos (your phone does this automatically, but verify).

**Compile your paper trail** — the signed contract, all change orders, every payment receipt or canceled check, text messages, emails, and voicemails. Print the texts; screenshot conversations before they're deleted.

**Get a completion estimate** — call two licensed contractors for written estimates to finish the remaining work. The difference between what you've already paid and what it costs to complete (using a new contractor) is your core damage figure.

**Note the calendar** — write down the last date the contractor performed work, the last date they communicated, and today's date. These intervals matter for the cure notice.

## The cure notice: your required first move

California courts generally require that you give the breaching party an opportunity to fix the problem before you terminate the contract and pursue damages. This is your "cure notice."

A proper cure notice includes:

1. A clear statement that the contractor has failed to perform
2. A specific deadline to resume work (10–14 days is standard)
3. A statement that failure to cure will result in contract termination
4. Reference to the specific contract provision being violated

Send this certified mail, return receipt requested. Also send it via email and text if you have those contacts. You want proof of delivery that a court will accept.

> **B&P Code § 7107** — Abandonment of a construction project without legal excuse constitutes a cause for disciplinary action against a contractor's license.

If the contractor doesn't respond within your cure period, you have now satisfied the notice requirement and can proceed to termination and remedies.


## Track 1: The CSLB complaint

The Contractors State License Board exists for exactly this scenario. Filing a complaint is free and can result in:

- License suspension or revocation
- Restitution orders (the CSLB can order the contractor to pay you back)
- Citation and fine against the contractor
- Referral to the CSLB Arbitration Program for claims between $7,500 and $45,000

**How to file:** Go to cslb.ca.gov, click "File a Complaint," and submit the online form. Attach your contract, payment records, cure notice, and photos. The CSLB assigns an investigator, who will contact both parties.

**Timeline:** Investigation typically takes 3–6 months. This is slow — which is why you pursue other tracks simultaneously.

**Pro tip:** The mere existence of a CSLB complaint creates leverage. Many contractors will negotiate a refund once they realize their license is at risk. A demand letter that mentions a pending CSLB complaint tends to get faster responses than one that doesn't.

## Track 2: The contractor's bond claim

Every licensed California contractor must maintain a $25,000 contractor license bond. This bond exists specifically to compensate consumers harmed by the contractor's failure to perform.

| Bond Claim Element | What You Need |
|-------------------|---------------|
| **Who to claim against** | The surety company listed on the contractor's CSLB license record |
| **Maximum recovery** | $25,000 (shared among all claimants) |
| **Deadline** | Within 4 years of the abandonment |
| **How to find the surety** | CSLB website → License Check → look for bond information |
| **Processing time** | 30–90 days typical |

The bond claim process: write to the surety company with your contract, proof of payment, evidence of abandonment, and your damage calculation. The surety will investigate and either pay the claim or deny it. If denied, you can sue the surety directly.

**Important limitation:** The $25,000 bond is shared among ALL claimants. If your contractor abandoned multiple projects (common with failing companies), you may only recover a fraction of your claim. File early.


## Track 3: The demand letter

This is often the fastest path to resolution. A formal demand letter — especially one from an attorney — communicates that you're serious, you know your rights, and you've already begun the process of pursuing them.

An effective contractor abandonment demand letter includes:

- Statement of the contract terms and work scope
- Documentation of payments made vs. work completed
- The specific cost to complete using a replacement contractor
- Citation to B&P § 7107 (abandonment) and § 7109 (failure to complete)
- Reference to the CSLB complaint (if filed)
- A specific dollar demand and a 10-day response deadline
- A statement of intent to pursue litigation if unresolved

The demand letter serves double duty: it's your last attempt at resolution *and* it becomes Exhibit A in any subsequent lawsuit showing the court you tried to resolve this without litigation.

## Track 4: Litigation (when the other tracks fail)

If the demand letter, CSLB process, and bond claim don't fully compensate you, civil litigation is the remaining option.

**Small claims court** (up to $10,000, or $5,000 against a business entity): No lawyer needed. Fast — usually heard within 60–90 days. Best for smaller projects where the overpayment is under the limit.

**Limited civil** ($10,001–$25,000): Streamlined discovery, usually resolved within 6–12 months.

**Unlimited civil** (over $25,000): Full litigation. Expensive. Reserved for large projects or cases where you want to pursue punitive damages for fraud.

## The damage calculation

Your damages in an abandonment case follow a specific formula:

**Amount paid to contractor** – **Value of work actually completed** + **Cost to complete with a new contractor** – **Original remaining contract balance** = **Your damages**

Example: You had a $100,000 contract. You paid $60,000. The contractor completed $35,000 worth of work (as estimated by your replacement contractor). Finishing the project costs $75,000 with the new contractor.

$60,000 – $35,000 + $75,000 – $40,000 = **$60,000 in damages**

That accounts for the overpayment ($25,000) plus the increased cost of completion ($35,000 more than the original remaining balance).

## Protecting yourself during the gap

While you pursue these tracks, your half-built project sits exposed. Practical steps:

- **Cover exposed areas** to prevent weather damage (this is mitigation — you're required to minimize damages)
- **Don't hire a replacement contractor immediately** unless weather or safety requires it — give the cure notice period time to expire
- **Don't dispose of the original contractor's materials or tools** left on site without written notice
- **Keep paying your property taxes and HOA** — an unfinished project doesn't excuse other obligations

## The timeline that actually works

| Week | Action |
|------|--------|
| **Week 1** | Document everything. Send cure notice. Research CSLB license and bond info. |
| **Week 2** | Cure period expires. File CSLB complaint. Get completion estimates. |
| **Week 3** | Send formal [demand letter](/blog/contractor-walked-off-job-california). File bond claim with surety. |
| **Week 4–6** | Wait for demand response. Begin hiring replacement contractor. |
| **Week 6–8** | If no resolution: file in small claims or retain litigation counsel. |
| **Month 3–6** | CSLB investigation progresses. Bond claim processes. Litigation (if filed) moves toward trial. |

## When you need a lawyer from day one

Three scenarios where DIY isn't advisable:

1. **The project is over $50,000 and you've paid more than 50%** — the stakes justify professional help from the start.
2. **The contractor is threatening to lien your property** — you need immediate legal response to a fraudulent lien claim.
3. **There are safety hazards** (exposed electrical, structural instability) — liability issues require professional guidance on mitigation obligations.

For everything else, the demand letter is your most cost-effective first professional intervention. It costs a fraction of litigation, resolves most disputes within 30 days, and positions you for every subsequent legal option if it doesn't.
