If someone is using your business name in California, you likely have legal options — even without a federal trademark registration. California Business and Professions Code § 14245 prohibits use of a mark that is "likely to cause confusion" with an existing mark, and California's Unfair Competition Law (Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200) broadly covers unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business practices including name theft. Your first practical step is sending an attorney-signed cease and desist letter demanding they stop using the name.

## Do I Need a Registered Trademark to Protect My Business Name?

No. California recognizes common-law trademark rights based on first use in commerce. If you were using the name first in your geographic area, you have priority — even without filing anything. That said, a registered trademark (state or federal) makes enforcement dramatically easier because it creates a legal presumption of ownership.

If you have a California state trademark registration, you can seek injunctive relief and damages under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 14245. Federal registration under the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1114) opens the door to statutory damages, attorney's fees, and nationwide protection.

## What Is My Strongest Legal Claim?

It depends on your situation, but most California business name disputes fall into one of three buckets:

**Trademark infringement** — If the other party is using an identical or confusingly similar name in the same industry, this is your strongest claim. You can bring it under state law (Bus. & Prof. Code § 14245) or federal law (15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)) even without registration.

**Unfair competition** — California's UCL (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200) is one of the broadest unfair competition statutes in the country. If the name use is misleading customers or diverting your business, you have a claim here.

**False advertising** — If the other business is actively using your name to imply an association or endorsement that doesn't exist, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17500 applies.

## Should I Send a Cease and Desist Letter First?

Almost always yes. A cease and desist letter from an attorney is the fastest, cheapest way to resolve a business name dispute. Most small operators don't realize they're infringing — or don't want to fight a legal battle — and will change their name after receiving a formal letter.

## What If They Refuse to Stop?

If the letter doesn't work, your options escalate:

1. **File a complaint with the California Secretary of State** — If the other business registered an LLC or corporation under your name, you can challenge the registration.
2. **File a UDRP complaint** — If they registered a domain name using your business name, you can dispute it through ICANN's Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy.
3. **File a lawsuit** — In California Superior Court for state claims, or federal court if you have a registered trademark or the infringement crosses state lines.
