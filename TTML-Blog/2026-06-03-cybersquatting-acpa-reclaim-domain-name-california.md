If a cybersquatter has registered a domain name using your trademark, you can reclaim it — usually through a UDRP arbitration that resolves in about two months, or through a federal lawsuit under the Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act (ACPA), 15 U.S.C. § 1125(d), which also lets you recover money damages. California businesses additionally have unfair-competition claims under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200. The right path depends on whether you want the domain back fast and cheap, or whether you want to punish the squatter financially.

**Short answer:** Document the bad-faith registration, send a demand letter, and then either file a UDRP complaint to transfer the domain (weeks, lower cost, no damages) or sue under the ACPA for transfer plus statutory damages of $1,000–$100,000 per domain.

## What is cybersquatting under California and federal law?

Cybersquatting is registering, trafficking in, or using a domain name that is identical or confusingly similar to someone else's trademark, with a bad-faith intent to profit from that mark. Congress created the ACPA in 1999 precisely because squatters were grabbing brand names and either parking them for ad revenue or ransoming them back to the rightful owners.

The law focuses on intent. Buying a generic domain for a real business is perfectly legal. Buying "yourbrandname.com" because you hope the brand owner will pay to get it is not. The ACPA gives trademark owners a federal cause of action even when the squatter never sells a single product, because the harm is the misappropriation of the mark itself.

California's Unfair Competition Law reinforces these federal rights. Section 17200 broadly prohibits "unlawful, unfair or fraudulent" business practices, and using another company's brand in a domain to divert customers can qualify. For California businesses, this means you often have both a federal and a state angle.

## How do you prove bad-faith intent?

Bad faith is the heart of an ACPA claim, and the statute lists nine factors courts weigh. In plain terms, these are the signals that point to a squatter:

The registrant has no trademark or other rights in the name. They aren't using it for a real, legitimate business. They offered to sell the domain to you — or to the public — without ever using it for a bona fide site. They provided false contact information to the registrar. They've registered multiple domains matching other companies' marks. And the domain incorporates a mark that was distinctive or famous when they registered it.

By contrast, a registrant has a defense if they reasonably believed their use was fair or lawful. Someone who registered a domain matching your mark for genuine commentary, a fan site, or an unrelated business in a different field may not be liable. This is why documentation matters so much: the clearer the bad faith, the stronger your case.

## What evidence should you gather first?

Before you send anything or file anything, build your record. Take dated screenshots of the domain's landing page — especially if it's a parking page full of pay-per-click ads or a "this domain is for sale" banner. Save any emails or listings where the squatter offered to sell the domain. Pull the WHOIS registration data showing when the domain was created and who registered it (even if the details are masked by a privacy service).

Then document your own trademark rights. If you have a federal registration, note the registration number and first-use date. If you rely on common-law rights, gather evidence of your actual use in commerce — your website launch date, advertising, sales records, and press coverage. The earlier your provable brand use predates their registration, the better.

## Is UDRP or an ACPA lawsuit right for you?

Every major domain registrar requires registrants to agree to the Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy (UDRP). That contractual agreement is what makes UDRP arbitration possible without going to court. You file a complaint with an approved provider such as the WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center or the Forum, pay a filing fee, and a panel decides whether the domain should be transferred to you or cancelled.

To win a UDRP case, you must show three things: the domain is identical or confusingly similar to a mark in which you have rights; the registrant has no legitimate interest in the name; and the domain was registered and is being used in bad faith. The process is paper-based, fast (often around two months), and far cheaper than litigation. The catch is that the only remedies are transfer or cancellation — you can't collect money.

The ACPA lawsuit is the heavier tool. It's slower and more expensive, but it offers what UDRP can't: statutory damages between $1,000 and $100,000 per infringing domain, plus the possibility of recovering the domain even when the squatter is anonymous, through an "in rem" action filed against the domain itself in the judicial district of the registrar. For a serial squatter, or one also infringing your brand offline, the ACPA route can be worth the cost.

Many California businesses choose UDRP first because it solves the immediate problem — getting the domain back — at a fraction of the cost. They reserve the ACPA for situations involving damages, repeat offenders, or registrants who can't be reached any other way.

## What does the step-by-step recovery process look like?

Start with a demand letter. A clear, attorney-signed cease and desist that identifies your mark, the offending domain, and your intent to pursue UDRP or ACPA remedies resolves a surprising number of disputes. Squatters who counted on an easy ransom often release the domain rather than face a formal proceeding. A demand letter is also the cheapest first step and creates a paper trail showing you tried to resolve things before escalating.

If the demand fails, decide between UDRP and ACPA based on your goals. For UDRP, you draft and file a complaint with your chosen provider, lay out the three required elements with your evidence, and wait for the panel's decision. If you win, the registrar transfers the domain to you. For ACPA, you file in federal court, which is appropriate when you want damages or need the in rem option against an unreachable registrant.

Throughout, keep your trademark portfolio in order. A registered mark makes both UDRP and ACPA claims dramatically easier, because you don't have to prove common-law rights from scratch. If you haven't registered yet, see [Can I Trademark My Brand Name Myself or Do I Need a Lawyer in California?](/blog/2026-06-03-trademark-your-brand-name-yourself-or-lawyer-california).

## How much does it cost and how long does it take?

A demand letter is the lowest-cost option and can resolve a dispute in days if the squatter folds. UDRP filing fees through providers like WIPO typically run into the low thousands of dollars for a single-domain, single-panelist case, plus any attorney time to draft the complaint, and decisions usually land in roughly two months. An ACPA lawsuit is the most expensive and least predictable on timing, but it's the only route that puts money damages on the table.

For most California small businesses, the math favors starting small: send the letter, escalate to UDRP if needed, and reserve litigation for the cases that truly warrant it.

## What about related brand attacks?

Cybersquatting rarely travels alone. The same bad actor may also be running ads on your brand name or impersonating you on social media. For those parallel problems, see [Someone Is Using My Brand Name in Their Google Ads — What Can I Do in California?](/blog/2026-06-03-someone-using-brand-name-google-ads-california) and [How Do I Send a Cease and Desist for Trademark Infringement on Instagram in California?](/blog/2026-06-03-cease-and-desist-trademark-infringement-instagram-california). Treating the whole campaign together — domain, ads, and social — is usually more effective than fighting one front at a time.

The bottom line for California businesses: cybersquatting is one of the more winnable IP fights, because the law is squarely on the side of trademark owners and the UDRP system was built to resolve these disputes quickly. The key is good evidence, a strong demand, and choosing the remedy that matches your goals.

## What about typosquatting and lookalike domains?

A close cousin of classic cybersquatting is "typosquatting" — registering common misspellings of your domain (a dropped letter, a swapped character, a different extension) to catch customers who fumble the address. These lookalike domains can be used to run ads, phish your customers, or redirect traffic to a competitor. The ACPA and UDRP both reach typosquatting, because the test is confusing similarity, not identical spelling.

If you find a typosquatted version of your domain, treat it like any other squatting case: document the bad-faith use, send a demand, and escalate to UDRP or the ACPA if needed. Because typosquatters often run several variations at once, a single UDRP complaint or ACPA action covering multiple domains can be more efficient than handling them one at a time. Proactively registering the most obvious misspellings of your own domain is a cheap insurance policy that takes those targets off the board entirely.

## A quick checklist before you act

Confirm your trademark rights and first-use date. Capture dated screenshots of the squatter's page and any sale offer. Pull the WHOIS record. Send an attorney-signed demand. Then choose your remedy: UDRP for a fast, low-cost transfer, or the ACPA when you want damages or need an in rem action against an unreachable registrant. Following that sequence keeps your costs down and your leverage high.
