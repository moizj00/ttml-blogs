If a debt collector is calling repeatedly, threatening you, or contacting you at work in California, that conduct may be illegal. The Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (Civil Code § 1788 and following) and the federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (15 U.S.C. § 1692) prohibit harassment, false threats, and abusive tactics — and let you recover statutory damages.

**Short answer:** Under California's Rosenthal Act and the federal FDCPA, collectors can't harass, threaten, or lie to you. You can demand they stop contacting you in writing, and a violation can entitle you to statutory damages.

## What collection tactics are illegal in California?

The Rosenthal Act and FDCPA bar a wide range of conduct: calling repeatedly to annoy or harass; using profane or threatening language; falsely threatening arrest or lawsuits the collector can't or won't pursue; contacting you at work after being told it's prohibited; disclosing your debt to third parties; and misrepresenting the amount or legal status of the debt. California's Act extends these rules to creditors collecting their own debts, broader coverage than federal law alone.

## How do I make a debt collector stop contacting me?

Send a written request that they cease contact. Under the FDCPA, once a collector receives a written notice to stop, it generally must end communications except to confirm it will stop or to notify you of a specific legal action. Keep a copy and proof of mailing — it creates a record if the collector violates the law afterward.

## Can I sue a debt collector for harassment?

Yes. Both statutes provide for statutory damages (up to $1,000 under the FDCPA), plus actual damages and attorney's fees in successful cases. A documented pattern of illegal calls or threats can become a strong claim.

## What if I don't even owe the debt?

You can demand validation. Within the FDCPA framework, you may ask the collector in writing to verify the debt; until it does, it generally must pause collection. If the debt isn't yours, is the result of identity theft, or is past the statute of limitations (written contracts in California are typically four years under Code of Civil Procedure § 337), say so in writing. Collecting on a debt the collector knows is invalid or time-barred can itself violate the law.

## Where do I report an abusive collector?

You can file complaints with the California Department of Financial Protection and Innovation, the federal Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, and the California Attorney General. For a comparison of complaint venues and when to bring in a lawyer, see [where to file a consumer complaint in California](/blog/2026-06-05-where-to-file-consumer-complaint-california-2026).

## When should you send a formal demand letter?

A cease-contact and demand letter that cites the Rosenthal Act and FDCPA does two things: it legally stops the calls and it builds your damages record if the collector ignores it. Collectors take statute-citing letters seriously because each violation can carry its own liability.
