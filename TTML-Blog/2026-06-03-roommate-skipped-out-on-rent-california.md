# Your Roommate Moved Out and Left You Holding the Rent. Now What?

The room's empty. Their half of the rent isn't. The lease has both your names on it, which means the landlord doesn't care whose fault it is — they want the full amount, on the first, from whoever's still there.

That's you. The unfairness is real. But "unfair" and "owed" are two different ledgers, and you can work both at once.

## Two problems, not one

You actually have two separate fights here, and it helps to keep them apart.

The first is with your landlord. Under most California leases, co-tenants are "jointly and severally liable" — a dry phrase that means each of you is on the hook for all of the rent, not just your half. The landlord can demand the whole amount from you, and the fact that your roommate left doesn't change that. That's between you and them.

The second is with the roommate. They agreed, to you, to cover their share. Walking out didn't erase the promise. The money they didn't pay is money you can go after.

## The letter that starts the clock

A demand letter to a former roommate does the unglamorous work of turning a grievance into a claim. It names the amount, the months, and a deadline. It points to whatever you agreed to: a signed roommate agreement if you have one, or the text thread where they promised to pay if you don't.

Most people skip this and go straight to resentment. The letter is what converts resentment into something a court can actually read.

Keep the receipts — the lease, the record showing you covered their share, any message where they acknowledged owing it. A roommate who ghosts on rent usually ghosts on the first few texts too, which is exactly why a dated, mailed letter matters.

## If they don't pay

California small claims court handles up to $12,500 for an individual, no lawyer, a hearing in weeks. A few months of someone's unpaid share fits comfortably under that. The letter tends to settle it; when it doesn't, you've already built the spine of your case.

(How co-tenant liability works depends on your lease and your facts. Confirm with a licensed attorney before relying on any of this.)
