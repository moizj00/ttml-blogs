Yes — in California you can usually still collect on unpaid work even if you never signed a formal contract. Oral agreements are enforceable, and a trail of emails, texts, proposals, and delivered work often proves the deal just as well as a signature would. The absence of a PDF with signatures is a complication, not a dead end.

**Short answer:** No signed contract is fine. California enforces oral agreements (CCP § 339) and "account stated" claims built from emails, invoices, and accepted work. Your evidence is the contract.

## Is an oral agreement really enforceable in California?

For most freelance and service work, yes. California recognizes oral contracts, and the statute of limitations for them is two years under Code of Civil Procedure § 339 (versus four years for written contracts under § 337). The catch isn't whether an oral deal counts — it's proving its terms. That's where your paper trail does the heavy lifting.

## What evidence proves the deal?

You're reconstructing the agreement from whatever exists:

- The email or text where the client asked you to start, or approved a quote
- A proposal, estimate, or Statement of Work they replied "looks good" to
- The delivered files, drafts, or access logs showing you did the work
- Invoices you sent and any partial payments (a partial payment is strong evidence the debt is real)

A client who paid your first invoice and then ghosted on the second has effectively admitted the arrangement. That history is powerful.

## What is an "account stated"?

When you send an invoice and the client doesn't dispute it within a reasonable time, California law can treat the balance as an "account stated" — an acknowledged debt. You don't always need to re-prove every term of the original deal; you show the invoice was sent, received, and not objected to. This is one reason to keep invoicing on a clear schedule even when a client goes quiet.

## How do you escalate without a contract?

Gather the trail first, then make the demand formal. An attorney-signed demand letter that lays out the timeline — what was agreed, what you delivered, what's owed — signals you can prove the claim despite the missing signature. For the deadline math behind your claim, see [the statute of limitations on unpaid invoices](/blog/2026-06-04-statute-of-limitations-unpaid-invoices-california-2026).

## What about the Freelance Worker Protection Act?

California's Freelance Worker Protection Act (SB 988), effective in 2025, actually requires a written contract for freelance work of $250 or more — and if the hiring party never provided one, that failure cuts against *them*, not you. The law also exposes non-paying clients to double damages. Missing paperwork can be the client's problem, not yours.

## Bottom line

A missing contract lowers the odds, it doesn't erase the claim. Assemble your emails, invoices, and proof of delivered work, then escalate. In California, the deal you can document is the deal the law will usually enforce.
