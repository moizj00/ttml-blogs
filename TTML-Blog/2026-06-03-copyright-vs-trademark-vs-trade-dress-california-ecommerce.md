If you run an ecommerce business in California, your intellectual property is likely your most valuable asset — and it's protected by three overlapping but distinct legal frameworks. Copyright covers your product photos, listing copy, and marketing content under 17 U.S.C. § 101 et seq. Trademark covers your brand name, logo, and slogans under the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1051 et seq.) and California Business and Professions Code § 14200 et seq. Trade dress covers the distinctive visual appearance of your product or packaging under 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a).

Understanding which protection applies to what is critical — because each one has different registration requirements, enforcement mechanisms, and remedies. Send a DMCA takedown for a trademark issue and it will be rejected. File a trademark complaint over stolen photos and you'll get nowhere. This guide breaks down all three so you know exactly which tool to use in each situation.

## What Does Copyright Protect for Ecommerce Sellers?

Copyright protects original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium. For ecommerce sellers, this includes:

- **Product photographs** — the single most commonly stolen asset in ecommerce
- **Original listing descriptions** — if they contain creative expression (not just factual specifications)
- **Marketing videos, graphics, and social media content**
- **Blog posts, guides, and website copy**
- **Product instruction manuals and packaging text** (if sufficiently creative)

Copyright attaches automatically the moment you create the work — no registration required for ownership. But registration with the U.S. Copyright Office ($55–$85) unlocks two critical enforcement tools: the ability to file a federal lawsuit and access to statutory damages of up to $150,000 per work (17 U.S.C. § 504(c)).

**Enforcement mechanism:** DMCA takedown notices to platforms (free, fast, removes specific content) and cease and desist letters to the infringer (addresses the person, prevents re-offense).

**What it doesn't cover:** Product designs, functional features, brand names, or ideas. You can't copyright a recipe, a product concept, or a color scheme standing alone.

## What Does Trademark Protect for Ecommerce Sellers?

Trademark protects words, names, symbols, and devices that identify the source of goods. For ecommerce sellers:

- **Your brand name** — the name customers associate with your products
- **Your logo** — the visual mark on your packaging and listings
- **Slogans and taglines** — if they identify your brand
- **Product names** — individual product line names can be trademarked

Like copyright, you get common-law trademark rights just by using the mark in commerce — no registration needed. But federal registration through the USPTO ($250–$350 per class) gives you nationwide priority, a legal presumption of validity, and the ability to use the ® symbol, which deters would-be infringers.

California also has a state trademark registration system (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 14200 et seq.) that costs $70 per class and provides state-level protection.

**Enforcement mechanism:** Cease and desist letters, platform trademark complaint forms (Amazon Brand Registry, eBay VeRO), and federal or state court litigation. The Lanham Act provides injunctive relief, actual damages, the infringer's profits, and in exceptional cases, attorney's fees (15 U.S.C. § 1117).

**What it doesn't cover:** The visual appearance of products (that's trade dress), the content of your listings (that's copyright), or functional product features (that's potentially patent territory).

## What Does Trade Dress Protect?

Trade dress protects the overall visual impression or "look and feel" of a product or its packaging — the combination of elements that, taken together, tell consumers who made it. Think of it as the aesthetic identity of a product beyond just the logo. Examples include:

- The distinctive shape of a Coca-Cola bottle
- The specific color, layout, and design of your product packaging
- The characteristic look of your product itself (product configuration trade dress)
- The overall design of a retail space or website (in rare cases)

Trade dress is protected under the same Lanham Act provision as trademarks (15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)), but it has a higher bar to clear. You must prove the trade dress is (1) distinctive and (2) non-functional, following the Supreme Court's framework in *Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 763 (1992), and *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Bros.*, 529 U.S. 205 (2000).

**Enforcement mechanism:** Cease and desist letters and federal court litigation. Trade dress claims use the same infringement framework as trademark claims (likelihood of confusion), with the additional elements of distinctiveness and non-functionality.

**What it doesn't cover:** Individual design elements standing alone (just a color, just a shape), functional features, or elements that are common in the industry.

## Side-by-Side Comparison: Copyright vs. Trademark vs. Trade Dress

**What it protects:**
Copyright covers creative works (photos, text, videos). Trademark covers brand identifiers (names, logos, slogans). Trade dress covers the overall look of products or packaging.

**How you get rights:**
Copyright arises automatically at creation. Trademark arises from use in commerce. Trade dress arises from use in commerce plus acquired distinctiveness (for product configuration).

**Registration cost:**
Copyright: $55–$85. Federal trademark: $250–$350 per class. Trade dress: $250–$350 per class (higher evidence burden).

**Primary enforcement tool:**
Copyright: DMCA takedown + cease and desist. Trademark: Platform complaint + cease and desist. Trade dress: Cease and desist + federal litigation.

**Maximum statutory damages:**
Copyright: $150,000 per work. Trademark: Infringer's profits + your damages (trebled for willful). Trade dress: Same as trademark.

## Which IP Protections Should Your California Ecommerce Business Have?

For most California ecommerce sellers, the minimum IP portfolio should include:

**Register your copyright** on your core product photography and any original content that generates revenue. Cost: $55–$85 per registration. This is the single highest-ROI IP investment for ecommerce sellers because product photos are the most frequently stolen asset.

**Register your trademark** — your brand name and logo — with the USPTO. Cost: $250–$350 per class. This is essential for Amazon Brand Registry enrollment and gives you the strongest enforcement tools against counterfeiters and copycats.

**Document your trade dress** if you have distinctive packaging. Even without formal registration, documenting the specific design elements, their market recognition, and their non-functional nature creates the evidence you'll need if someone copies your look.

**Have a cease and desist template ready.** When infringement happens — and in ecommerce, it's a matter of when, not if — you need to be able to respond within days, not weeks. An attorney-signed cease and desist letter is the enforcement tool that works across all three IP types.

## What Should You Do When You Discover Infringement?

First, identify which type of IP is being infringed. Someone stealing your photos? That's copyright — file a DMCA takedown and send a [cease and desist letter](/blog/2026-05-27-ecommerce-sellers-legal-playbook-fighting-counterfeits-california). Someone using your brand name? That's trademark — file a platform complaint and send a cease and desist. Someone copying your packaging design? That's trade dress — send a cease and desist and consult with an IP attorney about federal litigation.

The cease and desist letter is the common thread across all three. It's the single most versatile enforcement tool in IP law — and for most ecommerce disputes, it's the only one you'll need.
