"Net-30" means payment is due 30 days after the invoice date; "net-60" means 60. The difference isn't just calendar math — it sets your cash-flow risk and, just as important, the moment a friendly reminder should turn into a formal demand. In California, knowing where you are on that clock tells you when to stop nudging and start escalating.

**Short answer:** Net-30 and net-60 set the due date (30 or 60 days out). Once an invoice is genuinely past due and reminders fail, escalate to a written demand — don't let it drift.

## What do net-30 and net-60 actually mean?

The number is the window the client has to pay after you invoice. Net-30 is the common default for freelance and agency work; net-60 is a concession larger clients often request. Longer terms mean you finance the client's operations for an extra month, so price that risk in or push back before agreeing.

Be explicit about when the clock starts — invoice date versus delivery date versus receipt — because "net-30" with an ambiguous start date is where disputes hide.

## Why does the difference matter beyond cash flow?

The terms define what "late" means, which defines when your leverage kicks in. You can't credibly send a past-due notice on day 20 of a net-30 invoice. But the day after the term expires, a payment is unambiguously late, your late-fee clause (if any) activates, and your reminders carry weight. Clear terms make your escalation defensible instead of pushy.

## When should a past-due invoice become a demand letter?

A practical timeline once an invoice goes past due:

1. **Days 1–7 past due:** polite reminder, restate the balance and terms.
2. **Days 8–21 past due:** firmer notice, reference any late fee, set a hard deadline.
3. **Past ~30 days overdue with no response:** escalate to an attorney-signed demand letter.

The exact days flex with the relationship, but the principle holds: once a clear deadline passes in silence, more emails from you won't change the outcome.

## Does waiting hurt your legal position?

Not immediately — you generally have four years on a written contract (CCP § 337) and two on an oral one (CCP § 339). But aging debt is harder to collect, and a client who senses you won't follow through has no reason to prioritize you. Timely escalation is about leverage, not deadlines. For the full timeline, see [the statute of limitations on unpaid invoices](/blog/2026-06-04-statute-of-limitations-unpaid-invoices-california-2026).

## Bottom line

Set the terms clearly, start the clock unambiguously, and treat the day after the term expires as the start of your escalation ladder. Net-30 and net-60 aren't just billing jargon — they're the schedule that tells you when a reminder should become a demand.
