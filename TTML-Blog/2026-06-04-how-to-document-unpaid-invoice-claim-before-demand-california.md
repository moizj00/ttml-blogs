Before you send a demand letter on an unpaid invoice, the work that determines whether it succeeds happens off-stage: assembling the evidence that proves the debt is real, owed, and yours to collect. In California, a demand backed by a clean record — agreement, invoices, proof of delivery, and a tidy timeline — carries far more weight than an angry email, because it signals you could prove the case in court if it came to that.

**Short answer:** Gather your contract or accepted proposal, every invoice, proof you delivered the work, all relevant communications, and a dated timeline. That evidence pack is what makes a demand letter credible.

This guide walks through exactly what to collect, how to organize it, and how it maps onto the claim you're making. Do this once, properly, and you'll never scramble to reconstruct a debt later.

## Why documentation decides the outcome

A demand letter works on leverage, and leverage comes from credibility. A client deciding whether to pay is really estimating one thing: how likely are you to follow through and win? When your demand references specific, verifiable facts — the proposal they approved on a date, the files delivered, the invoices ignored — that estimate shifts toward "they can prove this." When your demand is vague, the client reads it as a bluff. The evidence pack is what turns the bluff into a credible threat of enforcement.

It also forces honesty. Assembling the record sometimes reveals a weak spot — an ambiguous scope, a missing approval — that's better to find now than after you've filed. Knowing your weak points lets you frame the demand around your strengths.

## Step 1: Establish the agreement

Start with proof that a deal existed and what its terms were:

- A signed contract or Statement of Work, if you have one.
- An accepted proposal or estimate — including the email where the client said "approved," "go ahead," or similar.
- For oral deals, the messages that confirm the arrangement: texts, DMs, or emails discussing scope, price, and timing.

If your agreement was written, you're on the four-year limitations track under Code of Civil Procedure § 337; if oral, the two-year track under § 339. Either way, capture whatever defines the terms. Under California's Freelance Worker Protection Act (SB 988), the hiring party was generally required to provide a written contract for work of $250 or more — and their failure to do so is evidence in your favor, not against you.

## Step 2: Compile every invoice

Pull together each invoice tied to the debt, showing:

- Invoice number, date, and the amount due.
- The payment terms (net-30, net-60) and due date.
- Any agreed late fee and how it was calculated.
- The delivery method and date sent, so you can show the client received it.

If the client never disputed an invoice within a reasonable time, you may have an "account stated" — an acknowledged debt — which simplifies your claim. Note any invoice that went unanswered for weeks; silence is useful evidence.

## Step 3: Prove you delivered the work

Performance is half the claim. Collect whatever shows you did what you promised:

- Final files, deliverables, or links, with timestamps.
- Access logs, shared-drive history, or version histories.
- Sign-offs, "this looks great" messages, or the client using the work publicly.
- Photos, screenshots, or exports for work that isn't a single file.

A client who used your deliverable — published the site, ran the campaign, shipped the product — has effectively admitted they received value. Capture that.

## Step 4: Gather the communications trail

Assemble the back-and-forth in chronological order:

- The thread where the deal was struck.
- Status updates and approvals during the project.
- Your payment reminders and any client responses (including partial payments, which can reset the limitations clock and prove the debt is real).
- Any excuses, promises to pay, or acknowledgments — these are gold.

Keep these in their original form (emails with headers, full text threads). Reconstructed or paraphrased messages are weaker than the originals.

## Step 5: Build a one-page timeline

Now distill everything into a single dated narrative:

1. Date the agreement was made and its key terms.
2. Dates work was delivered.
3. Invoice dates, amounts, and due dates.
4. Each reminder you sent and the client's response (or silence).
5. The current balance, including any late fee and statutory interest.

This timeline is the spine of your demand letter. It lets an attorney — or a judge — understand the whole dispute in sixty seconds, and it exposes any gaps while you still have time to fill them.

## Step 6: Calculate what you're actually owed

Separate the components so the number is defensible:

- **Principal:** the unpaid invoice total.
- **Contractual late fee:** only if it was agreed in writing and is reasonable under Civil Code § 1671.
- **Statutory interest:** prejudgment interest, often 10% per year under Civil Code § 3289 once the amount is fixed, may apply even without a contractual rate.

A precise, itemized figure reads as someone who knows their rights. A round "you owe me about five grand" reads as a guess.

## Turning the pack into a demand

With the evidence assembled, the demand letter nearly writes itself: it states the agreement, the delivery, the unpaid invoices, the total owed, and a firm deadline — each point anchored to a document you hold. An attorney-signed version adds the final increment of credibility, because it tells the client a lawyer has reviewed the file and is prepared to act. ## Common documentation mistakes to avoid

- **Waiting until the debt is old to organize.** Evidence is freshest now; gather it before memories and inboxes decay.
- **Relying on memory instead of records.** "We agreed on $4,000" is far weaker than the email showing it.
- **Overstating the claim.** Inflated late fees or vague extras undercut the credible parts. Keep the demand to what you can prove.
- **Deleting the messy parts.** The client's excuses and broken promises are evidence — keep them.

## Bottom line

A demand letter is the visible tip of a claim; the evidence pack is the mass beneath it. Establish the agreement, compile the invoices, prove delivery, gather the communications, build a timeline, and calculate a precise total. Do that, and your demand stops looking like a complaint and starts looking like the first step of a case you're ready to win — which is exactly why most clients pay before it gets that far.
