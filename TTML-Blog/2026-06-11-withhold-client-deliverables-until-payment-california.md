Your client owes you $6,000 and keeps promising the check is coming. You're sitting on the finished website, the completed designs, the final video edit. The temptation is obvious: don't hand over the work until the money shows up. But can you actually do that in California without creating a bigger problem for yourself?

**Short answer: It depends on your contract. If your agreement states that ownership of deliverables transfers only upon full payment, you're on solid ground. If it's silent on the issue — or if you've already delivered partial work product — withholding can breach your own contractual obligations and expose you to counterclaims. California recognizes a common-law "retaining lien" for some service providers, but it's narrower than most freelancers assume.**


## When Withholding Works

The strongest position: your contract explicitly says "all deliverables remain the property of [freelancer] until final payment is received in full." With that clause, you're exercising a contractual right, not playing games. The client agreed to these terms.

> **California Civil Code § 2872–2883 (Liens)** — California recognizes various possessory liens — the right to retain property you've worked on until you're paid. Mechanics, artisans, and service providers who add value to physical property have statutory lien rights. For digital deliverables, your contract is the lien.

The key factors:

- **Written contract with payment-contingent transfer clause:** You're in the clear.
- **No written contract, but work not yet delivered:** You have an argument, though it's weaker.
- **Work already partially delivered:** Withholding the remainder is murkier and depends on whether the delivered portion is usable independently.

## When It Backfires

| Scenario | Risk Level | Why |
|----------|-----------|-----|
| Contract says ownership transfers on delivery | High | Withholding breaches YOUR obligation |
| Client already has access to servers/files | High | You can't "undeliver" what they already have |
| Work includes client's proprietary content | High | Holding their own data hostage invites counterclaims |
| No contract exists | Medium | Courts may imply reasonable terms favoring delivery |
| Contract explicitly conditions transfer on payment | Low | You're exercising a contractual right |

The worst scenario: you withhold deliverables, the client sues for breach, and a court orders you to deliver the work AND awards them damages for the delay. Now you're owed $6,000 and you owe them $10,000. Losing while holding the stronger hand.


## The Smarter Play

Rather than a silent holdout, send a formal [demand letter](/blog/client-ghosted-after-final-deliverable-california) that makes the situation explicit: "Payment of $X was due on [date]. Per Section [Y] of our agreement, deliverables will be transferred upon receipt of full payment. If payment is not received by [deadline], we will pursue collection remedies under California law."

That letter does three things at once: it documents the outstanding debt, invokes your contractual right to withhold, and signals that legal action follows. It converts a standoff into a structured negotiation.

For freelancers without a payment-contingent transfer clause in their current contracts, the fix is prospective: add one to every future agreement. One sentence in a contract is worth more than any amount of leverage after the fact.
