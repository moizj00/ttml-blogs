**Short answer: California Civ. Code § 1954 requires your landlord to give you at least 24 hours' written notice before entering, and only for specific reasons. Entering without notice or for unauthorized purposes is illegal. You can send a written demand, sue for trespass, or — in extreme cases — break the lease.**

You come home and something's off. A maintenance guy you didn't call was in your unit. Or your landlord admits she "stopped by to check on things." Or you find a lockbox code was used while you were at work.

California doesn't treat your apartment like your landlord's property that you happen to sleep in. It treats it like your home — because it is.


## What the Law Actually Requires

> **California Civ. Code § 1954** — A landlord may enter a dwelling unit only for specific purposes: agreed-upon repairs, emergencies, showing the unit to prospective tenants or buyers, or under court order. Except for emergencies, the landlord must give at least 24 hours' reasonable written notice.

The notice must state the date, approximate time, and purpose of entry. "I'm coming by sometime this week" doesn't count. Neither does a text sent at 11 PM for a 9 AM visit.

The landlord can enter without notice in exactly one scenario: a genuine emergency. Burst pipe, gas leak, fire. "I wanted to see if you're keeping the place clean" is not an emergency. Neither is "the HOA asked me to check the balcony."

## Legal vs. Illegal Entry: The Quick Test

| Scenario | Legal? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 24-hour written notice for a plumber you requested | Yes | Agreed repair + proper notice |
| Landlord enters during a fire/flood | Yes | Emergency exception |
| Landlord shows unit to buyers with 24-hour notice | Yes | Statutory right, proper notice |
| Landlord "stops by" without any notice | No | No notice, no emergency |
| Landlord enters while you're on vacation to "inspect" | No | Inspection without notice or consent |
| Landlord uses a key to enter at night | No | Unreasonable time, likely no notice |
| Landlord enters to harass or intimidate | No | Not a permitted purpose under § 1954 |

## What to Do When It Happens

**Step 1 — Document it.** Write down exactly what happened: the date, what you found disturbed, any witnesses, any camera footage. If your landlord admitted to entering, save the text or email.

**Step 2 — Send a written warning.** A calm, factual letter citing Civ. Code § 1954, stating what happened and requesting that all future entries comply with the statute. Send it by email *and* certified mail so you have a paper trail.

**Step 3 — Escalate if it continues.** Repeated unauthorized entries are grounds for:

- **A formal demand letter** from an attorney, which puts the landlord on legal notice.
- **A lawsuit for trespass** — California courts can award actual damages plus statutory damages for intentional violations.
- **Lease termination** — if the invasions are severe or continuous, you may have grounds to break your lease under the constructive eviction doctrine without penalty.

The practical reality: most landlords stop after a single attorney letter. The ones who don't tend to be the ones a judge needs to hear about.

## One Thing to Watch For

Some landlords retaliate after a tenant pushes back — raising rent, cutting services, or serving a bogus eviction notice. California's anti-retaliation statute (Civ. Code § 1942.5) makes that illegal too. If your landlord retaliates within 180 days of you exercising your rights, the burden shifts to *them* to prove the action was unrelated.

Your apartment is your home. The law says so. A [demand letter](/blog/is-demand-letter-required-before-small-claims-california) is the cheapest way to make a landlord who forgot that remember.
