A consumer warranty demand letter is a written notice that formally invokes your warranty rights and demands the seller or manufacturer repair, replace, or refund a defective product. In California, it is backed by the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civil Code § 1790 and following), which gives buyers strong remedies — including the implied warranty of merchantability under Civil Code § 1792.

**Short answer:** A warranty demand letter cites the Song-Beverly Act and demands the seller honor an express or implied warranty. Sending one creates a paper trail and often resolves the dispute before litigation.

## What does a warranty demand letter do?

It converts a frustrating phone runaround into a documented legal demand. The letter identifies the product, the defect, the warranty (express or implied), and the remedy you want, with a deadline to respond. It signals you understand California's warranty law and are prepared to escalate. Because Song-Beverly allows recovery of attorney's fees to a prevailing buyer, sellers have a real incentive to resolve a well-founded claim rather than fight it.

## When should you send one?

Send a warranty demand letter when a seller or manufacturer has refused or stalled on a covered repair, replacement, or refund; when informal contact has failed; or when you need a clear record before filing in small claims court. It is the natural step after the front-line customer-service channel breaks down.

## What should the letter include?

A strong letter states the purchase date and price, describes the defect and prior repair attempts, identifies the applicable warranty, cites the Song-Beverly Act, and demands a specific remedy by a firm deadline.

It should also attach copies (never originals) of the receipt, the warranty terms, and any repair records, and keep proof of mailing. Specificity matters: a vague complaint is easy to ignore, while a dated, document-backed demand is hard to dismiss.

## What rights does the Song-Beverly Act give me?

The Act implies a warranty of merchantability in most consumer-goods sales (Civil Code § 1792), meaning the product must work for its ordinary purpose. For goods covered by an express warranty, the manufacturer must repair defects within a reasonable number of attempts; if it can't, you may be entitled to a replacement or refund. Prevailing buyers can recover attorney's fees, which is why these claims carry weight even when the dollar amount is modest.

## Does this apply to cars too?

Yes — vehicles are consumer goods under Song-Beverly, and the Act is the backbone of California's "lemon law." If your defective product is a car, see our detailed guide to the [California Lemon Law and the Song-Beverly Act](/blog/2026-06-05-california-lemon-law-song-beverly-act-2026).

## What happens if the seller ignores the letter?

If the deadline passes with no resolution, your next step is usually small claims court (limit $12,500 for individuals under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220) or, for larger or fee-bearing claims, a civil action where Song-Beverly's attorney-fee provision applies. The demand letter you sent becomes key evidence that you gave the seller a fair chance to comply.
