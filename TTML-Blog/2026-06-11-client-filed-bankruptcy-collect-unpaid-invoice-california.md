You finished the work, sent the invoice, and then got the email no freelancer wants: your client filed for bankruptcy. Your first instinct is to demand payment louder. That instinct is wrong — and potentially illegal.

**Short answer: Once a client files for bankruptcy, an automatic stay (11 U.S.C. § 362) prohibits you from collecting, suing, or even calling to demand payment. You must file a "proof of claim" with the bankruptcy court to get in line as a creditor. Whether you recover anything depends on the type of bankruptcy (Chapter 7 vs. Chapter 11), the client's assets, and where your claim ranks among other creditors.**


## The Automatic Stay: Why You Must Stop All Collection Efforts

The moment a bankruptcy petition is filed, 11 U.S.C. § 362 freezes every collection action against the debtor. That means:

- No demand letters
- No small claims filings
- No calls or emails requesting payment
- No sending the debt to collections

Violating the automatic stay can result in sanctions, contempt, and liability for damages. The court takes this seriously even when the creditor didn't know about the filing.

> **11 U.S.C. § 362(a)** — The filing of a bankruptcy petition "operates as a stay, applicable to all entities," of any act to collect, assess, or recover a claim against the debtor that arose before the filing date.

## Chapter 7 vs. Chapter 11: What It Means for Your Invoice

| Factor | Chapter 7 (Liquidation) | Chapter 11 (Reorganization) |
|--------|------------------------|----------------------------|
| What happens | Assets sold, business closes | Business continues operating |
| Your claim | Filed as unsecured creditor | May be paid under reorganization plan |
| Typical recovery | 5–15 cents on the dollar | 20–50 cents on the dollar (varies widely) |
| Timeline | 3–6 months | 6–18 months |
| Your action | File proof of claim by the bar date | File proof of claim, monitor the plan |

Chapter 7 is worse for you. The business is being dissolved, and unsecured creditors like freelancers sit near the bottom of the priority list — behind secured creditors, tax authorities, and administrative expenses.


## Filing Your Proof of Claim

You'll receive a notice from the bankruptcy court listing a deadline (the "bar date") to file your proof of claim. Miss it and you're out — no exceptions. The form requires:

- The amount owed and the basis for the claim (your contract or invoice)
- Whether the claim is secured or unsecured (yours is almost certainly unsecured)
- Supporting documentation (attach your invoice, contract, and any communications)

File electronically through the court's PACER system or mail the form to the bankruptcy court listed in the notice.

## When a Demand Letter Still Makes Sense

A demand letter sent *before* the bankruptcy filing is still valid — it's part of your paper trail proving the debt existed and was due. If you suspect a client is heading toward bankruptcy but hasn't filed yet, a [demand letter from an attorney](/blog/statute-of-limitations-unpaid-invoices-california-2026) creates urgency and documentation simultaneously. Once the petition is filed, that window closes.

If the client's principal personally guaranteed the debt — common in small business contracts — the guarantee may survive the business bankruptcy. That personal liability is a separate collection avenue worth exploring with an attorney.

## The Honest Truth

Most freelancers owed less than $5,000 by a Chapter 7 debtor won't recover enough to justify hiring a bankruptcy attorney. File the proof of claim yourself (it's free), attend the creditors' meeting if you want, and accept whatever distribution comes. The real lesson is upstream: require deposits, milestone payments, and [kill-fee clauses](/blog/no-written-contract-can-i-still-collect-california-freelancer) in your contracts so a single client's failure can't gut your cash flow.
