A defamation cease and desist letter is a formal demand — usually from an attorney — that a person or business stop making false statements about you and remove existing ones. In California, defamation is a false statement of fact, published to a third party, that damages reputation: written defamation is libel (Civil Code § 45) and spoken defamation is slander (Civil Code § 46). The letter works best when the statements are provably false facts, sent while the harm is fresh, because California's statute of limitations for defamation is only one year (Code of Civil Procedure § 340(c)).

**Short answer:** A defamation cease and desist demands the false statements stop and existing posts come down. It works when the statement is a false fact (not opinion), you can prove falsity, and you act within California's one-year deadline. Most disputes end at the letter stage because the sender's litigation threat is credible.

## What Counts as Defamation in California?

Four elements: a statement of fact (not opinion), about you, published to at least one third party, that is false and harms your reputation. "This contractor stole my deposit" is a factual claim that can be proven true or false — potentially defamatory. "This contractor was rude and I wouldn't hire him again" is opinion — protected speech. Certain statements are defamatory on their face (libel per se), including false claims that someone committed a crime, has a loathsome disease, is incompetent in their profession, or engaged in serious sexual misconduct. For those, damage to reputation is presumed.

## What Does the Letter Actually Demand?

A typical defamation cease and desist demands four things: stop making the statements, remove existing posts or publications, preserve evidence (no deleting the post history), and sometimes a retraction or correction. It identifies each false statement specifically, explains why it is false, and states the legal consequences of continuing — a defamation lawsuit seeking damages.

Specificity is what separates an effective letter from an ignored one. "Stop lying about me" achieves nothing. Quoting the exact statement, identifying where and when it was published, and attaching proof of falsity forces the recipient to confront real litigation exposure.

## When Does a Defamation Cease and Desist Work?

It works most often when three things are true. The statement is verifiably false — you have documents proving the opposite. The speaker has something to lose — a business, professional license, or reputation of their own. And the letter arrives early — before the statement spreads and hardens. A recipient facing a credible, specific letter usually deletes the post quietly, because the cost of being wrong in court (damages plus their own attorney fees) dwarfs the cost of removing a post.

It works poorly against anonymous posters (you may need a subpoena to identify them first), against true statements (truth is an absolute defense), and against pure opinion.

## What Is the Anti-SLAPP Risk in California?

California's anti-SLAPP statute (CCP § 425.16) protects speech on public issues. If you sue over protected speech and can't show a probability of winning, the court can dismiss your case early and order you to pay the other side's attorney fees. This is the single biggest reason to have an attorney evaluate a defamation claim before threatening suit: an overreaching threat against protected opinion or review-site commentary can backfire expensively. A well-drafted letter targets only provably false factual assertions, which keeps the threat credible and the anti-SLAPP risk low.

## Should You Demand a Retraction Too?

Often, yes. Against newspapers and broadcasters, California Civil Code § 48a requires a retraction demand within 20 days of learning of the publication if you want to preserve a claim for general and punitive damages. Against ordinary individuals and online posters, § 48a doesn't strictly apply, but a retraction demand still makes sense — a public correction often repairs more reputation than a quiet deletion.

## What Does It Cost and What's Next If Ignored?

A flat-fee attorney cease and desist costs a small fraction of defamation litigation, which routinely runs tens of thousands of dollars. If the letter is ignored and the statements continue, your options are a defamation suit in superior court or, for modest measurable losses, small claims court. The letter itself becomes evidence that the speaker knew the statements were disputed and continued anyway — which supports malice and higher damages.
