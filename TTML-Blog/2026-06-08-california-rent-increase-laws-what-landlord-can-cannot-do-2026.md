**Short answer: Under AB 1482, most California landlords cannot raise rent more than 5% plus local CPI (capped at 10%) in any 12-month period. Many cities impose even stricter limits. The landlord must give 30 days' written notice for increases up to 10%, or 90 days for increases over 10%. Violations are void — you don't have to pay the excess.**

California's relationship with rent increases is a story about two different ideas of what a rental market should be. One says landlords own the property and should charge what the market will bear. The other says housing is too essential to leave entirely to market forces — that a person who pays rent on time and follows the rules deserves some predictability about what that rent will be next year.

AB 1482, signed in 2019, was California's answer. It didn't settle the argument. It drew a line.


## How Does the California Rent Cap Work Under AB 1482?

> **California Civ. Code § 1947.12 (Tenant Protection Act / AB 1482)** — For covered residential properties, rent increases are limited to 5% plus the percentage change in the regional Consumer Price Index (CPI), or 10% of the lowest rent charged in the prior 12 months — whichever is lower.

The formula is straightforward. Take your current rent. Add 5%. Add your county's CPI change. If the total exceeds 10%, it caps at 10%. That's the maximum your landlord can increase rent in a 12-month period.

For 2025–2026, regional CPI adjustments range from roughly 3.2% to 4.5% depending on the metro area. That means effective rent caps in most California cities are running between 8.2% and 9.5% — well below the 10% hard cap.

A few things the statute requires beyond the math:

- The cap applies per 12-month period. A landlord can't stack two 9% increases six months apart.
- The baseline is the *lowest rent charged* in the prior 12 months — not the current rent. If the landlord gave a temporary discount, the cap is calculated from the discounted amount.
- AB 1482 sunsets on January 1, 2030. Unless the legislature extends it, these protections expire.

## Which Properties Are Exempt From the Rent Cap?

AB 1482 is broad but not universal. The exemptions matter because they're where landlords most often overclaim.

| Property Type | Exempt? | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| Single-family homes owned by a natural person | Yes | Owner must not be a corporation, REIT, or LLC with a corporate member. Must provide written notice of exemption per Civ. Code § 1947.12(d)(5). |
| Condos owned by a natural person | Yes | Same conditions as single-family homes |
| Duplexes where owner occupies one unit | Yes | Owner must live in one unit as primary residence |
| Units built within the last 15 years | Yes | Rolling window — a 2011 building became covered in 2026 |
| Deed-restricted affordable housing | Yes | Already subject to separate regulatory rent limits |
| Dormitories at higher education institutions | Yes | — |
| Mobile homes / manufactured housing | Yes | Governed by the Mobilehome Residency Law instead |
| Properties with existing local rent control (pre-1995) | Depends | Local ordinance applies instead of AB 1482 if it's more restrictive |

The single-family home exemption has a catch that many landlords miss. The statute requires the owner to provide a specific written notice to the tenant *before* the exemption applies. The notice must include statutory language from Civ. Code § 1947.12(d)(5). If the landlord forgot to include this notice in the lease or deliver it separately, the property is covered by AB 1482 regardless of its type.

This is not a technicality. Courts have enforced it.

## How Does Local Rent Control Interact With AB 1482?

Roughly a dozen California cities have their own rent stabilization ordinances — most adopted decades before AB 1482. The interaction between state and local law creates a two-layer system.

| City | Local Rent Cap (approx. 2025–2026) | AB 1482 Cap | Which Applies? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Los Angeles | ~4% (RSO) | ~8–10% | Local (lower) for RSO units |
| San Francisco | ~3.6% | ~8–10% | Local (lower) for covered units |
| Oakland | ~3.5% (CPI-based) | ~8–10% | Local (lower) |
| Berkeley | ~3.5% | ~8–10% | Local (lower) |
| San Jose | 5% | ~8–10% | Local (lower or equal) |
| West Hollywood | ~4% | ~8–10% | Local (lower) |
| Beverly Hills | ~4% (CPI-based) | ~8–10% | Local (lower) |
| Unincorporated areas | None local | ~8–10% | AB 1482 only |

The rule: when both state and local law apply, the one more favorable to the tenant wins. In practice, that's almost always the local ordinance.

But local rent control ordinances have their own exemption lists — often narrower than AB 1482's. A unit exempt from AB 1482 might still be covered by local rent control, or vice versa. If you're in a rent-controlled city, check *both* layers.

## What Are the Notice Requirements for a Rent Increase?

> **California Civ. Code § 827** — A landlord must give at least 30 days' written notice for a rent increase of 10% or less (calculated over the prior 12 months). For increases exceeding 10%, at least 90 days' written notice is required.

The notice must be served properly — in person, by mail (with extra time for mailing), or posted on the door if the tenant is absent. A text message alone generally doesn't satisfy the requirement.

For month-to-month tenancies, the increase takes effect after the notice period expires. For fixed-term leases, the landlord generally cannot raise rent mid-lease — the increase takes effect at renewal.

## What Can a Tenant Do About an Illegal Rent Increase?

An increase above the legal cap is void under California law. The tenant is not obligated to pay the excess. But "void" doesn't mean the problem solves itself — it means the tenant has leverage, if they use it.

**Day 0:** You receive a rent increase notice that looks too high.

**Day 1–3:** Calculate the legal maximum. Pull your county's CPI from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Apply the formula: current rent × (1 + 5% + CPI%), capped at 10%. If you're in a local rent control jurisdiction, check their published allowable increase.

**Day 3–5:** Send a written response to your landlord. Cite Civ. Code § 1947.12 (AB 1482) and, if applicable, the local rent ordinance. State that you will pay the maximum legal increase and that the notice is void to the extent it exceeds the cap. Send via email and certified mail.

**Day 5–14:** If the landlord doesn't back down, send a formal demand letter. An attorney-signed letter citing the specific statute, the correct maximum, and the consequences of enforcement (tenant remedies, potential attorney's fees) resolves most of these disputes without litigation.

**Day 14–30:** If the landlord proceeds with the illegal increase — deducting from your security deposit, serving a pay-or-quit notice for the excess, or threatening eviction — those actions constitute retaliation under Civ. Code § 1942.5. Document them. They strengthen your position.

**Day 30+:** You can file a complaint with your local rent board (if one exists), file in small claims court to recover any excess rent paid, or — in cases involving willful misconduct — pursue civil penalties.

## What Happens When AB 1482 Expires in 2030?

AB 1482's rent cap and just-cause eviction protections are set to sunset on January 1, 2030. The legislature could extend the act, modify it, or let it expire. If it expires, tenants in cities without local rent control would lose statewide rent cap protections entirely.

Several ballot initiatives and legislative proposals are already in discussion for 2027–2028 to either make AB 1482 permanent or replace it with something broader. For now, the protections are in effect through December 31, 2029.

## The Cost Comparison: Fighting an Illegal Increase vs. Paying It

| Option | Cost | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Pay the illegal increase for 12 months | $1,200–$4,800+ in overpayment | Landlord keeps the excess; sets a higher baseline for next year's increase |
| Send a written demand yourself | $0 (your time) | Works sometimes; no legal weight behind it |
| Send an attorney demand letter | ~$199 flat fee | Resolves most disputes; creates a legal paper trail |
| File with local rent board | $0 | Effective in cities that have one; slower process |
| File in small claims court | $30–$75 filing fee | Can recover excess rent paid plus statutory damages |
| Hire an attorney for litigation | $2,000–$10,000+ | Nuclear option; warranted for repeated/willful violations with multiple tenants |

For most tenants, the demand letter is the right tool. It costs less than one month of the illegal overpayment and it forces the landlord to respond on the record. If they ignore it, the letter becomes evidence in court.

## The Bottom Line

California rent increase law gives tenants more protection than most states. But protection only works if you know the numbers and assert them. A landlord who sends a 15% increase to a tenant who doesn't know about AB 1482 gets away with it. A landlord who sends the same increase to a tenant with an attorney letter citing § 1947.12 does not.

The math is public. The cap is statutory. The remedies are real. The only variable is whether you use them.
