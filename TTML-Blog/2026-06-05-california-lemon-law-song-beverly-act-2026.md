If you bought or leased a vehicle in California that keeps breaking down despite repeated repair attempts, the state's Lemon Law may entitle you to a refund or a replacement vehicle. The law lives inside the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civil Code § 1790 and following), and it is one of the strongest consumer-protection statutes in the country — including a provision that makes the manufacturer pay your attorney's fees if you win.

**Short answer:** California's Lemon Law (the Song-Beverly Act) requires a manufacturer to refund or replace a vehicle it can't repair within a reasonable number of attempts. If your car still has a substantial defect covered by warranty, you likely have a claim.

This guide walks through what qualifies as a lemon, the repair-attempt rules that trigger the law, what remedies you can demand, and how to enforce your rights in 2026.

## What is the California Lemon Law?

The California Lemon Law is not a standalone statute with that name — it is part of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, codified at Civil Code § 1790 and following. The Act covers consumer goods sold with a warranty, and vehicles are its most litigated category. The core promise is simple: if a manufacturer or its authorized dealer cannot fix a warrantied defect after a reasonable number of attempts, the manufacturer must either replace the vehicle or refund what you paid.

The law applies to new vehicles bought or leased for personal, family, or household use, and in many cases to used vehicles still covered by the manufacturer's original or certified warranty. It covers the buyer's actual problems — drivetrain failures, electrical gremlins, braking issues, persistent warning lights — not cosmetic preferences.

## What qualifies a vehicle as a "lemon"?

A vehicle generally qualifies when it has a defect that substantially impairs its use, value, or safety, the defect is covered by warranty, and the manufacturer or dealer has been given a reasonable number of opportunities to repair it but has failed.

"Reasonable number of attempts" is the crux. The Song-Beverly Act includes a presumption — sometimes called the Tanner Consumer Protection Act provision (Civil Code § 1793.22) — that the threshold is met when, within the first 18 months or 18,000 miles, either: the manufacturer made four or more repair attempts for the same substantial defect; the vehicle was out of service for repair for a cumulative 30 or more days; or there were two or more repair attempts for a defect likely to cause death or serious injury.

Meeting the presumption makes your case stronger, but it is not the only way to win. Even outside the 18-month/18,000-mile window, you can prove the manufacturer had a reasonable number of attempts based on the facts. The presumption is a floor, not a ceiling.

## What remedies can I get — refund or replacement?

Under Civil Code § 1793.2(d), when a manufacturer can't conform the vehicle to warranty after a reasonable number of attempts, you can choose between a replacement and a refund.

A refund (often called a "buyback") generally includes the price you paid — down payment, monthly payments, and the payoff amount — plus collateral charges like sales tax, registration, and finance charges, and incidental costs like towing and rental cars. From that total, the manufacturer may subtract a "mileage offset" for your use of the vehicle before the first repair attempt for the defect. That offset is calculated using a statutory formula tied to the miles driven before the problem first arose, not your entire ownership.

A replacement means a substantially identical new vehicle, with the manufacturer covering the associated fees. You choose which remedy you prefer — the manufacturer cannot force a replacement on you when you want your money back, or vice versa.

## What is the "civil penalty" and why does it matter?

The Song-Beverly Act allows up to a civil penalty of two times your actual damages if the manufacturer's failure to honor the warranty was willful. This penalty is what gives the law its teeth. A manufacturer that ignores a clear lemon, drags out the process, or refuses an obvious buyback risks paying far more than the original refund. Combined with the attorney-fee provision, the penalty motivates manufacturers to settle legitimate claims rather than gamble at trial.

## Do I have to pay a lawyer out of pocket?

Usually not. Song-Beverly entitles a prevailing buyer to recover reasonable attorney's fees and costs from the manufacturer. Because of this fee-shifting rule, many lemon-law attorneys take cases on a contingency or fee-recovery basis, meaning the manufacturer — not you — ultimately pays the legal bill when you win. This structure is deliberate: the Legislature wanted consumers to be able to enforce their rights without the cost of a lawyer wiping out the value of the remedy.

## How do I start a Lemon Law claim in California?

Begin by building your record. Keep every repair order, even for visits where "no problem found" was noted — those still count as repair attempts. Note how many days the car spent at the shop. Save communications with the dealer and manufacturer.

Next, give the manufacturer formal notice. A written demand letter that identifies the vehicle, the defect, the repair history, and your demand for a buyback or replacement under the Song-Beverly Act puts the manufacturer on notice and starts the clock. Many claims resolve at this stage because the repair history speaks for itself.

If the manufacturer refuses or stalls, you can pursue the claim through the manufacturer's arbitration program (if one is certified and you choose to use it) or by filing a civil lawsuit. For smaller disputes, small claims court is an option, with an individual limit of $12,500 under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220 — though most full buyback claims exceed that and belong in civil court where the fee-shifting and civil-penalty provisions apply.

## What is the deadline to file?

Lemon-law claims are generally subject to a four-year statute of limitations tied to breach of warranty. The clock typically starts when the breach is discovered — often when repair attempts make clear the manufacturer can't fix the problem — rather than the purchase date. Because the timing analysis can be fact-specific, don't wait: the longer you drive a known lemon, the larger the mileage offset and the harder some arguments become.

## Does the Lemon Law cover used and leased vehicles?

Leased vehicles are covered, and lessees have similar refund and replacement rights. Used vehicles can qualify when they are still covered by the manufacturer's original warranty or a certified pre-owned warranty at the time the defects arise. Pure "as-is" private-party sales with no warranty generally fall outside Song-Beverly, though other consumer-protection theories may apply if the seller misrepresented the vehicle.

## How does the Lemon Law fit with other consumer remedies?

The Lemon Law is one tool among several. If the dealer concealed a prior accident or rolled-back odometer, you may also have claims under the Consumers Legal Remedies Act (Civil Code § 1750 and following) or the Unfair Competition Law (Business & Professions Code § 17200). If the problem is a general product defect rather than a vehicle, the same Song-Beverly warranty framework still applies — see our explainer on the [consumer warranty demand letter](/blog/2026-06-05-consumer-warranty-demand-letter-california). ## The bottom line

California's Lemon Law gives owners of defective vehicles real leverage: a refund or replacement, a possible civil penalty for willful conduct, and manufacturer-paid attorney's fees. The keys are documentation and timing. Keep every repair record, count the days out of service, and put the manufacturer on formal notice. A well-supported demand often resolves the matter before a lawsuit — and when it doesn't, the Song-Beverly Act is built to make enforcement worthwhile.
