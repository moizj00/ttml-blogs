**Short answer:** California subcontractors who aren't getting paid have three legal tools: the 20-day preliminary notice (required to preserve lien rights), the mechanics lien (a claim against the property itself), and the stop notice (freezes construction loan funds). Each has strict deadlines. Miss the preliminary notice within 20 days of starting work, and you lose your lien and stop notice rights entirely. A demand letter citing these tools often gets results before you need to use them.

## The payment problem that's unique to subcontractors

General contractors have a contract with the property owner. If they don't get paid, they sue the person who hired them. Subcontractors have a harder problem: they have a contract with the general contractor, not the owner. When the GC doesn't pay — whether from financial trouble, a dispute with the owner, or simple dishonesty — the sub has no direct contract to enforce against the person who actually owns the property.

California recognized this structural unfairness and built a system of statutory protections that gives subcontractors leverage they wouldn't otherwise have. The mechanics lien lets you put a claim on the property itself — the owner can't sell or refinance without dealing with it. The stop notice freezes construction loan funds before the GC can drain them. And the preliminary notice is the ticket that gets you into both systems.

These tools are powerful. They're also unforgiving about deadlines. One missed date, one procedural error, and the tool evaporates.

## Tool 1: The preliminary 20-day notice — your entry ticket

> **California Civ. Code § 8200** — A subcontractor, material supplier, or laborer who has not been paid must serve a preliminary notice on the property owner, the general contractor, and the construction lender (if any) within 20 days of first furnishing labor or materials. The notice preserves the right to file a mechanics lien and stop notice. Without it, those rights are lost.

The 20-day clock starts the first day you do any work or deliver any materials to the project. Not the day you signed the contract. Not the day you sent your first invoice. The day boots hit the ground.

Serve the preliminary notice on three parties: the property owner, the direct contractor (GC), and the construction lender. Use certified mail, registered mail, or personal delivery. The CSLB provides a standard form, or you can draft your own as long as it includes the required statutory information under § 8202.

What happens if you miss the 20-day window? You can still serve a late preliminary notice, but it only protects your lien rights for work done in the 20 days before you served it — not for work already performed. On a six-month project where you served the notice in month three, you've lost lien protection for five months of work. That's money you can never lien for.

**The practical rule:** Serve the preliminary notice on Day 1 of every new project, before you even start worrying about whether you'll get paid. It costs nothing (postage and a form), takes 15 minutes, and it's the single most important thing a sub can do to protect cash flow.

## Tool 2: The mechanics lien — a claim against the property

A mechanics lien is a security interest in the real property. When you record one, it attaches to the land and improvements. The owner can't sell, refinance, or get clear title until your lien is resolved. It is, by design, the single most powerful non-judicial collection tool in California construction law.

> **Civ. Code § 8412** — A mechanics lien must be recorded within 90 days after a notice of completion is filed by the owner, or within 60 days after the owner files a notice of cessation. If neither notice is filed, the deadline is 90 days after the project is actually completed.

The mechanics lien must include: the amount owed, a description of the work performed, the property address and legal description, and your preliminary notice information. File it with the county recorder's office where the property is located.

| Deadline Trigger | Lien Filing Deadline | Enforcement Suit Deadline |
|-----------------|---------------------|--------------------------|
| Notice of completion filed | 90 days from recording | 90 days from recording the lien |
| Notice of cessation filed | 60 days from recording | 90 days from recording the lien |
| No notice filed by owner | 90 days from actual completion | 90 days from recording the lien |

After recording the lien, you must file an enforcement action (lawsuit) within 90 days. If you don't, the lien expires and can be removed from the property. This deadline is absolute — no extensions.

One detail that trips up subcontractors: you must serve a copy of the recorded lien on the property owner within 10 days of recording under Civ. Code § 8416. Miss this service requirement and the lien is still valid, but you may be liable for the owner's attorney's fees to remove it.

## Tool 3: The stop notice — freezing construction loan funds

The stop notice is the tool most subcontractors don't know about, and it's often the most effective. If the project has a construction loan, you can serve a stop notice on the lender, and the lender must withhold enough money from future disbursements to cover your claim.

> **Civ. Code § 8502** — A claimant who has a lien right may give the construction lender a bonded or unbonded stop notice. The lender must withhold funds sufficient to pay the claim. An unbonded stop notice requires the lender to withhold funds; a bonded stop notice (backed by a surety bond equal to 125% of the claim) gives the claimant priority over other stop notices.

The stop notice hits harder than a mechanics lien in many cases because it freezes actual cash. The GC can't draw the next disbursement until your claim is resolved. That creates immediate pressure — the GC needs that money to keep the project moving, and the owner needs the project to move.

## How the three tools work together — the strategic sequence

The real power is in the combination. Each tool alone is good. Together, they're a three-layer collection system that leaves the GC with no room to stall.

**Day 1 of the project:** Serve the preliminary 20-day notice. This is not aggressive — it's standard practice. Professional subs serve it on every job regardless of their relationship with the GC.

**Day the invoice goes unpaid (usually net-30 or net-45):** Send a demand letter. Cite the overdue amount, the contract terms, and the fact that you have preserved your lien rights via the preliminary notice. The demand letter is the first signal that you're not going to wait quietly.

**Day 14 after the demand letter:** If no payment or payment plan, serve the stop notice on the construction lender (if there is one). The GC will hear about this within hours — lenders call immediately.

**Before the lien deadline:** Record the mechanics lien with the county recorder. This is your nuclear option — it clouds the title and forces resolution. Most disputes settle between the demand letter and the stop notice. The lien filing is for the cases that don't.

## California's prompt payment statute — another weapon

> **Bus. & Prof. Code § 7108.5** — A contractor who receives payment from the owner for work performed by a subcontractor must pay the subcontractor within 10 days of receiving the funds. Failure to pay within this window is grounds for license discipline.

This statute is the one GCs don't want you to know about. If the owner has paid the GC and the GC is sitting on your money, that's a licensing violation — not just a breach of contract. You can report it to the CSLB alongside your demand letter and bond claim. The threat of license discipline is often more motivating than the threat of a lawsuit.

## Common mistakes that cost subcontractors their rights

Missing the 20-day preliminary notice deadline is the biggest, but there are others. Filing the mechanics lien after the 90-day window. Serving the stop notice on the wrong entity (the owner instead of the lender). Recording a lien with the wrong property description. Failing to enforce the lien within 90 days of recording.

Each mistake is fatal to the specific tool it affects. And unlike most legal deadlines, these are not flexible — courts enforce them strictly because the lien system is statutory, not equitable. A judge cannot extend a missed mechanics lien deadline.

The best defense against procedural mistakes is to start the process early. Serve the preliminary notice on Day 1. Don't wait until you're already in a payment dispute to figure out deadlines.
