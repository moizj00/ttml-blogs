California tenants can sometimes respond to a landlord's failure to make serious repairs by using repair-and-deduct or, in limited circumstances, withholding rent — but only for genuine habitability problems and only after following specific steps. These are real legal remedies, not a free pass to stop paying rent over a cosmetic complaint, and using them incorrectly can expose a tenant to eviction.

**Short answer:** Yes, but narrowly. For serious habitability defects, California tenants may use repair-and-deduct (Civil Code § 1942) or limited rent withholding — only after proper notice and only for conditions that affect health and safety.

## What counts as a serious enough problem?

These remedies apply to breaches of the "implied warranty of habitability" — conditions that make the unit unsafe or unlivable, such as no heat, no hot water, serious plumbing failures, dangerous electrical problems, pest infestations, or structural hazards. A dripping faucet or chipped paint generally doesn't qualify. The defect must materially affect health and safety, and it usually can't be something the tenant caused.

## How does repair-and-deduct work?

Under Civil Code § 1942, a tenant may, after giving the landlord reasonable notice and time to fix a qualifying problem, pay to repair it and deduct the cost from rent. The limits are important:

- The cost generally can't exceed one month's rent.
- A tenant can use this remedy no more than twice in any 12-month period.
- The tenant must give the landlord reasonable notice and an opportunity to make the repair first.

It's best for discrete, fixable problems where the cost is clear.

## Is outright rent withholding allowed?

Rent withholding — paying nothing until serious defects are fixed — is recognized in California for substantial habitability breaches, but it's riskier than repair-and-deduct. A tenant who withholds rent over a problem a court later finds wasn't serious enough, or who didn't give proper notice, can end up facing a nonpayment eviction. Some tenants pay rent into escrow or set it aside rather than spend it, to show good faith. Because the stakes are high, this is the remedy where legal guidance matters most.

## What does proper notice look like?

In every case, the tenant should notify the landlord in writing, describe the problem specifically, and give a reasonable time to fix it before acting. That written notice is both a legal prerequisite and the tenant's protection if the dispute escalates. Keep copies and document the conditions with photos and dates.

## Where do landlords and tenants clash?

The friction is usually over whether the defect was serious, whether notice was adequate, and whether the landlord was given a fair chance to repair. Both sides are well served by a clear written record. A tenant with documented notice and a real habitability problem is on strong ground; a landlord who responded promptly to a vague or minor complaint is too. For the landlord-side view of repair demands, see [just-cause protections under AB 1482](/blog/2026-06-01-tenant-protection-act-ab-1482-just-cause-rent-cap-california-2026).

## Bottom line

California tenants aren't powerless when a landlord ignores serious repairs — repair-and-deduct and limited withholding exist for exactly that. But these remedies are narrow, notice-dependent, and reserved for real habitability problems. Used correctly and in writing, they protect the tenant; used loosely, they can backfire into an eviction. When the issue is serious and the landlord won't act, a documented written demand is the safer first move.
