Moving yourself or a close family member into a property you own is a legitimate reason to end a tenancy in California — but it's a regulated one. For tenancies covered by the Tenant Protection Act (AB 1482), an owner move-in is a "no-fault just cause," which means it's allowed only if you follow specific notice, good-faith, and relocation requirements. Treat it casually and the eviction can fail; local ordinances often add even stricter rules.

**Short answer:** Owner move-in is a no-fault just cause under AB 1482. You generally need a written notice stating the cause, a good-faith intent for the owner or a qualifying relative to occupy, and relocation assistance (often one month's rent). Local rules may be stricter.

## Is owner move-in a valid reason to evict?

Yes, for covered tenancies it's one of the recognized no-fault just causes. The key is that the move-in must be genuine. The owner — or, where the law permits, a spouse, domestic partner, child, parent, or grandparent — must actually intend to occupy the unit as their primary residence. Using "owner move-in" as a pretext to remove a tenant and then re-renting at a higher price is unlawful and can expose the landlord to significant liability.

## Who counts as a qualifying occupant?

Under AB 1482, the unit must be for occupancy by the owner or specified close family members. Some leases also require that the right to invoke owner move-in be reserved in the lease for tenancies that began after the law took effect. And critically, many **local ordinances narrow this further** — limiting which relatives qualify, requiring that no comparable vacant unit be available, or restricting how often an owner can use the cause. The local rule frequently controls.

## What notice is required?

For a no-fault just cause, you generally must serve a written termination notice that:

- States the just cause (owner or relative move-in).
- Provides the legally required notice period (commonly 60 days for tenants of a year or more).
- Complies with any local content and timing requirements.

The notice must be accurate and complete. A notice that omits the cause, understates the period, or ignores a local requirement is a frequent point of failure.

## What about relocation assistance?

No-fault just cause comes with a cost to the landlord: relocation assistance. Under AB 1482, that's typically **one month's rent**, provided either as a direct payment or as a waiver of the tenant's final month's rent, within the timeframe the statute requires. Local ordinances sometimes require more. Failing to provide required relocation assistance can invalidate the termination.

## The good-faith requirement and anti-pretext rules

The good-faith requirement is the heart of owner move-in law. The owner or relative must genuinely intend to move in — and usually to stay for a minimum period. If the unit is re-rented to someone else shortly after the tenant leaves, that's strong evidence the move-in was a pretext, and the displaced tenant may have claims for wrongful eviction, including damages. Document the genuine intent: who is moving in, the relationship, and the plan to occupy.

## How do local ordinances change things?

Cities with their own just-cause and rent-control laws — and many do — frequently impose stricter owner move-in rules: longer notice, higher relocation payments, limits on how many units an owner can recover, requirements that the owner own a certain percentage, or protections for elderly, disabled, or long-term tenants. Before serving any notice, confirm the rules for the specific city. Assuming the statewide standard when a stricter local one applies is a costly mistake.

## Practical steps for a lawful owner move-in

1. Confirm whether the tenancy is covered by AB 1482 or a stricter local ordinance.
2. Verify the intended occupant qualifies under the applicable law.
3. Prepare a written termination notice stating the cause and the correct notice period.
4. Calculate and provide the required relocation assistance on time.
5. Document the genuine intent to occupy and keep that record.
6. Never re-rent the unit in a way that suggests the move-in was pretextual.

## Bottom line

California lets you reclaim your property for yourself or a close relative — but only through a regulated, good-faith, no-fault just-cause process with proper notice and relocation assistance, and subject to whatever stricter local rules apply. Done correctly and documented, an owner move-in is straightforward. Done loosely or used as a pretext, it can turn into a wrongful-eviction claim.
