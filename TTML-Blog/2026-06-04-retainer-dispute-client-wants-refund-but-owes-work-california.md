If a California client demands their retainer back after you've already performed, you generally get to keep the portion you earned and may owe back only the unearned balance. The outcome turns on how your engagement defined the retainer and what work you can document. A blanket "give it all back" demand rarely reflects what the law actually requires.

**Short answer:** You usually keep the earned portion of a retainer and refund only what's unearned. Your engagement terms and a clear record of work performed decide the split.

## Earned vs. unearned: why the distinction matters

Most retainers are advance payments against work to be done. As you perform, you "earn" the fee; the unearned remainder is still, in effect, the client's money until you do the work. So when a client asks for a refund, the real question is how much you've earned, not whether they can change their mind. They usually can — but not retroactively for work already delivered.

A true non-refundable retainer (paid to secure your availability rather than for specific deliverables) is treated differently, but California scrutinizes "non-refundable" labels closely. Calling something non-refundable doesn't make it so if it was really a prepayment for services.

## What does your engagement letter say?

This is the whole ballgame. A clear agreement should state whether the retainer is a deposit against hourly or milestone work, how unused funds are handled, and what happens on early termination. If your terms spell out that fees are earned as work is performed and the unearned balance is refundable, you have a clean answer to give.

## How do you document what you earned?

Reconstruct the value you delivered: hours logged, milestones hit, files produced, meetings held. A simple reconciliation — retainer received, work performed and its value, balance refundable (if any) — turns an emotional dispute into arithmetic. Send it before the client escalates and you often defuse the whole thing.

## What if they're demanding the full amount back?

Respond with the reconciliation, not silence. If the client keeps pushing for money you've earned, that's a collection posture in reverse — you're defending earned fees rather than chasing an unpaid invoice. An attorney-signed letter that lays out your terms and the earned/unearned breakdown signals you won't simply refund work you've already done. For the broader playbook, see [how small agencies handle B2B collections in California](/blog/2026-06-04-b2b-collections-playbook-small-agencies-california-2026).

## Bottom line

A refund demand isn't a refund order. Keep what you earned, return what you didn't, and let your engagement terms plus a tidy reconciliation do the talking. Vague retainer agreements are where these disputes get expensive — so the fix for next time is a clearer engagement letter.
