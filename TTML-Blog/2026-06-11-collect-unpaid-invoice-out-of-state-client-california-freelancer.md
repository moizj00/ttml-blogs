The gig economy doesn't respect state borders. You're a California-based freelancer, your client is in Texas, and they owe you $7,500. You know you could sue them in California small claims — except you're not sure you can. The client never set foot in this state. Your contract doesn't have a forum-selection clause. And flying to Dallas to file in their local court costs more than the invoice.

**Short answer: California courts can exercise personal jurisdiction over an out-of-state client if they "purposefully directed" business activities toward California — for instance, by hiring a California-based freelancer and communicating regularly into the state. A demand letter works across state lines with no jurisdictional issues. If you need to litigate, your options include filing in California (if jurisdiction exists), filing in the client's state, or using a forum-selection clause in future contracts to prevent this problem entirely.**


## The Jurisdiction Problem, Explained Without the Jargon

You can't sue someone in a state that has no authority over them. That authority — "personal jurisdiction" — requires a connection between the defendant and the state where you're filing.

> **California Code of Civil Procedure § 410.10 (Long-Arm Statute)** — California's long-arm statute extends jurisdiction to the constitutional maximum: a court can exercise jurisdiction over any person or entity with sufficient "minimum contacts" with the state, consistent with due process under the Fourteenth Amendment.

For freelancer-client disputes, the analysis usually turns on these questions:

- Did the client reach into California to hire you? (Browsing your California-based portfolio, emailing you at a California address, negotiating terms with someone they knew was in California)
- Was there an ongoing business relationship with regular communication into California?
- Did the client benefit from work performed in California?

If yes to most of these, a California court likely has jurisdiction. One-off, minimal-contact engagements are closer to the line.

## Why a Demand Letter Is Your Best First Move Across State Lines

A demand letter has no jurisdictional requirements. An attorney in California can send a demand letter to a client in any state. The letter carries the same weight — legal letterhead, specific dollar amount, deadline, and the implicit promise of litigation.


For cross-border disputes, the demand letter is especially effective because it forces the client to calculate whether ignoring it is worth the risk. If your letter cites [SB 988's double-damages provision](/blog/california-freelance-worker-protection-act-sb-988-guide) and California's favorable freelancer protections, the out-of-state client now faces the prospect of defending a California lawsuit — which means either traveling here or hiring a California attorney. Most rational clients pay the invoice instead.

## The Enforcement Problem: Winning Is Not the Same as Collecting

Suppose you win a $7,500 judgment in California small claims against your New York client. You have a piece of paper from a California court. Your client's bank account, office, and assets are in New York. Now what?

You need to "domesticate" the judgment — register it in the client's home state so it becomes enforceable there. Under the Uniform Enforcement of Foreign Judgments Act (adopted by 47 states including California and New York), this is procedural, not adversarial. You file the California judgment with the clerk in the client's county, pay a filing fee, and it becomes enforceable as if it were a local judgment.

**Day 0:** Win judgment in California small claims.
**Day 14:** File domestication paperwork in client's state.
**Day 30:** Judgment is enforceable in client's state.
**Day 45:** Initiate wage garnishment or bank levy if client still hasn't paid.

The process costs $50–$200 in filing fees and doesn't require an attorney in most states.

## The Forum-Selection Clause: Preventing This Problem in Future Contracts

The simplest solution: put this in every contract:

"Any dispute arising under this agreement shall be resolved exclusively in the courts of [your county], California."

This single sentence eliminates the jurisdiction question for every future engagement. The client agreed to California jurisdiction when they signed. Courts routinely enforce these clauses under California law and federal law (see *M/S Bremen v. Zapata Off-Shore Co.*, 407 U.S. 1 (1972)).


Pair it with an attorney-fee shifting clause and a California choice-of-law clause ("This agreement shall be governed by California law"), and you've built a contract that makes it expensive and inconvenient for any out-of-state client to stiff you.

## The Bottom Line

An out-of-state client who owes you money hasn't escaped — they've just added a procedural step. A demand letter crosses state lines without any friction. If litigation becomes necessary, California's long-arm statute covers most freelancer-client relationships where the client actively hired into the state. And a judgment, once domesticated, is enforceable anywhere the client has assets.

The real fix is upstream: a forum-selection clause, a choice-of-law clause, and a milestone payment structure that limits your exposure before the relationship sours. For the invoice that's already overdue, a [flat-fee demand letter](/blog/how-much-does-demand-letter-cost-california-2026) from a California attorney is the first and most cost-effective escalation.
