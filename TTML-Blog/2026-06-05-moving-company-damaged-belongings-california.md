If a California moving company damaged your belongings or is refusing to deliver them until you pay more than the agreed price, you have rights. Household-goods movers in California are licensed and regulated by the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC), which prohibits "hostage loads" and sets rules for damage claims. A mover generally must deliver your goods once you pay the lawfully quoted charges.

**Short answer:** California movers are CPUC-regulated and cannot hold your goods hostage over surprise fees. You can demand delivery, file a CPUC complaint, and pursue the mover for damage or overcharges.

## Can a moving company legally hold my belongings?

Only for the charges allowed under its CPUC authorization and the estimate you agreed to. A licensed household-goods carrier (look for a "Cal-T" or "MTR" number) must deliver your property once you tender the lawful charges. Demanding sums far above the binding or not-to-exceed estimate as a condition of delivery is a hostage-load practice the CPUC prohibits. An unlicensed mover holding your goods may also be committing conversion.

## What can I do if the mover damaged my things?

California movers must offer a claims process and a level of valuation coverage. File a written damage claim promptly and document everything with photos and an itemized list of damaged or missing items. If the mover undervalues or ignores the claim, you can escalate to the CPUC and pursue the loss in small claims court (limit $12,500 for individuals under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220).

## How do I report an abusive mover?

File a complaint with the CPUC's Transportation Enforcement Branch, which licenses and disciplines household-goods carriers. The CPUC can investigate hostage-load demands, unlicensed operators, and overcharging.

For interstate moves, the federal Surface Transportation Board and FMCSA rules apply instead of the CPUC, but the core principle is the same: a mover cannot demand ransom-style payments beyond the agreed and lawful charges to release your property.

## Does a demand letter work against a moving company?

Often, yes — and quickly. Movers depend on their CPUC license and online reputation, so a letter that cites their regulatory obligations, the agreed estimate, and the prohibition on hostage loads frequently gets goods released or a damage claim paid. The letter should reference your binding or not-to-exceed estimate, the specific charges in dispute, and your intent to file a CPUC complaint and a small claims action.

## What if the mover was unlicensed?

Unlicensed movers create extra exposure for themselves. Operating as a household-goods carrier without CPUC authority is unlawful, and holding your property can be treated as conversion. You can report them to the CPUC and pursue civil recovery. Keep your contract, texts, and payment records — they establish what you agreed to versus what the mover later demanded.

## When should you escalate?

If your belongings are being withheld right now, move fast: send a written demand for delivery, file the CPUC complaint, and preserve your documentation. The combination of regulatory pressure and a credible threat of small claims usually resolves a moving dispute without a drawn-out fight.
