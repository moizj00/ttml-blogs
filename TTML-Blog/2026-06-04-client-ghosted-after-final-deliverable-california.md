When a client disappears after you've delivered the final deliverable, you're owed the money and you have leverage — you've already performed. In California the path is straightforward: document a short, firm follow-up sequence, then escalate to an attorney-signed demand letter before the silence hardens into a write-off.

**Short answer:** A ghosting client still owes you. Document two or three clear follow-ups, then send an attorney-signed demand letter citing the delivered work and the amount due.

## Why does delivering the work strengthen your position?

You completed your side of the bargain. Under an account-stated theory, an invoice that's gone undisputed for weeks is treated as an acknowledged debt, and the delivered files prove performance. A client who's avoiding you isn't disputing the work — they're hoping you'll give up. That makes your claim cleaner than a dispute over half-finished work.

## What should your follow-up sequence look like?

Keep it short, professional, and on the record:

1. A friendly nudge a few days after the due date ("just flagging invoice #X is past due").
2. A firmer reminder a week later stating the balance and the late terms.
3. A final notice giving a specific deadline and saying what happens next.

Send these by email so there's a timestamped trail. Resist long, emotional messages — brief and factual reads as someone who will follow through.

## When should you stop chasing and escalate?

Once a client has blown past a clear written deadline with no response, more reminders won't help. That's the moment an outside voice changes the dynamic. An [attorney-signed demand letter](/blog/2026-06-04-b2b-collections-playbook-small-agencies-california-2026) lands differently than your fourth email: it tells the client you've moved from hoping to enforcing.

## What goes in the demand?

The principal owed, the agreed terms, the date you delivered, any contractual late fee, and a firm deadline to pay. It should reference the evidence — the accepted proposal, the delivery, the ignored invoices — without arguing the whole case. The goal is to make paying easier than the alternative.

## How fast do you need to move?

You generally have years before the statute of limitations runs (four years for written contracts under CCP § 337, two for oral under § 339), so you're not in immediate danger. But debts get harder to collect as they age and clients move on, so escalate within weeks, not months.

## Bottom line

Ghosting is a tactic, not a defense. Document a tight follow-up sequence, then put an attorney's name on a demand. A client who ignored four of your emails tends to read the one that comes on legal letterhead.
