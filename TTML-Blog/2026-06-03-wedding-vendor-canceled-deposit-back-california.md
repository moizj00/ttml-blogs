# The Wedding Vendor Took the Deposit and Vanished. Getting It Back.

Six weeks out, the photographer stops answering. The caterer's voicemail is full. The deposit cleared months ago. And the date on the calendar isn't moving.

There's a particular dread in this one. The money stings. The bigger problem is a date that can't be rescheduled and a vendor who's stopped pretending to care.

## A deposit is a promise, and the law treats it like one

When you paid that deposit, you and the vendor struck a contract: money now, service on a set date. If they walk away — cancel without cause, go silent, fail to show — that's a breach. A breach puts the deposit, and often more, back in play.

You're not asking for a favor when you demand it back. You're enforcing a deal they broke.

## Move fast, and put it in writing

Timing matters more here than in most disputes, because the date is a cliff. The closer it gets, the stronger your claim to real damages: the replacement vendor you booked at a premium, the deposits you lost downstream, the scramble you never signed up for.

A demand letter does three things at once. It states the breach in plain terms. It demands the deposit back, plus any extra costs you can document, by a specific date. And it signals that you're keeping records, which is the last thing a vendor hoping you'll give up wants to see.

Keep every text, every email, every canceled-on-you timestamp. A screenshot of a dead phone line is evidence. So is the booking confirmation from whoever you scrambled to hire instead.

## If they still don't move

California small claims court hears cases up to $12,500 for an individual — no lawyer, a hearing in weeks. For most event deposits, that ceiling sits well above what you're owed. A demand letter usually settles it first. When it doesn't, the letter you already sent becomes the opening exhibit in a case you've been quietly building.
