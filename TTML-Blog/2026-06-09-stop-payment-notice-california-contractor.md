**Short answer:** A stop payment notice is a written demand sent to the person holding construction funds — usually the owner or a construction lender — directing them to withhold enough money to cover what the contractor owes you. In California, this right exists under Civil Code §§ 8530–8538 and doesn't require filing a court case first.

## Who can send one and when

Stop payment notices aren't just for property owners frustrated with a general contractor. The tool is designed primarily for *subcontractors and suppliers* who haven't been paid by the GC — but property owners have a parallel power: they can withhold funds from a contractor who isn't performing.

Here's where it gets interesting for homeowners: if you're making progress payments to a general contractor and you discover they're not paying their subs, you have both the right and arguably the *obligation* to withhold further payments. Why? Because under California's mechanic's lien laws, those unpaid subs can lien your property even though you already paid the GC.


## The two types: bonded vs. unbonded

| Feature | Unbonded Stop Notice | Bonded Stop Notice |
|---------|---------------------|-------------------|
| **Who receives it** | Property owner | Construction lender |
| **Bond required?** | No | Yes (125% of claim amount) |
| **Funds frozen** | Owner must withhold from next payment | Lender must set aside funds |
| **Deadline to send** | Before project completion + 30 days | Before project completion + 30 days |
| **Enforcement** | Lawsuit within 90 days of completion | Lawsuit within 6 months |

The unbonded version is the accessible one for most disputes. You don't need a bond, don't need a lawyer to draft it (though one helps), and it takes effect the moment the owner receives it.

## What a valid stop payment notice must contain

Civil Code § 8532 requires:

1. The claimant's name and address
2. A general description of the work or materials furnished
3. The name of the person who contracted for the work
4. A statement of the amount claimed
5. A statement that the claim is for work or materials provided to the project

> **Civ. Code § 8532** — A stop payment notice must be in writing and contain specified information identifying the claimant, the work, and the amount due.

Miss any of these elements and the notice is technically defective — though California courts have been lenient about minor omissions when the intent is clear.


## The practical sequence

**Day 1:** Send the stop payment notice via certified mail to the property owner (or lender, if bonded). Keep a copy.
**Day 2–10:** The owner is now legally obligated to withhold the claimed amount from the contractor's next draw.
**Day 10–30:** The contractor will likely contact you — either to dispute the claim or negotiate payment. This is exactly the leverage the statute is designed to create.
**Day 30+:** If no resolution, you have 90 days after project completion to file a lawsuit to enforce the withheld funds.

## Why this works better than a demand letter alone

A demand letter asks someone to pay. A stop payment notice *removes their ability to spend the money on something else*. The funds sit frozen — either with the owner or the lender — until the dispute resolves. That changes the incentive structure completely.

For homeowners dealing with a non-performing GC: combining a [demand letter citing specific code violations](/blog/contractor-walked-off-job-california) with a decision to withhold future progress payments gives you two forms of leverage simultaneously.

## When to get an attorney involved

If the amount exceeds $10,000, if the project involves a construction lender, or if the contractor responds with a release bond (forcing you into litigation), an attorney demand letter or legal filing becomes the next step. The stop payment notice itself costs nothing to send — but enforcing it may require counsel.
