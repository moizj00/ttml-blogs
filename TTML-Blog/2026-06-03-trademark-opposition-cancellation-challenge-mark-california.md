If someone is trying to register a trademark that conflicts with yours, a California business can file an "opposition" while the mark is still pending, or a "cancellation" after it has registered, before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) at the USPTO. Oppositions are authorized under the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1063, and cancellations under § 1064. These proceedings let you block or undo a registration without filing a full federal lawsuit — though they decide only the registration, not money damages. California's Unfair Competition Law, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200, may provide a parallel state claim.

**Short answer:** Oppose a pending mark during its 30-day publication window (extendable), or petition to cancel a registered mark on valid grounds like likelihood of confusion or abandonment. The TTAB decides registration rights; for damages or an injunction you'd go to federal court.

## What's the difference between opposition and cancellation?

The timing is what separates them. When the USPTO is ready to approve an application, it "publishes" the mark in the Official Gazette. That publication starts a 30-day window during which anyone who believes they'd be harmed can file an opposition to stop the registration. If you miss that window and the mark registers, your tool changes to a cancellation petition, which asks the TTAB to remove an already-registered mark.

Both are handled by the TTAB, an administrative tribunal inside the USPTO. They function somewhat like streamlined litigation — there are pleadings, discovery, evidence, and briefs — but they resolve only whether the mark should be registered. The TTAB cannot award you money or order the other party to stop using the mark in the marketplace; only a court can do that.

## Who has standing to challenge a mark?

You must have a real interest in the outcome — typically because the other mark would harm your own brand. A California business that already uses a similar name for similar goods, holds an earlier registration, or has a pending application of its own usually has standing. You don't need to be a direct competitor, but you do need a genuine stake; the TTAB won't entertain challenges from strangers with no connection to the mark.

This is one reason to monitor new applications in your field. Many businesses use watch services that alert them when a confusingly similar mark is published, so they can act inside the opposition window rather than facing the harder task of cancelling a registered mark later.

## What grounds can you use to oppose or cancel?

The most common ground is likelihood of confusion — the proposed or registered mark is so similar to yours, for related goods, that consumers would likely be confused about the source. If you have earlier rights (a prior registration or prior use), this is often your strongest argument.

Other grounds include that the mark is merely descriptive or generic; that it's been abandoned through non-use; that it was obtained by fraud on the USPTO (for example, a false claim of use); that it falsely suggests a connection with your business; or that it dilutes a famous mark. Importantly, some grounds are available to cancel a registration at any time (like abandonment or genericness), while others must be raised within the first five years after registration. After five years, a mark can become "incontestable," which narrows the grounds you can use — another reason not to sit on your rights.

## What does the TTAB process actually look like?

A TTAB proceeding begins with a notice of opposition or a petition to cancel, which lays out who you are, your standing, and your grounds. The other side files an answer. Then comes a discovery period, where both sides exchange documents, take depositions, and serve written questions — much like a court case, but governed by the TTAB's own rules.

After discovery, each side submits evidence and trial briefs. There's no live courtroom trial in the traditional sense; the case is decided "on the papers" by a panel of administrative trademark judges, with optional oral argument. The TTAB then issues a decision either sustaining or dismissing the challenge. A losing party can appeal to a federal court or the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit.

The process is more affordable than full federal litigation, but it is not quick or trivial — contested proceedings can run well over a year. That timeline is worth weighing against your goals before you file.

## When is a demand letter or negotiation better than a TTAB fight?

Many conflicts never need the TTAB. A well-drafted cease and desist or a coexistence negotiation can resolve a dispute faster and cheaper than a contested proceeding. If the other party is a small business that simply didn't realize the conflict, a letter may prompt them to abandon their application or agree to limit their goods so the two marks can coexist.

The TTAB makes the most sense when negotiation fails, when the other side won't budge, or when you need to clear a registered mark that's blocking your own application. ## The bottom line for California businesses

## What does it cost, and how do you keep costs down?

A contested TTAB proceeding is more affordable than full federal litigation but still involves real expense — pleadings, a discovery period, evidence, and briefing, often spread over a year or more. The single biggest cost driver is whether the case goes the distance or settles. That's why experienced practitioners try to resolve disputes early: a request for an extension of time to oppose, followed by negotiation, frequently produces a coexistence agreement or a voluntary abandonment without a full trial.

To keep costs down, act inside the opposition window so you're stopping a pending mark rather than cancelling a registered one, which is harder. Lead with a demand letter where appropriate. And go into any proceeding with your evidence already organized — your registration, your first-use date, and proof of the marketplace overlap that creates confusion. The better your file, the shorter and cheaper the fight tends to be.

## How does this compare with going straight to court?

The TTAB decides only registration; a federal court can also order the other side to stop using the mark in the marketplace and award damages. Sometimes the smarter move is to skip the TTAB and file in court — for example, when the other party is actively selling infringing goods and you need an injunction now, not a registration ruling in a year. Other times the TTAB is exactly right, because all you need is to clear a registration blocking your own application. Choosing between them is a strategic call that depends on what remedy you actually need, which is where a short consult with counsel pays off.

## The bottom line for California businesses

Challenging someone else's mark is a real and accessible option — you don't have to accept a conflicting registration as a fact of life. Watch for newly published marks so you can oppose within the window, know the grounds that fit your situation, and remember the five-year clock on certain cancellation arguments. And weigh the TTAB against a simple letter: sometimes the fastest path to protecting your brand is a clear demand, not a year-long proceeding. When the stakes are high or the issues are complex, an attorney can help you choose the most efficient route.
