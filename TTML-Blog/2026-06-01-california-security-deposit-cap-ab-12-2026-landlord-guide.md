California changed the rules on security deposits with Assembly Bill 12, which limits most residential security deposits to a single month's rent. Combined with the long-standing rules on returning deposits and itemizing deductions, this gives California one of the more tenant-protective deposit regimes in the country — and makes it easy for a landlord who hasn't kept up to slip into a violation. This guide covers the cap, the exception, the return timeline, and what you can and can't deduct.

**Short answer:** Under AB 12, most California landlords may collect no more than one month's rent as a security deposit. A narrow small-landlord exception allows up to two months. Deposits must be returned, itemized, within 21 days (Civil Code § 1950.5).

## What does AB 12 actually change?

Before AB 12, California allowed security deposits of up to two months' rent for an unfurnished unit (and up to three for furnished). AB 12 replaced that with a flat cap: the security deposit generally may not exceed **one month's rent**, regardless of whether the unit is furnished. The total deposit — however you label it (last month's rent, pet deposit, cleaning deposit) — is what counts toward the cap.

This is a structural change. A lease that tries to collect "first, last, and security" can quickly exceed one month's worth of deposit and run afoul of the cap, so landlords need to rethink move-in cost structures.

## Is there an exception for small landlords?

Yes — a limited one. A landlord who is a natural person (or an LLC whose members are all natural persons) and who owns no more than a small number of residential units (generally two properties with no more than four units total) may collect up to **two months' rent** as a deposit. There's an additional wrinkle: this exception generally doesn't apply if the prospective tenant is a service member, who is protected by the one-month cap regardless. Confirm the current statutory details before relying on the exception, and document that you qualify.

## How long do you have to return the deposit?

The 21-day rule under Civil Code § 1950.5 still governs and is where many disputes start. Within 21 days after the tenant moves out, the landlord must either:

- Return the full deposit, or
- Return the balance with an **itemized written statement** of any deductions, plus copies of receipts or invoices for repairs or cleaning over a threshold amount.

Miss the deadline or skip the itemization, and you can lose the right to keep deductions — and potentially face penalties for bad-faith retention.

## What can you lawfully deduct?

Civil Code § 1950.5 limits deductions to specific categories:

- **Unpaid rent.**
- **Cleaning** to return the unit to the condition it was in at move-in (less normal wear and tear).
- **Repair of damage** beyond ordinary wear and tear caused by the tenant.
- **Restoration or replacement** of personal property or appurtenances, where the lease allows, again excluding normal wear.

You cannot charge for ordinary wear and tear — faded paint, minor carpet wear, small nail holes — which is the single most common deduction dispute. Document the unit's condition at move-in and move-out (photos, a signed checklist) so any deduction is defensible.

## What about the new pre-move-out inspection and photos?

California law gives tenants the right to request an initial inspection before move-out so they can fix issues and avoid deductions, and recent updates emphasize photographic documentation of the unit's condition. Taking dated move-in and move-out photos protects both sides: it justifies legitimate deductions and shields you from claims that you withheld money unfairly. Build this into your turnover routine.

## What happens if you get it wrong?

A landlord who retains a deposit in bad faith can face statutory penalties on top of returning the money — California allows up to twice the deposit amount in damages for bad-faith retention in addition to the actual deposit. Given how mechanical the rules are (one-month cap, 21 days, itemize, no wear-and-tear charges), most violations are avoidable with a simple checklist. The cost of sloppiness, by contrast, is real.

## Practical checklist for landlords

- Cap the deposit at one month's rent (or confirm you qualify for the two-month small-landlord exception).
- Don't let combined move-in charges exceed the lawful deposit limit.
- Document move-in condition with a checklist and dated photos.
- Offer the pre-move-out inspection when requested.
- Return the deposit or itemized balance within 21 days, with receipts for larger items.
- Deduct only for unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear, and cleaning to original condition.

## Bottom line

AB 12 made the deposit math simpler and stricter: one month's rent for most landlords, two for qualifying small ones. Pair that with the 21-day itemized-return rule and the limits on deductions, and the path to compliance is clear — and so is the path to liability if you cut corners. A tidy move-in record and a calendar reminder for the 21-day deadline prevent the overwhelming majority of deposit disputes.
