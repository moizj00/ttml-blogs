In California, a landlord generally cannot enter a tenant's home whenever they please. Civil Code § 1954 requires advance written notice — usually 24 hours — and limits entry to specific, legitimate reasons. A tenant has a right to quiet enjoyment of their home, and a landlord who ignores the entry rules risks complaints, liability, and a damaged relationship.

**Short answer:** No. Under Civil Code § 1954, a California landlord must usually give at least 24 hours' written notice and enter only for permitted reasons, during normal business hours, except in genuine emergencies.

## What does Civil Code § 1954 actually require?

The statute allows a landlord to enter only for limited purposes, and (outside emergencies) only after giving the tenant reasonable written notice — defined as 24 hours by default — with entry during normal business hours. The notice should state the date, an approximate time, and the purpose.

Permitted reasons generally include:

- Emergencies (no notice required).
- To make necessary or agreed-upon repairs, improvements, or services.
- To show the unit to prospective tenants, buyers, lenders, or contractors.
- When the tenant has abandoned or surrendered the unit.
- Pursuant to a court order.

## When can a landlord enter without notice?

The main no-notice exception is a real emergency — a burst pipe, a fire, a gas leak — where waiting 24 hours would risk serious harm or property damage. "I was in the neighborhood" or "I wanted to check on things" is not an emergency. The other no-notice situations are narrow: the tenant is present and consents at the time, or the unit has genuinely been abandoned.

## Can a landlord show the unit on shorter notice?

For showings to prospective buyers, the law permits oral notice in some circumstances if the landlord has notified the tenant in writing within the prior 120 days that the property is for sale — but the entry must still be at a reasonable time, and the landlord should leave evidence of entry. Even then, 24 hours' written notice remains the safest practice.

## What can a tenant do about illegal entry?

A tenant facing repeated entries without notice has options: document each incident, send a written request that the landlord follow § 1954, and escalate if it continues. Persistent unlawful entry can support claims for breach of quiet enjoyment or harassment, and in serious cases California law (Civil Code § 1940.2) penalizes landlords who use unlawful entry to influence a tenant to vacate. A documented written demand often resolves it before it gets that far. For related boundary issues, see [just-cause and tenant protections under AB 1482](/blog/2026-06-01-tenant-protection-act-ab-1482-just-cause-rent-cap-california-2026).

## Bottom line

A California landlord's right to enter is real but limited: 24 hours' written notice, a permitted reason, and normal business hours — with emergencies as the narrow exception. For landlords, following § 1954 avoids liability; for tenants, it's the rule that protects your home. When entries cross the line, a clear written record is the first and often only step needed to stop them.
