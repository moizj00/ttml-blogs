If a client owes you money in California, the law gives you a limited window to enforce that debt in court. For most unpaid invoices, that window is four years when the agreement was in writing and two years when it was oral. Miss it, and a debt that's genuinely owed can become legally uncollectible — the client can raise the statute of limitations as a complete defense and walk away.

**Short answer:** In California, you generally have four years to sue on a written contract (CCP § 337) and two years on an oral one (CCP § 339). The clock usually starts when payment was due and missed.

Understanding this timeline matters even if you never plan to file a lawsuit, because the entire weight of a demand letter rests on the credible possibility that you *could*. Once the limitations period runs, that leverage evaporates. This guide walks through how long you have, when the clock starts, what can pause or reset it, and why waiting is almost always the wrong move.

## How long do you have to collect an unpaid invoice?

California sets different deadlines depending on the form of your agreement:

- **Written contracts — four years.** Code of Civil Procedure § 337 gives you four years from breach to sue on a written agreement. A signed contract, an accepted written proposal, or a Statement of Work the client approved in writing generally falls here.
- **Oral contracts — two years.** Code of Civil Procedure § 339 sets a two-year limit for agreements that were spoken rather than signed. Handshake deals and verbal "yes, go ahead" arrangements live here.
- **Open book accounts — four years.** For ongoing running accounts where you bill against a continuing relationship, the four-year book-account period under § 337a can apply, often measured from the last entry.

The practical takeaway: a written agreement nearly doubles your enforcement window. That alone is a reason to get even simple engagements into writing — and, after 2025, California's Freelance Worker Protection Act (SB 988) requires a written contract for freelance work of $250 or more, so the written-contract timeline increasingly applies by default.

## When does the clock actually start?

The limitations period generally begins on the date of the breach — for an unpaid invoice, that's typically the day payment was due and didn't arrive, not the day you delivered the work. If your invoice said "net 30," the clock usually starts 30 days after the invoice date.

This sounds simple but gets nuanced with ongoing relationships. If you did a series of jobs, each unpaid invoice may have its own start date. A project billed in milestones might generate several separate clocks. When in doubt, treat the earliest missed payment as your governing deadline and act well before it.

## What can pause or reset the clock?

A few events can change the timeline in your favor:

- **Partial payment.** If the client makes a partial payment on the debt, it can restart the limitations clock from the date of that payment. A client who pays a little to "show good faith" may have just reset your four-year window.
- **Written acknowledgment.** A new written promise to pay, or a clear written acknowledgment of the debt signed by the client, can also restart the period under Code of Civil Procedure § 360.
- **Tolling.** Certain circumstances — such as the defendant being out of state — can pause ("toll") the clock so that time doesn't count against you.

These rules cut both ways. Don't rely on them as a rescue plan; rely on them only to understand why a stale-looking debt might still be alive.

## Does sending a demand letter affect the statute of limitations?

This is a common misconception worth clearing up: sending a demand letter does **not** stop or extend the limitations clock. Only filing a lawsuit (or one of the reset events above) does that. A demand letter is a tool to get paid *before* litigation — it works by making the consequences concrete — but it doesn't buy you time against the statute. So if your deadline is approaching, a demand letter is the step that precedes filing, not a substitute for it.

That said, the vast majority of well-documented claims never need a lawsuit. A clear, attorney-signed demand often resolves the debt precisely because the client knows the clock favors you and the courthouse is a realistic next step. If you're early in the timeline, you have room to escalate methodically; if you're late, speed matters.

## Why you shouldn't wait, even with years on the clock

Having four years doesn't mean using four years. Several forces work against a debt as it ages:

- **Evidence decays.** Emails get buried, team members leave, and memories fade. The crisp paper trail you have today is harder to assemble in year three.
- **Clients disappear or fold.** Businesses close, freelancers' clients move on, and a solvent client today may be judgment-proof later. Collecting is easier while the client still exists and has money.
- **Leverage fades.** A debt you chase promptly signals you take payment seriously. A debt you let sit for two years signals the opposite, and clients behave accordingly.

The strategic move is to escalate within weeks of a clear default — not because the law forces you to, but because every month of delay erodes both your evidence and your leverage.

## How this fits your collection strategy

Think of the statute of limitations as the outer boundary of a process you should be running much faster. A typical sequence: invoice with clear terms, firm reminders once payment is past due, then an attorney-signed demand letter when reminders fail. If the demand doesn't produce payment and the amount justifies it, small claims court or a civil filing remains available — as long as you're inside the window.

Knowing your deadline also helps you triage. A six-month-old written-contract debt has years of runway and can be worked patiently. A nearly-two-year-old oral debt needs immediate attention. Map each unpaid invoice to its start date and its deadline, and you'll know which ones to push first. ## Bottom line

In California you generally have four years to enforce a written-contract debt and two years for an oral one, measured from the missed payment. Partial payments and written acknowledgments can reset the clock; demand letters do not. But the deadline is a backstop, not a plan — collect while your evidence is fresh, your client is solvent, and your leverage is real. The best time to act on an unpaid invoice is the month it goes past due, not the year it nearly expires.
