**Short answer: No. Under California's Tenant Protection Act (AB 1482), most landlords cannot raise rent more than 5% plus the local rate of inflation — capped at 10% total — in any 12-month period. Some properties are exempt. If your landlord exceeded the cap, you can push back with a written demand.**

The question lands in every California renter's inbox eventually. Your lease renewal arrives, the number is higher, and you wonder: is this even legal?

The answer depends on one statute and one exemption list.


## What the Law Actually Says

> **California Civ. Code § 1947.12 (AB 1482)** — A landlord of covered residential property may not increase rent more than 5% plus the percentage change in CPI for the region, or 10%, whichever is lower, within any 12-month period.

That's the ceiling. Your landlord doesn't get to pick a number and hope you don't Google it.

The CPI component varies by county. In Los Angeles County, the 2025 CPI adjustment was about 3.6%, making the effective cap around 8.6%. In San Francisco, it ran closer to 4.1%, capping at 9.1%. The statewide backstop: even if inflation somehow spikes, the increase can never exceed 10%.

## Which Properties Are Covered

AB 1482 covers most rental housing in California built more than 15 years ago. That rolling window means a unit built in 2011 became covered in 2026.

But some units are exempt:

| Property Type | Covered by AB 1482? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Apartments built before 2011 | Yes | Rolling 15-year window |
| Single-family homes (owner not a corporation) | Exempt | Only if owner gave written notice of exemption |
| Duplexes where owner occupies one unit | Exempt | Owner must live there as primary residence |
| Units built within the last 15 years | Exempt | Becomes covered once the building ages past 15 years |
| Deed-restricted affordable housing | Exempt | Already subject to separate rent limits |
| Mobile homes | Exempt | Covered by the Mobilehome Residency Law instead |

The single-family home exemption trips people up. It only applies if the owner is a natural person (not an LLC or corporation) *and* they included the required statutory notice in the lease. No notice? The cap applies anyway.

## What Happens if Your Landlord Exceeds the Cap

A rent increase above the legal limit is void. You are not required to pay the excess. Here's the sequence:

**Day 0:** You receive a rent increase notice that exceeds the cap.

**Day 1–3:** Calculate the maximum legal increase. Pull your county's CPI from the Bureau of Labor Statistics or your city's rent board website. Multiply your current rent by (5% + CPI%), capped at 10%.

**Day 3–7:** Send your landlord a written response citing Civ. Code § 1947.12. State the maximum lawful increase and that you will pay that amount — not the inflated figure. Keep a copy.

**Day 7–14:** If the landlord insists on the higher amount, send a formal demand letter. An attorney-signed letter carries more weight here because it signals you know the statute and aren't bluffing.

**Day 30+:** If the landlord retaliates — serves a notice to quit, cuts services, or threatens eviction — that's a separate violation under Civ. Code § 1942.5 (anti-retaliation). Document everything.

## Local Rent Control Adds Another Layer

Some California cities — Los Angeles, San Francisco, Oakland, Berkeley, San Jose, West Hollywood, and others — have their own rent stabilization ordinances with caps *lower* than AB 1482. If you live in one of these cities, the local cap wins. LA's RSO limits increases to about 4% in most years. San Francisco's 2025 allowable increase was 3.6%.

AB 1482 is the floor, not the ceiling, of tenant protection. Check your city's rent board.

## The Bottom Line

Your landlord cannot raise your rent by any amount they want. The math is public, the cap is statutory, and an increase above it is void.
