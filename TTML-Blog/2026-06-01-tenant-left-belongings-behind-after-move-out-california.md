When a former tenant leaves belongings behind in California, you can't just throw them out — even though they're gone and you want the unit back. State law treats abandoned personal property as something you must handle through a notice-and-wait process before disposing of or selling it. Skip the steps, and you can turn a minor nuisance into a liability claim.

**Short answer:** Don't trash it. California requires written notice to the former tenant describing the property and giving a deadline to claim it before you may sell or dispose of it (Civil Code §§ 1965, 1980–1991).

## Why can't you just throw it away?

The tenant's lease may be over, but their ownership of their possessions isn't. Disposing of someone's property without following the statutory process exposes you to a claim for the value of what you discarded. California built a specific procedure precisely so landlords have a lawful path — use it and you're protected; ignore it and you're exposed.

## What's the required process?

The framework in Civil Code §§ 1980–1991 generally works like this:

1. **Send written notice of abandoned property.** Describe the items with enough detail that the tenant knows what you have, state where they can be claimed, and give a deadline (commonly at least 15–18 days depending on how notice is delivered).
2. **Store the property safely** in the meantime — you can't damage or dispose of it during the waiting period.
3. **If unclaimed by the deadline,** your options depend on the estimated value: lower-value property may be kept or disposed of, while property over the statutory threshold generally must be sold at public auction with the proceeds handled as the statute directs.

The exact dollar thresholds and timelines are set by statute and can change, so confirm the current figures before acting.

## What about the security deposit?

Handle the deposit on its own track under Civil Code § 1950.5 — the 21-day itemization clock runs regardless of the abandoned property. Don't try to fold the cost of storing or removing belongings into deposit math unless it properly qualifies as a deduction; mixing the two is a common way to create a deposit dispute on top of everything else.

## Can you charge for storage?

You can generally recover reasonable storage costs from the proceeds or from the tenant when they claim the items, but you can't hold the property hostage in a way that conflicts with the statute. Keep your costs reasonable and documented.

## When does this become a legal dispute?

Most abandoned-property situations resolve quietly: you send notice, the tenant either claims the items or doesn't, and you proceed. It becomes contentious when a tenant later claims you destroyed valuable property without notice. That's why the written notice and a dated inventory (photos help) are your protection. If a former tenant escalates, a clear record that you followed the statute is your best answer. For the related deposit timeline, see [returning a security deposit in California](/blog/2026-06-01-california-security-deposit-cap-ab-12-2026-landlord-guide).

## Bottom line

Abandoned belongings aren't yours to discard on sight. Send the statutory notice, store the property during the waiting period, and dispose of or sell it only after the deadline passes — documenting each step. Following the process turns a headache into a clean, low-risk close-out instead of a lawsuit waiting to happen.
