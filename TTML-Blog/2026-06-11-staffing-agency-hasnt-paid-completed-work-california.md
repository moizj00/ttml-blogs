You worked the hours, the agency billed the client, and somewhere between those two events your paycheck disappeared. Staffing agencies in California aren't some gray area — they're your employer of record, and they owe you every protection that comes with that label.

**Short answer: California Labor Code §§ 201–204 require staffing agencies to pay you on time regardless of whether the end client has paid them. If they don't, you can file a wage claim with the Division of Labor Standards Enforcement (DLSE), send a demand letter, or sue for the wages plus waiting-time penalties of up to 30 days' additional pay under Labor Code § 203.**


## Why "The Client Hasn't Paid Us Yet" Is Not a Legal Defense

Staffing agencies love this excuse. It doesn't work. Under California law, the agency is your employer. Their cash flow problem with their client is their problem — not yours.

> **California Labor Code § 204** — Wages earned between the 1st and 15th of the month must be paid by the 26th. Wages earned between the 16th and the last day of the month must be paid by the 10th of the following month. Staffing agencies must meet these same deadlines.

If you were classified as an employee (W-2), these rules apply absolutely. If you were classified as an independent contractor (1099), the payment terms in your contract govern — but California's strict ABC test under AB 5 means many staffing-agency workers are misclassified, which gives you additional claims.

## The Waiting-Time Penalty: Where It Gets Expensive for Them

**Day 1 (after pay is due):** One day's wages accrues as a penalty.
**Day 7:** Seven days' wages in penalties.
**Day 14:** Fourteen days' wages.
**Day 30:** The cap — 30 days of your daily wages, added on top of what they already owe.

For someone earning $300/day, that's $9,000 in penalties alone. The DLSE routinely awards these. Staffing agencies that delay payment are gambling with real money.


## Your Three Best Moves

| Option | Cost | Timeline | Best When |
|--------|------|----------|-----------|
| DLSE wage claim | Free | 3–6 months (Berman hearing) | You want government enforcement behind you |
| Attorney demand letter | $199–$500 | 2–4 weeks | The agency is solvent and wants to avoid penalties |
| Small claims court | $30–$75 | 30–70 days | Owed under $12,500 and want faster resolution |

Start with the [demand letter](/blog/demand-letter-for-unpaid-wages-california). Name the Labor Code sections, calculate the waiting-time penalties accruing daily, and set a 14-day deadline. Agencies that ignore a freelancer's email tend to respond differently to a letter citing § 203 penalties by name. If they don't, the DLSE complaint costs nothing and the state does the enforcement.

## The Joint Employer Angle

Under SB 588, if the staffing agency can't or won't pay, you may be able to hold the client company jointly liable. The end client isn't always off the hook just because they contracted through an agency. This is especially relevant when the agency is small, undercapitalized, or has a pattern of late payments.

An attorney can assess whether the joint-employer theory applies to your situation. For most freelancers, the demand letter to the staffing agency resolves it — the penalties make settling cheaper than fighting.
