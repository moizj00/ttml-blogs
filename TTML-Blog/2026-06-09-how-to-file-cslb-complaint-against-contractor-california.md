**Short answer:** You can file a complaint with the Contractors State License Board (CSLB) online or by mail, for free, against any licensed or unlicensed contractor in California. The CSLB investigates, and penalties range from a warning to license revocation — but the process takes 6-12 months and doesn't directly recover your money. For faster financial recovery, a demand letter or small claims filing often works better.

## What the CSLB actually does when you file

Most homeowners assume the CSLB is a money-recovery agency. It isn't. The Board is a licensing regulator — it disciplines contractors, not compensate victims. That distinction matters because it shapes your entire strategy.

When you submit a complaint, a CSLB investigator reviews the facts and decides whether the contractor violated the Business and Professions Code. If the violation is serious enough, the Board can suspend or revoke the license, issue a citation with a fine, or order restitution as a condition of keeping the license. That last one — restitution — is the closest the CSLB gets to getting your money back, and it's not guaranteed.

> **California Bus. & Prof. Code § 7028** — Contracting without a valid license is a misdemeanor punishable by up to six months in jail and a fine of up to $5,000 for a first offense. For any project over $500 in labor and materials, a valid license is required.

## How to file step by step

The process is simpler than most people expect:

**Day 0:** Go to the CSLB website (cslb.ca.gov) and click "File a Complaint." You can also call 800-321-CSLB (2752) or mail the form to the Sacramento office. Online is fastest.

**Day 1-3:** Gather your documentation. The CSLB wants your contract (or proof there wasn't one), receipts and cancelled checks, photos of defective work, and any written communication with the contractor. The more specific your evidence, the faster the investigation moves.

**Day 7-14:** You'll receive a written acknowledgment. A case number is assigned and an investigator contacts you for details.

**Month 2-6:** The investigator contacts the contractor, inspects the work if needed, and builds the case. This is the slow part. The CSLB handles thousands of complaints per year, and staffing constraints are real.

**Month 6-12:** Resolution — citation, license action, or closure if evidence is insufficient.

| Path | Timeline | Gets Your Money Back? | Punishes the Contractor? |
|------|----------|-----------------------|--------------------------|
| CSLB complaint | 6-12 months | Maybe (restitution order) | Yes — license at risk |
| Demand letter | 2-4 weeks | Often (threat of lawsuit) | No |
| Small claims court | 2-3 months | Yes (if you win judgment) | No |
| Bond claim | 60-90 days | Yes (up to $25,000) | Indirectly |

## When a CSLB complaint is the right move — and when it isn't

File the complaint when the contractor is doing harm to other people. Unlicensed work, dangerous electrical or plumbing, systematic fraud — those are enforcement problems, and the CSLB is the right agency. Your complaint protects the next homeowner.

But if your primary goal is recovering money for incomplete or defective work, the CSLB is the slow lane. A formal demand letter from an attorney — citing the specific Bus. & Prof. Code violations and the threat of a bond claim — puts financial pressure on the contractor in weeks, not months. Most contractors respond when they see their license or bond is at stake.

The sharpest play is both: file the CSLB complaint for the public record, and send a demand letter for the money. The complaint strengthens your legal position if you end up in small claims, and the demand letter gets the conversation moving while the CSLB investigation grinds forward.

> **Bus. & Prof. Code § 7159(d)** — Home improvement contracts over $500 must be in writing, include a detailed scope of work, and give the homeowner a 3-day right to cancel. A contractor who skipped the written contract already has a Code violation — that's leverage in your demand letter.

## What if the contractor is unlicensed?

Unlicensed contractors cannot sue you to collect payment for their work. Under Bus. & Prof. Code § 7031(a), a person who performs work requiring a license without holding one "shall not maintain any action" to collect compensation. You may be able to recover all money paid under § 7031(b) — a powerful disgorgement remedy that goes beyond just the disputed amount.

The CSLB can investigate unlicensed contractors and refer the case for criminal prosecution. File the complaint, but also talk to an attorney about the § 7031(b) disgorgement claim — it could mean getting every dollar back.
