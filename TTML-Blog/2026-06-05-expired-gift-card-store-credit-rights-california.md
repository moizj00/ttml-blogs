In California, most gift cards and gift certificates cannot legally expire, and a business that sold you one generally must honor it indefinitely. Under Civil Code § 1749.5, it is unlawful to sell a gift certificate that contains an expiration date (with narrow exceptions), and any remaining balance under $10 can be redeemed for cash.

**Short answer:** California bans expiration dates on most gift cards. If a store says your card or credit "expired," that refusal is likely unlawful under Civil Code § 1749.5, and you can demand the value or a cash redemption for small balances.

## Can a gift card expire in California?

Generally, no. Civil Code § 1749.5 makes it unlawful to issue a gift certificate that has an expiration date or that loses value through dormancy or service fees. The card holds its value until it is redeemed. There are limited exceptions — for example, certain promotional cards given away for free, or cards usable across multiple unaffiliated sellers — but a card you *paid* for almost always cannot expire.

## What is the cash-back rule for small balances?

If your gift certificate has a remaining balance of less than $10, California law lets you require the business to redeem it for cash. This prevents merchants from pocketing small leftover amounts that customers rarely spend. The business cannot force you to keep spending until the card is exhausted if the balance has dropped below the threshold.

## What if the business refuses to honor the card?

First, point the business to Civil Code § 1749.5 in writing and ask it to honor the full value. Many refusals come from front-line staff who don't know the law, and a written request to a manager resolves it.

If the business still refuses, you can escalate. A refusal to honor a valid gift certificate can support a claim under the Unfair Competition Law (Business & Professions Code § 17200) and the Consumers Legal Remedies Act (Civil Code § 1750 and following). A short demand letter that cites § 1749.5 usually prompts a quick reversal, because the statute is unambiguous and the penalties for deceptive practices add up.

## Does store credit count as a gift certificate?

Often, yes. Store credit issued in place of a refund frequently falls within the same protections, because it functions as prepaid value you are entitled to redeem. The label a business uses ("store credit," "merchandise credit," "e-gift") does not let it impose an unlawful expiration date on value you already paid for or earned through a return.

## How do I enforce my rights if the amount is small?

For low-dollar disputes, small claims court is the practical venue — the individual limit is $12,500 under Code of Civil Procedure § 116.220, far more than any gift card. But you rarely need to file. Because the statute is clear, a documented demand referencing Civil Code § 1749.5 is usually enough. For guidance on choosing the right escalation path, see our overview of [where to file a consumer complaint in California](/blog/2026-06-05-where-to-file-consumer-complaint-california-2026).

## When should you send a demand letter?

If a business is sitting on your money behind a bogus expiration date, a firm letter citing the exact statute signals you know your rights and are prepared to escalate to the courts or a consumer-protection complaint.
