**Short answer:** A California contractor who walks off your job without legal cause has likely committed abandonment under Bus. & Prof. Code § 7107. You can demand completion or a refund via a formal letter, file a CSLB complaint, claim against their $25,000 license bond, or sue in small claims court — and you can do all four simultaneously.

## The first 72 hours matter more than you think

Most homeowners freeze when a contractor disappears. They call, text, leave voicemails. A week passes. Then two. By the time they act, they've lost leverage they didn't know they had.

Here's why speed matters: California's contractor license bond — $25,000 sitting in a surety company's account with your contractor's name on it — has a filing deadline. If the owner files a Notice of Completion, you have just 15 days to make a written claim. No Notice of Completion? You still only get 75 days from when work stopped.


Miss that window and the bond money is gone — not to you, anyway.

## Your four legal tools (use them in parallel, not sequence)

| Tool | Cost | Timeline | What it gets you |
|------|------|----------|------------------|
| Attorney demand letter | $199–$580 | Response in 10–14 days | Refund or completion; creates paper trail for everything below |
| CSLB complaint | Free | 6–12 months investigation | License discipline; sometimes triggers refund to avoid revocation |
| Bond claim (surety) | Free to file | 30–90 days | Up to $25,000 recovery directly from bonding company |
| Small claims court | $75–$100 filing fee | 30–70 days to hearing | Judgment up to $12,500 (individuals) |

The demand letter isn't just step one — it's the evidence foundation. Every other tool works better when you've already put the contractor on written notice with a specific dollar amount and deadline.

## The statute that matters

> **Bus. & Prof. Code § 7109** — Departure from accepted trade standards for good and workmanlike construction, including abandonment of a project, constitutes a cause for disciplinary action against a contractor's license.

Abandonment also triggers § 7107 (diversion of funds if they took payment for work they didn't complete) and potentially § 7159 (home improvement contract violations if they exceeded the deposit cap or failed to provide required notices).

## What counts as "abandonment" vs. a legitimate stop

Not every work stoppage is abandonment. A contractor can legally pause if:
- You failed to make a scheduled progress payment
- A permit inspection requires corrections before proceeding
- You directed them to stop while you rethink the scope
- Weather or material delays make continuation temporarily impossible

Abandonment means they stopped, you've communicated willingness to proceed, and they've refused or gone silent for an unreasonable period. California courts have found 2–3 weeks of silence after demand sufficient to establish abandonment.


## The demand letter: what to include

Your letter should state: the contract date, total paid to date, work completed vs. work remaining, a specific dollar amount demanded (refund of overpayment based on percentage complete), a deadline (10–14 days is standard), and a clear statement that you'll pursue bond claims, CSLB complaints, and legal action if they don't respond.

An attorney-signed letter on law firm letterhead converts the conversation from "angry homeowner venting" to "documented legal claim with consequences." That shift matters — particularly because the bond company and CSLB both want to see written notice as part of your paper trail.
