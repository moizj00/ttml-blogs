If an Instagram account is using your brand name, logo, or product photos to sell knockoffs or impersonate your business, you can send a cease and desist letter demanding they stop, and you can simultaneously file a trademark or copyright report through Instagram's built-in IP form. Trademark infringement on social media is governed by the same federal law as anywhere else — the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1114 (infringement) and § 1125(a) (false association) — plus California's Unfair Competition Law, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200.

**Short answer:** Send a cease and desist letter identifying your mark and the infringing posts, and file a report through Instagram's IP reporting form at the same time. The platform report often gets the content removed in days; the letter creates a legal record and deters repeat behavior.

## What should the cease and desist letter include?

Identify your trademark (and the registration number if you have one), point to the exact infringing account and posts, explain the likelihood of consumer confusion, and demand the account stop using your mark by a specific date. Keep the tone firm and factual. An attorney-signed letter signals you're prepared to escalate, which moves copycats far more reliably than a message from a personal account.

## How does Instagram's IP report work alongside the letter?

Instagram (Meta) has a dedicated trademark and copyright reporting process. A trademark report targets misuse of your brand name or logo; a copyright report targets stolen photos or videos. Filing as the rights holder triggers Meta's review, which can disable the post or account. Because the platform acts quickly, many California sellers report first to stop the bleeding, then send the letter to address damages and prevent the seller from simply moving to a new handle.

## What if the copycat ignores both?

Persistent infringers can be pursued for an injunction and damages under the Lanham Act and Section 17200. Before suing, most California businesses send a final demand. ## How do you stop the copycat from just opening a new account?

This is the frustration most California sellers run into: you report the account, Instagram disables it, and a week later the same person is back under a slightly different handle. Platform reports treat the symptom (the post), while a cease and desist targets the person behind it. That's why pairing the two works better than either alone — the report removes the content now, and the letter creates legal accountability that follows the seller wherever they go.

To make repeat behavior harder, document each new account as it appears, keep your screenshots and report confirmations, and reference the pattern in your letter and in future platform reports. A demonstrated history of willful, repeated infringement strengthens any later claim for an injunction or enhanced damages under the Lanham Act, and it signals to Meta that the account is a serial offender rather than a one-time mistake.

## What does it cost to send a letter versus litigate?

A flat-fee attorney cease and desist letter is dramatically cheaper than a lawsuit, and for the vast majority of social-media copycats, the letter plus a platform report is the entire solution. Litigation only becomes worth considering when the infringer is causing real, quantifiable damage and can actually be identified and served. Start with the cheapest effective tool and escalate only if the copycat forces you to.
