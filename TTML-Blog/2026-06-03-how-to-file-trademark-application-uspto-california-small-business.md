To register a federal trademark, a California small business files an application with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) under the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1051, selects the correct class of goods or services, pays the per-class fee, and responds to any "Office Action" the examining attorney issues. The whole process commonly takes many months to over a year, but it gives you nationwide rights, a public record of ownership, and the ability to enroll in programs like Amazon Brand Registry. This guide covers each step so you know what to expect.

**Short answer:** Run a clearance search, pick the right filing basis and class, file through the USPTO's online system, respond to any Office Actions, and then maintain the registration with periodic filings. DIY is possible for distinctive marks; complex cases benefit from a lawyer.

## Why register federally instead of just relying on common-law rights?

In California, you gain some "common-law" trademark rights simply by using a brand in commerce, and you can also register at the state level through the California Secretary of State under Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 14200 et seq. But common-law and state rights are geographically limited. A federal registration under the Lanham Act gives you a legal presumption of ownership and the exclusive right to use the mark nationwide for your goods or services.

For an ecommerce business selling to customers across the country, that nationwide scope is the whole point. Federal registration also unlocks practical tools: you can record the mark with U.S. Customs to block counterfeit imports, enroll in Amazon Brand Registry to police your listings, and use the ® symbol. It strengthens every enforcement letter you'll ever send.

## Step one: how do you run a clearance search?

Before you invest in a brand, make sure it's available. Start with a free "knockout" search on the USPTO's public trademark database, looking for identical and similar marks in your category. Don't just search the exact spelling — search phonetic equivalents, alternate spellings, and designs that could be confused with yours.

A clearance search protects you two ways. It tells you whether your application is likely to be refused because of an existing mark, and it warns you whether you might be infringing someone else's rights by adopting the name at all. A comprehensive search (often done by an attorney or search firm) goes beyond the federal register to include state registrations and common-law uses. Skipping this step is the single most common reason DIY applications fail.

## Step two: what filing basis and class do you choose?

You must tell the USPTO why you're entitled to register. The two most common bases are "use in commerce" (you're already selling under the mark) and "intent to use" (you have a bona fide intent to use it soon). Intent-to-use lets you stake a claim before launch, but you'll eventually have to prove actual use before the mark registers.

You also select one or more "classes" describing your goods or services, drawn from the international classification system. Each class carries its own fee. Choosing the wrong class — or describing your goods too broadly or too narrowly — is a frequent and expensive mistake, because you generally can't expand the application later. Match the description precisely to what you actually sell.

## Step three: how do you actually file the application?

You file through the USPTO's online Trademark Electronic Application System. The application asks for the owner's name and address, the mark itself (a word, a logo, or both), the goods/services description and class, your filing basis, and — if you're claiming current use — a specimen showing the mark as actually used in commerce, such as a product label or a live sales page.

The standard application fee is currently $350 per class. Accuracy here pays off: a clean, correctly classified application with a proper specimen moves through examination far more smoothly than one riddled with vague descriptions or improper specimens. Double-check the owner name in particular — filing under the wrong entity (you personally versus your LLC) can create problems down the line.

## Step four: what happens during examination and Office Actions?

After filing, your application sits in a queue before a USPTO examining attorney reviews it. If the examiner finds a problem, they issue an "Office Action" — a formal letter explaining the refusal or requirement. Common Office Actions cite a likelihood of confusion with an existing mark, a finding that your mark is merely descriptive, or a technical issue with the specimen or goods description.

You typically have a set window to respond. Some Office Actions are easy to overcome with a clarifying argument or a corrected description; others, like a substantive likelihood-of-confusion refusal, require legal argument and evidence. This is the stage where DIY filers most often get stuck, and where an attorney's help is most valuable. If you respond successfully (or if there's no refusal), the mark is published for opposition, giving others a window to object. If no one does, registration follows.

## Step five: how do you keep the registration alive?

A trademark registration is not "set it and forget it." You must file maintenance documents at specific intervals to keep it active — including a declaration of continued use between the fifth and sixth years, and renewals roughly every ten years. Missing these deadlines can cancel your registration entirely, forcing you to start over and lose your priority date.

You should also actually use the mark and police it. Trademarks can weaken or be lost if the owner stops using them or fails to stop infringers. That's why registration and enforcement go hand in hand — owning the mark is only useful if you defend it.

## When should a California small business hire a lawyer for this?

DIY filing is realistic for a distinctive mark with a clean clearance search and simple goods. Bring in a lawyer when your mark is descriptive, when the search turns up close conflicts, when you receive a substantive Office Action, or when the brand is central to your business and a mistake would be costly. The cost of professional help is usually small compared to losing your priority date or your brand name. ## How does registration fit into protecting your brand overall?

Registration is the foundation, not the finish line. Once you hold a federal mark, you can enforce it efficiently — recording it with customs, enrolling in marketplace brand-protection programs, and sending attorney-signed cease and desist letters that carry real weight. ## What are the most common mistakes California filers make?

A handful of errors account for most failed or weakened applications. Skipping the clearance search is the biggest — filers fall in love with a name, file, and then get hit with a likelihood-of-confusion refusal that a search would have predicted. Filing under the wrong owner (yourself instead of your LLC, or vice versa) creates chain-of-title problems that surface years later when you try to sell or license the brand. Describing goods too broadly invites refusals and specimen problems, while describing them too narrowly leaves gaps a competitor can exploit.

Two more are easy to avoid. Using a "mockup" specimen instead of the mark as actually used in commerce is a frequent rejection, so submit a real label or a live sales page. And missing post-registration maintenance deadlines can quietly kill a mark you spent months securing. Putting those renewal dates on a calendar the day you register is the cheapest insurance there is.

## How long does the whole process take?

Timelines vary, but California applicants should plan for many months from filing to registration, and longer if an Office Action or opposition arises. Intent-to-use applications take additional time because you must later prove actual use before the mark issues. The wait is one more reason to file early: your rights and priority date generally relate back to your filing, so getting in the queue sooner protects you sooner — even while examination is still pending.

## How does registration make enforcement cheaper later?

The real payoff comes when someone copies you. With a federal registration, you skip the hardest part of an infringement claim — proving you own valid, enforceable rights — because the registration creates a legal presumption in your favor. That makes cease and desist letters more credible, marketplace takedowns faster, and litigation, if it ever comes to that, more winnable. In other words, the money you spend registering is money you save every future time you have to defend the brand.

The bottom line: federal registration is one of the highest-leverage legal investments a California small business can make. Done carefully, it locks in your brand nationwide and makes every future enforcement step easier and cheaper.
