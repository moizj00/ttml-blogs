Generally, yes — reselling your product below your Minimum Advertised Price (MAP) is legal in California. The first sale doctrine (codified at 17 U.S.C. § 109 for copyrighted goods and recognized in trademark law under *Prestonettes, Inc. v. Coty*, 264 U.S. 359 (1924)) allows anyone who legally purchases a product to resell it at whatever price they choose. However, manufacturers can implement unilateral pricing policies that give them leverage to cut off violating resellers without running afoul of antitrust law.

## What Is a MAP Policy and Is It Enforceable?

A Minimum Advertised Price policy sets the lowest price at which authorized dealers can advertise your product. It does not set the actual sale price — that distinction matters legally.

MAP policies are enforceable in California when structured as a unilateral policy (sometimes called a "Colgate policy" after *United States v. Colgate & Co.*, 250 U.S. 300 (1919)). The key: you announce the policy, and you independently decide to stop selling to violators. You cannot negotiate, threaten, or enter into an agreement with the reseller about pricing — that would be price-fixing under the Sherman Act (15 U.S.C. § 1) and the Cartwright Act (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 16720).

## What Can I Do About Unauthorized Resellers?

If the reseller is not an authorized dealer — they acquired your product through gray market channels, liquidation, or retail arbitrage — you have additional options:

**Trademark enforcement** — If the reseller is using your brand name, logo, or product images without authorization in a way that implies they're an official retailer, you may have a trademark infringement claim under the Lanham Act.

**Cease and desist letter** — An attorney letter demanding the unauthorized reseller stop using your branding or implying authorized dealer status can be highly effective. Many gray market resellers will comply rather than face litigation.

**Platform reporting** — Amazon, eBay, and other marketplaces have programs for brand owners to report unauthorized sellers who misuse trademarks or sell outside authorized channels.

## When Is Reselling Below MAP Actually Illegal?

Price-cutting itself is almost never illegal. But the reseller crosses the line when they:

- Use your trademark in a misleading way (e.g., claiming to be an "official" or "authorized" dealer when they're not)
- Sell materially altered products under your brand name
- Sell counterfeit products
- Engage in false advertising about the product's origin or warranty

In those cases, you have claims under the Lanham Act and [California's unfair competition laws](/blog/2026-05-27-california-intellectual-property-laws-small-businesses).

## What's the Most Practical First Step?

For most California brand owners dealing with rogue resellers, the answer is a well-drafted cease and desist letter focused on trademark misuse — not the pricing itself. You can't force someone to sell at your price, but you can stop them from using your brand to do it. Pair that with platform enforcement through Amazon Brand Registry or eBay's VeRO program, and most unauthorized sellers will either comply or move on to an easier target.
