Trade dress is the overall commercial image or "look and feel" of a product or its packaging that tells consumers who made it. Under the Lanham Act (15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)) and California Business and Professions Code § 14245, trade dress receives the same protection as a trademark — meaning a competitor who copies your distinctive packaging can be forced to stop and pay damages.

To qualify for trade dress protection, your packaging must be (1) distinctive — either inherently or through acquired secondary meaning — and (2) non-functional. The Supreme Court confirmed this two-part test in *Two Pesos, Inc. v. Taco Cabana, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 763 (1992).

## What Counts as Protectable Trade Dress?

Trade dress covers the combination of visual elements that create a recognizable impression. This can include:

- The shape and configuration of a product (think the Coca-Cola bottle)
- Color combinations and graphic layouts on packaging
- The overall "feel" of a retail store or website design
- Label design, font choices, and imagery used together

What's not protectable: purely functional features. If the shape of your packaging exists because it's the cheapest way to ship the product, that's functional — not trade dress.

## How Do I Prove Trade Dress Infringement in California?

You need to show three things:

**Distinctiveness** — Your trade dress must be recognized by consumers as identifying your brand. If your packaging is inherently distinctive (unusual, creative, arbitrary), you meet this element automatically. If it's more common, you'll need to show "secondary meaning" — that customers associate the look with your specific product through advertising, sales volume, or length of use.

**Likelihood of confusion** — The competitor's packaging must be similar enough that a reasonable consumer could confuse the two products or believe they come from the same source. California courts apply the *Sleekcraft* eight-factor test for likelihood of confusion.

**Non-functionality** — The design elements you're claiming cannot be essential to the product's use or purpose.

## Can a Cease and Desist Letter Stop a Competitor From Copying My Packaging?

Yes — and it's usually the right first move. A [cease and desist letter](/blog/2026-05-27-california-intellectual-property-laws-small-businesses) puts the competitor on legal notice. If they continue after receiving the letter, their infringement becomes "willful," which can triple your damages in court under 15 U.S.C. § 1117(a).

Most small competitors will redesign their packaging rather than face a federal lawsuit. The letter costs a fraction of what litigation would, and it creates the legal record you'd need if the case ever goes to court.

## How Much Does Trade Dress Enforcement Cost?

A cease and desist letter for trade dress infringement runs $199–$500 through a flat-fee service. Federal trademark registration for trade dress costs $250–$350 per class (though trade dress applications require additional evidence of distinctiveness). If you need to litigate, expect $15,000–$50,000 or more for a federal case — but most disputes resolve at the letter stage.
