# He Took the Deposit and the Kitchen Was Still Studs

The last text said "back Monday." It was a Thursday in March when she read it, standing in a kitchen that was no longer a kitchen — just studs, a capped gas line, and the chalk outline of where the island used to be. She'd paid him $14,000 of the $22,000 up front. Monday came. So did the Monday after that. The texts went from "back Monday" to delivered-but-unread to a number that no longer rang.

This is the part of getting cheated that the anger hides at first: the disbelief. You keep thinking there's been a mix-up, that a person who shook your hand in your own home couldn't simply *leave*. He could. People do. And the law, it turns out, has seen this exact Thursday many times before.

## What the silence is hiding

A contractor who takes a deposit and walks isn't just unreliable. In California, he may have broken several rules at once — and each one is a lever.

Start with the money. State law caps what a licensed contractor can collect as a down payment on a home improvement job: **10% of the contract price or $1,000, whichever is less.** On a $22,000 kitchen, that's a $1,000 legal ceiling. A $14,000 "deposit" wasn't a deposit. It was, under the rules contractors agree to when they get licensed, already a violation — before he ever missed a Monday.

Then the license itself. If he was licensed, the Contractors State License Board can act against that license, and there may be a recovery fund in play. If he *wasn't* licensed for a job this size, the ground shifts harder in her favor: unlicensed contractors in California can be ordered to return every dollar they were paid — even for work they actually completed.

She didn't know any of this on that Thursday. Most people don't. The information that turns a victim into a claimant is sitting in plain sight in the Business and Professions Code, written in a language nobody reads until the kitchen is studs.

## The move that changes the temperature

Here's what tends to happen next, and why it works.

A formal demand letter goes out. Not an angry one — a precise one. It names the contract, the date, the amount paid, the work left undone. It cites the down-payment limit he blew past. It references the license board and the specific consequences he's now exposed to. And it gives him a deadline to return the money or finish the work.

The change in temperature is almost physical. A contractor who ignored a dozen "where are you?" texts answers a letter like this within days — because the texts were from a frustrated homeowner, and the letter is from someone who clearly knows exactly which rules he broke and where to report him. One is noise. The other is a countdown.

If the letter doesn't land, the path forward is mapped: a complaint to the license board, and small claims court, which in California now handles disputes up to $12,500 — or a higher civil claim for a job this size. But most of these stories don't reach a courtroom. They end at the letter, with a transfer she half-didn't-believe would come, because the cost of ignoring a well-aimed letter finally exceeded the cost of paying her back.

## If your Monday never came

If you're standing in your own version of that kitchen, three things are true at once, and all three favor you.

The deposit he took was almost certainly more than the law allowed. The license he holds — or doesn't — gives you leverage he's hoping you won't find. And the letter that uses both of those facts is cheaper, faster, and more frightening to him than the silence he's betting on.
