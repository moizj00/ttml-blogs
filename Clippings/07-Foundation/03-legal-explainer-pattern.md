## The pattern

The best legal explainers (the ones that rank #1 and actually get the reader to act) share a skeleton:

### 1. The direct-answer opener
Answer the searcher's exact question in the first two sentences. Someone googling "can my landlord evict me without notice in California?" wants the answer *now*, not a history of tenancy law. Give it, then expand. (This also wins Google featured snippets.)

### 2. The plain-language definition
Define the one legal term the topic hinges on, in a sentence a non-lawyer says out loud. Build trust by removing fog.

### 3. The "what this means for you" turn
Shift from abstract law to the reader's situation and stakes. This is where worry gets named — and where the reader starts believing you understand their problem.

### 4. The concrete steps
Numbered, specific, doable. "Here's exactly what to do next." Legal content earns the right to use a list here — this is genuinely a list.

### 5. The honest edge cases / risks
Name the exceptions and what can go wrong. Counter-intuitively, admitting complexity *increases* trust and pre-empts objections.

### 6. The soft, earned CTA
Not a hard sell. After genuinely helping, point to the obvious next step: "If you need a formal letter that says exactly this, that's what TTML does." The help earns the ask.

## Specific moves to steal
1. **Answer first, context second.** Never bury the answer.
2. **One legal term, defined plainly.** Per [[01-pg-write-like-you-talk]].
3. **Name the reader's fear** before offering the fix — that's the human-sentiment hook.
4. **Earn the CTA by over-delivering** on the free help.

## Anti-tells specific to legal blogs
- Don't sound like a disclaimer. Warm, plain, confident — see [[anti-ai-tells]].
- Don't hedge into uselessness ("consult an attorney" as a dodge for every sentence). Give the real answer, *then* note when a lawyer is genuinely needed.
- No "navigating the complexities of the legal landscape." (Banned — see [[word-bank]].)
