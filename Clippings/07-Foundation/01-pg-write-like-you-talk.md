## Breakdown — what makes it work

### The hook
Opens with a flat, almost obvious claim — that formal writing is usually worse than spoken — then immediately makes you doubt your own habits. No throat-clearing, no "in this essay." It states a position and trusts it.

### Structure / spine
One idea, fully chased: *complicated language hides simple thoughts, and the cure is to write the way you'd say it to a friend.* He doesn't pad. Each paragraph is one move: the problem, why it happens (we use formality to seem smart), the test (would you say this out loud?), the fix.

### Voice & sentiment
The form is the argument — the essay itself is written exactly the way it tells you to write. Short sentences. Contractions. Direct address. Dry, confident, zero performance. That's the lesson made visible.

### Specific moves to steal
1. **The read-aloud test as a rule** — if you wouldn't say it to someone, cut it. (Already in our [[mechanics]] — this is the source.)
2. **State the thesis plainly and early**, then earn it; don't build suspense in an explainer.
3. **Let the prose model the point** — for TTML, write legal concepts the way a smart lawyer would explain them over coffee.

### The ending
Lands on the practical, not the grand. No summary — just the rule, restated so it sticks.

## For our TTML blog: how to apply this
- **When to reach for it:** every TTML post. Legal topics tempt writers into jargon; this is the antidote.
- **Adaptation note:** Take a legal term ("breach of contract," "notice to quit") and explain it in the sentence you'd actually say to a worried tenant. That move alone separates our blog from every stiff law-firm site.
