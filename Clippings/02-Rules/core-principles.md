# Core Principles

The non-negotiables, beneath any format. If a genre playbook ever conflicts with these, these win.

## 1. Clarity is a moral position
Confusing writing wastes the reader's life. Every sentence should be understandable on the first read. If the reader has to back up, rewrite it.

## 2. Specificity is the engine of good writing
"A bird" is forgettable. "A heron standing in the parking-lot puddle" stays. Default to the concrete noun, the real number, the named example. Abstraction is where writing goes to die.

## 3. Cut until it hurts, then cut one more
The first draft is for getting it down. The edit is the writing. Remove every word that isn't working. Especially: *very, really, quite, just, actually, simply, that, in order to, the fact that.*

## 4. Strong verbs, few adjectives
Let verbs do the lifting. "She sprinted" beats "she ran very fast." Adjective stacks ("a beautiful, sprawling, magnificent estate") signal a weak noun.

## 5. Rhythm is meaning
Vary sentence length on purpose. A run of long sentences and then a short one. Like this. Monotone prose, even if correct, is dead prose. Read it aloud in your head.

## 6. Earn the reader's trust, then their feeling
Be accurate and unhedged first. Sentiment only lands when the reader believes you. Overclaiming kills both.

## 7. Show, then (maybe) tell
Give the detail that produces the emotion before naming the emotion — or don't name it at all. "The chair at his desk was still pulled out" beats "I missed him."

## 8. Have an opinion
Wishy-washy writing is boring writing. Take a stance, defend it, acknowledge the strongest counter honestly. Conviction with humility, not mush.

## 9. The opening and the ending carry the piece
Most readers decide in the first line. Most remember the last. Spend disproportionate effort on both. Never open by restating the prompt; never close with "in conclusion."

## 10. Write for a person, not an audience
Imagine one specific reader. Write to them. "Audience" produces fog; a person produces voice.
