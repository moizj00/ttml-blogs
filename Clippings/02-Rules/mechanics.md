# Mechanics — Sentence & Paragraph Craft

The hand-tools. Use these to execute the principles.

## Sentences
- **Default to subject–verb–object, early.** Get to the verb fast. Bury the verb and the sentence goes limp.
- **Front-load the point.** The most important word often belongs at the start or the end — the stress positions — never buried in the middle.
- **Right-branch for momentum.** Main clause first, then the modifiers trailing off it. Left-branching (long wind-up before the subject) stalls the reader.
- **One idea per sentence.** If you find an "and... and... and," split it.
- **Cut the throat-clearers:** "There is / There are," "It is important to note," "What I mean is." Start with the real subject.

## Paragraphs
- **Vary length.** A one-line paragraph is a legitimate weapon. Use it for emphasis.
- **One move per paragraph.** A paragraph makes a single turn — sets up, pays off, or pivots.
- **First and last lines of a paragraph do the work.** Strong in, strong out.

## Word choice
- **Anglo-Saxon over Latinate** when you can: "use" not "utilize," "help" not "facilitate," "about" not "regarding," "show" not "demonstrate," "end" not "terminate."
- **Concrete over abstract:** "$40" not "an affordable price," "three weeks late" not "delayed."
- **Kill intensifiers:** very, really, extremely, incredibly, truly, deeply, highly. The strong word doesn't need them.
- **Prefer the active voice.** Passive is fine when the actor is unknown or irrelevant — not as a default dodge.

## Punctuation with intent
- **Period:** your best friend. When a sentence sprawls, a period often fixes it.
- **Colon:** sets up a payoff. Promise, then deliver.
- **Semicolon:** joins two related independent clauses; use it when "and" would be too loose and a period too hard.
- **Em-dash:** an interruption or a drumroll — sparingly.
- **Comma:** for rhythm and clarity, not decoration. When in doubt, read it aloud and breathe where the commas are.

## Transitions
- Best transition is often **none** — let ideas abut.
- When you need one, prefer plain ("but," "so," "and yet," "still") over inflated ("furthermore," "moreover," "additionally," "in addition to this").

## Formatting (prose vs. lists)
- In essays, narrative, and most professional prose: **write in paragraphs.** Bullets fragment thought.
- Use lists only when the content is genuinely a list (steps, options, specs) or when scanning matters (some emails, docs).
- Headers: use them to help a long piece breathe — not to chop a short one into confetti.

## The read-aloud test
Before you ship, run the whole thing through your head as if speaking it. Anywhere you stumble, run out of breath, or sound like a brochure — fix that spot. This single habit catches most problems.
