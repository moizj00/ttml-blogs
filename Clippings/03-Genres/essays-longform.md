# Genre: Essays & Longform

Articles, op-eds, blog posts, founder essays, newsletters. Where craft shows most.

## The job
Take the reader somewhere they couldn't go alone, and make the trip feel inevitable in hindsight. An essay is an argument with a pulse.

## Structure that works (not a formula — a toolkit)
- **The hook (first 1–3 sentences):** a concrete scene, a sharp claim, a surprising fact, or a real question. Never a definition, never "throughout history."
- **The turn:** within the first paragraph or two, signal the tension — the thing that's not obvious, the problem, the stakes. Why should anyone read on?
- **The spine:** one central idea. If you can't say it in a sentence, the essay isn't ready. Everything serves the spine.
- **The body:** move by scenes, examples, and evidence — not by abstract assertion. Each section earns the next.
- **The strongest counterargument, handled honestly.** Skipping it makes you sound like a salesman. Facing it makes you credible.
- **The landing:** a turn, not a summary. Return to the opening image with new meaning, or push one step past the argument into its implication. Leave a resonance.

## Voice in longform
- Let yourself be present. "I" is allowed and often essential. A point of view is the product.
- Earn one or two memorable lines per piece — a sentence the reader could quote. Build toward them; don't pepper everything with cleverness.
- Use rhythm across paragraphs: a dense argumentative stretch, then a short, plain sentence that lets it settle.

## Length & pacing
- Cut the first paragraph after you finish the draft — it's usually warm-up. Often the second paragraph was the real opening.
- A longform piece should feel *shorter* than its word count. Pace is generosity.

## Do
- Open in motion. Argue with examples. End with a turn.

## Don't
- Don't "set the stage" with background nobody asked for.
- Don't write the "what I'll cover" paragraph.
- Don't end with "In conclusion" or a tidy bow that flattens the idea.
